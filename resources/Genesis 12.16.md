## Commentary
“He treated Abram well for her sake. He had sheep, oxen, male donkeys, male and female servants, female donkeys, and camels.”

By every outward measure, Egypt worked. Abraham went down to escape a famine (==Genesis 12:10==) and came out “very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold” (==Genesis 13:2==). His plan even produced the exact result he'd hoped for. He had asked Sarai to pose as his sister “that it may be well with me for your sake” (==Genesis 12:13==), and this verse tells us the outcome: Pharaoh “treated Abram well for her sake.”

This tells us where the increase came from...and what it cost. The one treating Abraham well is Pharaoh, not the LORD, and the herds are in effect payment for Sarai, who at this moment is in another man's house (==Genesis 12:15==). The woman through whom God intended to bring the promised offspring had been traded for livestock. Abraham had never been wealthier, and the promise had never been in more danger.

Scripture does not treat outward increase as a reliable sign that a person is walking in God's will or wisdom. Moses struck the rock in disobedience, and water still came out abundantly (==Numbers 20:11-12==). Israel demanded meat in the wilderness, and God “gave them their request, but sent leanness into their soul” (==Psalm 106:15==). The church at Laodicea said, “I am rich, have become wealthy, and have need of nothing,” while Jesus called it wretched and poor (==Revelation 3:17==). Increase can come from God's hand, from another person's favor, or from our own maneuvering, and from the outside all three look the same. At the end of the day, obedience to what God has said is the measure of being in His will; results are not.

The wealth of Egypt that Abram acquired also came with hidden complications. The herds grew too large for Abraham and Lot to remain together (==Genesis 13:6-7==). And among the “female servants” listed in this verse was, in all likelihood, a young Egyptian woman named Hagar. Scripture never states where Sarai acquired her, but it introduces her as “an Egyptian maidservant” (==Genesis 16:1==), using the same Hebrew word for servant that appears here, and this is the only time the household is recorded as being in Egypt and receiving servants. Hagar most likely left Egypt with them as part of Pharaoh’s payment.

For ten years she was simply part of Abram's household. But when the promise of a son was delayed, Sarai proposed a solution that was already living in their tent: “Please, go in to my maid; perhaps I shall obtain children by her” (==Genesis 16:2-3==). What Abraham acquired in a season of leaning on his own understanding set the stage for what was arguably the greatest mistake of his life. The result was contempt between the two women, harsh treatment that drove Hagar into the wilderness (==Genesis 16:4-6==), and years later a divided home that grieved Abraham deeply when Hagar and Ishmael were sent away (==Genesis 21:9-11==). Hagar herself was not the problem. She was a servant with no say in any of it, and the LORD saw her, spoke to her, and provided for her son (==Genesis 16:13==; ==Genesis 21:17-20==). The problem was what Abraham and Sarai did with what they brought home from Egypt. Compare this with Solomon's description of God's blessing: “The blessing of the LORD makes one rich, and He adds no sorrow with it” (==Proverbs 10:22==). 

## Word Study
**Treated well** is the Hebrew *yatab* (יָטַב)—to be good, to go well; in its causative form, to do good to someone, to deal well with them. It's the same verb Abraham used in verse 13 (“that it may be well with me”), so verse 16 reads as the direct fulfillment of his own plan. Elsewhere the verb describes what God Himself does for His people: He promised Jacob, “I will surely treat you well” (==Genesis 32:12==); He led Israel through the wilderness, humbling and testing them, “to do you good in the end” (==Deuteronomy 8:16==); and in the new covenant He promises, “I will not turn away from doing them good” (==Jeremiah 32:40==). Summary: the same word describes two kinds of good—the good God gives His people after testing, and the good Pharaoh gave Abraham in exchange for compromise.

## Application
Don't evaluate a decision by its outcome alone. A raise, an open door, a growing ministry, or a season of ease can be the fruit of obedience, and it can just as easily be the fruit of compromise. Ask the questions that results alone can't answer: Did the Lord lead me here? What did it cost to get this, and who paid for it? Abraham built altars in Canaan (==Genesis 12:7-8==) and returned to one when he came back (==Genesis 13:3-4==), but no altar is recorded in Egypt, the place where he gained the most. If what you have is increasing while your worship and your faithfulness to God are decreasing, the increase is not a sign that God is pleased with the path you're on.
