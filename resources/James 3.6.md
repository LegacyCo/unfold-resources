## Commentary
“And the tongue is a fire, a world of iniquity… it defiles the whole body, and sets on fire the course of nature.”

Here’s a mark of kingdom people you can spot in any room: they know their tongue is on fire, so their words are few.

Fire isn’t handled casually by people who respect it. A man who knows he’s carrying a lit torch through a dry forest walks differently—slower, more deliberately, watching where every spark lands. That’s how the mature carry the tongue. Not because they have nothing to say, but because they know what a single careless sentence can burn down: a reputation, a friendship, a church, a child’s sense of worth. “In the multitude of words sin is not lacking, but he who restrains his lips is wise” (==Proverbs 10:19==).

Scripture keeps linking fewness of words with the fear of God: “God is in heaven, and you on earth; therefore let your words be few” (==Ecclesiastes 5:2==). “Even a fool is counted wise when he holds his peace” (==Proverbs 17:28==). And James sets the bar plainly: anyone who doesn’t stumble in word is a mature man, able to bridle the whole body (==James 3:2==). The measure of spiritual maturity isn’t how much fire comes out of your mouth. It’s knowing the fire is there—and choosing, most of the time, to keep the torch lowered.

## Application
Make David’s prayer your morning prayer this week: “Set a guard, O LORD, over my mouth; keep watch over the door of my lips” (==Psalm 141:3==). Then practice the pause—in the meeting, in the group chat, in the disagreement at home. Before you speak, ask three questions of the sentence you’re about to release: Is it true? Is it necessary? Does it give grace to the one hearing it (==Ephesians 4:29==)? You’ll be surprised how many torches never needed to leave your hand—and how much weight your words carry once people learn you don’t spend them cheaply.
