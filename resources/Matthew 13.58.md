## Commentary
There's an interesting connection between this passage and a prayer recorded in ==Jeremiah 14:7–9==.

During a severe drought in Judah, Jeremiah appeals to the Lord on behalf of the nation. He acknowledges their sin and then asks a remarkable question: “Why should You be like a stranger in the land?” He goes on to ask why God appears to be like a mighty warrior who is unable to save His people.

Then he makes this declaration: “Yet You, O Lord, are in our midst.”

Think about what Jeremiah is saying. God was still Israel's God. He had established His covenant with them, chosen to dwell among them, and promised to be their Savior in times of trouble. Yet the people were experiencing His presence as though He were a stranger in their own land. The God who had delivered their fathers from Egypt now seemed unwilling to intervene on their behalf.

But the problem wasn't that God had lost His power or forgotten His covenant. Israel's persistent disobedience had brought them under His judgment. In fact, the Lord responds to Jeremiah's prayer by confronting the nation's unwillingness to turn from its sinful ways (==Jeremiah 14:10–12==).

Now consider what is happening in Nazareth.

The God who had once dwelt among Israel in the tabernacle and temple had now come to dwell among them in the person of Jesus Christ. Matthew had already identified Him as Immanuel, meaning “God with us” (==Matthew 1:23==). The very presence of God that Israel had longed for was standing in their midst.

Yet they refused to receive Him.

And just as Jeremiah had described the Lord as appearing to be a mighty warrior who could not save, Matthew describes Jesus as doing very few mighty works among the people of Nazareth because of their unbelief.

What's striking about these two passages is that in both instances, God's people were experiencing something far less than what His presence made possible. And in neither case was the problem a deficiency in God.

We need to be careful here because this doesn't mean that every unanswered prayer or every absence of miraculous intervention is the result of unbelief. Scripture gives us numerous examples of faithful people who suffered, prayed, and waited upon the Lord without receiving the immediate deliverance they desired. Even Paul was told that God's grace would be sufficient for him rather than being granted the removal of his affliction (==2 Corinthians 12:7–10==).

But Matthew is making something clear about Nazareth: their unbelief had consequences. The One who had come to reveal the Father, proclaim the kingdom, and demonstrate its power was present among them, yet they refused to receive Him.

We don't need to convince God to become more present with us. Through Christ, He has already made His dwelling among and within His people (==John 14:23==; ==1 Corinthians 3:16==). What we need is a heart that remains tender to the One who is already here.
