## Commentary
Psalm 8 now moves from humanity’s identity to humanity’s assignment. God has crowned mankind with glory and honor, and that honor is expressed through delegated rule: “You have made him to have dominion over the works of Your hands.”

The Hebrew verb translated “have dominion” is *mashal* (מָשַׁל), meaning to rule, govern, or exercise authority. The idea is not that humanity owns creation independently of God, but that humanity has been entrusted with authority beneath God. Creation remains “the works of Your hands.” The world belongs to God; humanity has been appointed to govern what belongs to Him.

This language reaches back to ==Genesis 1:26–28==, where God creates humanity in His image and says, “Let them have dominion.” In the ancient world, a king’s “image” could represent his authority in territory where the king himself was not physically present. Statues or images of the king were placed throughout his kingdom as visible reminders that this land belonged to him and remained under his rule. In that sense, the image represented the presence and authority of the one it portrayed. Genesis uses that kind of royal language for humanity: we are placed within creation as living images of God, meant to reflect His character and represent His rule in the world.

Psalm 8 is therefore not describing ownership so much as representation. Human authority is meant to resemble the authority of the God whose image we bear. Or as Paul says, "we are ambassadors" (==2 Corinthians 5:20==) God gives life, brings order from chaos, cultivates what is good, protects what He has made, and causes creation to flourish. Dominion becomes distorted whenever authority is separated from the character of the One who gave it.

Then David says, “You have put all things under his feet.” In the ancient world, placing something “under the feet” was an image of rule and subjection. Psalm 8 pictures creation placed beneath humanity’s authority. But again, this authority is received, not seized: “You have put” all things there. Humanity rules only because God has entrusted creation to us.

Yet there is an obvious tension here. When we look at the world, humanity does not appear to exercise the kind of harmonious dominion Psalm 8 describes. Creation resists us. Human beings exploit creation and one another. Death itself eventually puts every ruler beneath the ground.

Hebrews acknowledges this problem when it quotes Psalm 8:
“For in that He put all in subjection under him, He left nothing that is not put under him. But now we do not yet see all things put under him. But we see Jesus” (==Hebrews 2:8–9==).

The language there is critical: “We do not yet see… but we see Jesus.”

Psalm 8 describes humanity’s original vocation. Hebrews points us to the Man in whom that vocation is finally fulfilled. Jesus is the true image of God (==Colossians 1:15==), the true Son of Man, and the faithful human being who exercises God’s authority exactly as it was intended. What Adam failed to do, Christ fulfills.

Paul develops the same idea when he says that God “put all things under His feet” (==1 Corinthians 15:27==; ==Ephesians 1:22==). The language of Psalm 8 ultimately finds its fullest expression in Jesus: creation under the feet of the true Man.

Psalm 8:6 therefore tells us something profound about both humanity and Christ. We were created to represent God within His world, but that calling has been fractured by sin. Jesus does not abandon that original purpose—He fulfills it. And in union with Him, humanity is being restored to what it was always meant to be: the image of God faithfully representing the rule of God within the creation of God.

## Application
God has entrusted you with more than you may realize. Your work, your relationships, your influence, your resources, and the places He has given you responsibility are all part of the “works of His hands” placed within your care. The question is not whether you have authority, but whether your authority reflects the One who gave it to you. Wherever God has given you influence, represent Him well. Bring order where there is chaos, cultivate what is good, protect what is vulnerable, and leave what has been entrusted to you more fruitful than you found it.
