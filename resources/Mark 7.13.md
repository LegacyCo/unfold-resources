## Commentary
God’s word is the most powerful thing known to creation. He simply spoke, and the world came into being. He upholds all things by the word of His power (==Hebrews 1:3==). His word is living and active, piercing to the division of soul and spirit, discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart (==Hebrews 4:12==).

And yet Jesus says to the Pharisees: you are “making the word of God of no effect through your tradition.” Powerless. Nullified. That’s the absolute scandal of our gospel—the effectiveness of God’s all-powerful word is somehow connected to the heart of the person it’s spoken to.

Look at the specific example Jesus confronts in this passage, because it exposes how the nullifying of God's word actually works. God’s word said, “Honor your father and your mother.” But the tradition had built an exemption: a man could declare his resources “Corban”—a gift devoted to God—and then tell his aging parents there was nothing left to support them with (==Mark 7:10-12==). The workaround was *religious*. They didn’t nullify the word with rebellion; they nullified it with devotion. The most dangerous traditions never look like disobedience. They look like extra obedience—spiritual-sounding structures that excuse us from what God actually said.

Jesus taught the principle in the parable of the sower (==Mark 4:3-8==). The seed is the word of God, and the condition of the soil determines how effectively that seed grows and bears fruit. Some fell on the hard path, some on rocky ground, some among thorns, some on good soil. And notice: it’s never the seed’s fault. It isn’t the seed’s fault the soil was full of weeds that choked it, or too packed-down to receive it. The seed carries within it all the power it needs to produce generations of life and fruit. The problem is never the seed. The problem is the soil.

That’s what tradition had done to the Pharisees—and what it can do to us. Tradition hardens soil in a particular way: through familiarity. It teaches us how the word is *supposed* to sound, what it’s *allowed* to demand, where its claims are permitted to stop. The scribes were the most Bible-saturated men of their generation, and the word bounced off them—because a heart that has already decided what God can say cannot be pierced by what He *is* saying. We have our own Corbans: church habits, theological systems, denominational traditions, and personal precedents that let us handle the word constantly while keeping it at a safe distance from the areas it would rearrange.

But compacted soil is not a life sentence—it can be broken up. “Break up your fallow ground, for it is time to seek the LORD” (==Hosea 10:12==). James tells us how the word is meant to land: “receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls” (==James 1:21==). Meekness is soft soil—a heart that comes to the word with no exemptions pre-filed, ready to be told something it didn’t already believe. The power for transformation is in the word you receive. But how you receive it determines whether that power is released over your life. The word is not a lottery ticket—it’s an invitation to partner with God to see His word come to pass.

## Word Study
- *making of no effect* : **akyroō** — to revoke, invalidate, cancel; literally to strip something of its *kyros*, its authority. Jesus isn’t saying the Pharisees weakened the word’s power in itself—He’s saying they de-authorized it in their own lives, treating a standing decree as if it had been repealed.

The other notable use of this word:

- ==Galatians 3:17== — the law, coming 430 years later, “cannot *annul* (akyroō)” God’s covenant with Abraham. Nothing in heaven or earth can actually revoke what God has spoken.

Summary: set the two uses side by side and the irony is sharp. No power in existence can annul God’s word—except the strange veto power of a human heart over the word’s effect in its own life. Tradition can’t touch the seed. It can only ruin the soil.
