## Commentary
“Fight the good fight of faith, lay hold on eternal life.” The phrase “lay hold” is the Greek word *epilambanō*. Paul loves this word. It’s the same word he uses of his own pursuit: “I press on, that I may *lay hold* of that for which Christ Jesus has also *laid hold* of me” (==Philippians 3:12==).

*Epilambanō* is a violent word—an act of aggression and power. It’s the word used when Jesus grabbed Peter as he sank beneath the waves (==Matthew 14:31==): a forceful apprehension of the man and, at the same time, a trustful holding of the hand. Both things at once—violence and trust. That’s the grip Paul commands us to take on eternal life.

Which gives us a strange and beautiful portrait of God’s people. To the world, they are lambs. When attacked, they lay down their arms, pray for their persecutors, love their enemies. But toward heaven, they are aggressors—passionately violent about their inheritance in the cross (==Matthew 11:12==). We don’t receive salvation and then politely take a seat in our local church. We receive salvation as *permission*—forgiveness as the gate to pursuing Jesus with everything we have.

And here’s what that pursuit produces: by living in passionate first love for Jesus and warring against anything that pulls you from Him, you become conformed to the image of the One you’re pursuing—able to carry the weight of demonstrating God to a generation. Paul described his own life exactly this way: “I labored even more than all of them, yet not I, but the grace of God within me” (==1 Corinthians 15:10==). Grace doesn’t make the fight unnecessary; grace is what fights in you.

As Kathryn Kuhlman said, “God is not looking for gold vessels or silver vessels. He is looking for yielded vessels.” Yielded toward Him. Ferocious toward the prize. Lambs to the world—aggressors toward heaven.
