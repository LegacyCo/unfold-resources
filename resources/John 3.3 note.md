## Commentary
“Unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.”

You can hear the whole story of the suffering Messiah—accurately, movingly, in full theological detail—and walk away unchanged. Because up to that point, it’s a history lesson. And history lessons don’t save people. Facts matter, but until facts become personal testimony, they have no power to transform a life. The facts of breathing do me no good until I actually take a breath. I could spend my whole life learning facts about water and still die of thirst—unless I drink.

It’s the same with the cross. I can affirm that Jesus died for the sins of the world and still be untouched by it. Until I believe He died *in place of me*, His death won’t save me. His stripes won’t heal me. His righteousness won’t become my righteousness. The sacrifice is sufficient for everyone, but it’s received one person at a time, by faith.

And receiving it costs something specific: to take the life Jesus offers, I have to die to everything I inherited as a son of Adam. Not die to *save* myself—I die to *receive* the new life Jesus died to give me. “I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (==Galatians 2:20==). Before the gospel is ever “come and live,” it is first “come and die.” When I’m no longer in Adam, I can be re-born in Christ.

That’s why Jesus doesn’t tell Nicodemus—a teacher of Israel, a man drowning in accurate facts—to study more. He tells him he must be born again. And the way in hasn’t changed since Peter preached it at Pentecost: “Repent… and you shall receive. For the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are afar off” (==Acts 2:38-39==). Don’t settle for knowing the story. Drink the water. Take the breath. Be born.
