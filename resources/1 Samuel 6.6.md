## Commentary
There is something remarkable about the Philistine priests and diviners appealing to the story of Pharaoh and the Exodus. About 350 years had passed since God delivered Israel from Egypt, yet the memory of what He had done was still known among the surrounding nations. The plagues, the humiliation of Pharaoh, and the deliverance of Israel had left an impression that extended well beyond the people who witnessed those events. The Philistines understood that the God who had confronted Egypt was the same God whose ark they had captured, and they were beginning to recognize the danger of resisting Him.

Their question, “Why then do you harden your hearts?” is especially significant. They understood that Pharaoh's destruction was not simply the consequence of opposing a stronger God, but of repeatedly refusing to respond to what God was revealing. Pharaoh had been given numerous opportunities to humble himself. With each plague, the Lord demonstrated His power and gave Pharaoh another opportunity to surrender. Yet Pharaoh continued to resist, even as the consequences became increasingly severe (==Exodus 9:34–35==). The Philistine priests were essentially warning their own people not to make the same mistake.

What makes this passage particularly interesting is that these are pagan religious leaders recognizing a spiritual danger that God's own people would repeatedly struggle with throughout their history. They understood that a hardened heart could persist in resisting God even when the evidence of His power was undeniable. Seeing what God is doing and responding appropriately to Him are two very different things.

There is an important distinction here between recognizing God's power and surrendering to His lordship. The Philistines were willing to return the ark because they wanted relief from their suffering, but the narrative gives us no indication that they were prepared to abandon their gods and worship the Lord. They wanted the consequences of their resistance to end, but that did not necessarily mean they wanted a relationship with the God they had offended.

This is a warning worth taking seriously. It is possible to recognize God's hand in our circumstances, acknowledge that He is speaking, and even make adjustments to our behavior without genuinely surrendering our hearts to Him. The writer of Hebrews takes up this same warning when he writes, “Today, if you will hear His voice, do not harden your hearts” (==Hebrews 3:7–8==).

The danger of a hardened heart is not merely that we refuse to acknowledge God, but that we become increasingly accustomed to resisting Him. Every time we recognize His voice and refuse to respond, we reinforce a pattern of resistance. Conversely, every act of surrender cultivates a heart that is increasingly sensitive and responsive to Him. The question is not simply whether we recognize what God is saying, but whether we are willing to humble ourselves and respond when He speaks.
