## Commentary
"A fool vents all his feelings, but a wise man holds them back."

"Well, I'm just telling you how I feel." We say it like it settles the matter—as if honesty about a feeling automatically makes the feeling worth saying. Solomon disagrees. He says the one who vents *all* his feelings is a fool, and the wise hold them back.

That's not a call to stuff everything down and pretend. Feelings are real, and God can handle every one of them—the Psalms prove that. The question is not whether we have feelings but whether our feelings get the microphone. A feeling is information, not an authority. It's a report from somewhere inside us, and like all reports, it needs to be examined before it's broadcast (==Proverbs 25:28==).

Look at how the Psalms handle this. David is the most emotionally honest man in Scripture, and he doesn't hide it: "How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever?" (==Psalm 13:1==). But watch where he takes it. He doesn't announce it to the camp; he pours it out before God—"I pour out my complaint before Him" (==Psalm 142:2==)—and by the end of the psalm the feeling has been examined in the light of who God is and has become a conviction: "But I have trusted in Your mercy" (==Psalm 13:5==). David vented all his feelings—to the one Person who could actually do something with them. That's the difference between the fool and the wise. Not whether the feeling gets out, but *where* it goes first.

Because feelings that skip that step do damage. Notice how the verse describes the wise: he holds his feelings *back*—the Hebrew paints the picture of calming the feelings down. The wise man hasn't gotten rid of the feeling; he's settled it before it settled him. Anger, hurt, and fear all shout the loudest in the first few seconds. The fool speaks in those early moments of emotion. The wise man lets them pass, and what he says afterward is not the feeling itself but what he actually believes.

Jesus said we will give account for every careless word (==Matthew 12:36==). So don't be so quick to share how you feel—declare what you *believe*. When we stand before the Lord, let's not give account for a lifetime of vented feelings. Let's give account for convictions: words we weighed, tested against His word, and meant.

Hold the feeling. Examine it. Let it become either a conviction worth speaking or a burden worth praying about. The wise still say hard things—they just say them on purpose.

## Word Study
Words in this verse worth considering:

- *vents* : **yatsa** (Hebrew) — to go out, come out, bring forth. Literally, "a fool brings out all his spirit." The verb is used for a river *flowing out* of Eden (==Genesis 2:10==) and for Israel *going out* of Egypt (==Exodus 13:3==)—something leaving one place and entering another. When the fool speaks, his inner life doesn't stay inside; it exits and enters the room. What was his becomes everyone's.
- *feelings* : **ruach** (Hebrew) — spirit, breath, wind. The King James renders it "mind"; other translations "spirit," "anger," or "temper." It's the same word for the wind that blows where it wishes and the Spirit who hovered over the waters (==Genesis 1:2==). Solomon's point is that the fool lets *all* of his ruach out—the whole turbulent weather system of his inner life—unfiltered.
- *holds them back* : **shabach** (Hebrew) — to still, soothe, calm. This is the word for God *stilling* the roaring of the seas (==Psalm 65:7==) and *calming* the storm so its waves are hushed (==Psalm 89:9==). The wise man does to his spirit what God does to the ocean.

Summary: the fool lets the whole wind out; the wise man calms the wind first. Both feel the storm—only one becomes it.
