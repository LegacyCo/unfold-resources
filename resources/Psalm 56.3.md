## Commentary
“Whenever I am afraid, I will trust in You.”

David does not say “I am never afraid.” He says *whenever*—fear is assumed, and what matters is where it sends him. The fear and anxiety a believer experiences in a fallen world can actually be redemptive: they serve as an announcement that, in that moment, something else is occupying the throne of the heart. Fear is what it feels like to give more attention to your problem than to the God of your problem. It's the canary in the mine shaft telling you that you have slipped out from under the shadow of His wing (==Psalm 91:1==); the warning light on the dashboard telling you to pull over; the pain of the hot stove—a healthy response God designed to protect you, not a verdict to condemn you.

So don't let fear shame you. Let it pull you back. The moment David feels the fear, he converts it into trust—“whenever I am afraid, I will trust in You”—and by the next verse the conversion is already working: “In God I have put my trust; I will not fear. What can flesh do to me?” (==Psalm 56:4==). Handled this way, fear becomes an instrument of return rather than a weapon of accusation: it announces where your attention has gone, and the announcement is the invitation to bring it home (==Isaiah 26:3==; ==1 Peter 5:7==).
