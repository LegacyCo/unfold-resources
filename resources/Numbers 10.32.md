## Commentary
There is something significant happening in this exchange between Moses and Hobab, particularly when we consider that Hobab was a Midianite, not an Israelite.

To understand the conversation, we need to identify who these men were. In ==Numbers 10:29==, Hobab is introduced as the son of Reuel the Midianite. Reuel was Moses' father-in-law, the man we also encounter under the name Jethro (==Exodus 2:18; 3:1==). This would make Hobab Moses' brother-in-law, although some translations and passages identify Hobab himself as Moses' father-in-law, reflecting an ambiguity in the Hebrew terminology for relatives by marriage.

Moses had spent approximately forty years living among the Midianites after fleeing Egypt. During that time, he married Zipporah, the daughter of Reuel, and became a shepherd of his father-in-law's flocks. These were people Moses knew well, and it was while tending those flocks in the wilderness that God appeared to him in the burning bush.

Now, as Israel prepares to leave Mount Sinai and journey toward the Promised Land, Moses invites Hobab to accompany them.

Hobab initially declines, expressing his desire to return to his own land and relatives. But Moses urges him to reconsider, explaining that his knowledge of the wilderness would be valuable to Israel. In verse 31, Moses says, "You know how we are to camp in the wilderness, and you can be our eyes."

This is interesting because Israel was already being led by the presence of God in the cloud. Yet Moses still recognized the value of Hobab's practical knowledge and experience. Divine guidance did not eliminate the usefulness of human wisdom. God was directing their journey, but Moses understood that Hobab's familiarity with the terrain could still be of great benefit.

However, it is Moses' promise in verse 32 that I find particularly significant: "And it shall be, if you go with us—indeed it shall be—that whatever good the LORD will do to us, the same we will do to you."

Consider what Moses is offering.

God had made extraordinary promises to Abraham and his descendants. He had delivered Israel from Egypt, established His covenant with them, and promised to bring them into a land flowing with milk and honey. These were promises given specifically to Israel as God's covenant people.

Yet Moses now invites a Midianite to accompany them and share in the goodness God had promised.

This is an important reminder that God's purposes for Israel were never intended to terminate with Israel alone. When God first called Abraham, He declared that through him all the families of the earth would be blessed (==Genesis 12:3==). Israel was chosen not simply to receive God's blessing, but to become the people through whom His blessing would ultimately extend to the nations.

We see glimpses of this throughout the Old Testament. Rahab, a Canaanite woman, is welcomed among Israel after placing her faith in Israel's God (==Joshua 6:25==). Ruth, a Moabite woman, leaves her homeland and joins herself to the people of Israel, declaring, "Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God" (==Ruth 1:16==). Both women would eventually appear in the genealogy of Jesus.

And here, long before either of those stories, Moses is extending an invitation to a Midianite to share in the good that God has promised His people.

Interestingly, the biblical account never explicitly tells us whether Hobab accepted Moses' invitation. However, ==Judges 1:16== tells us that the descendants of Moses' Kenite father-in-law traveled with the people of Judah and settled among them. The Kenites were associated with Moses' Midianite relatives, suggesting that at least some of this family ultimately joined themselves to Israel.

There is a beautiful anticipation of the gospel in this invitation.

Through Jesus Christ, the blessings of God's covenant promises have been extended to people from every nation. Paul explains in ==Galatians 3:8–9== that the gospel was announced beforehand to Abraham in the promise that all nations would be blessed through him. Those who belong to Christ are now counted as Abraham's offspring and heirs according to the promise (==Galatians 3:29==).

Paul develops this further in ==Ephesians 2:12–19==, explaining that Gentiles who were once strangers to the covenants of promise have now been brought near through the blood of Christ. They are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God.

What Moses offered Hobab was a share in the good God was going to do for Israel. What Christ has accomplished is even greater. Through Him, people who were once far off have been brought into God's family and made fellow heirs of His promises.

There is also a practical invitation here. Hobab was being asked to leave the familiarity of his own people and homeland to accompany Israel into an uncertain future. Moses could not offer him a detailed account of everything they would encounter along the way. He could only point to what God had promised and assure him that he would share in the good God intended to do.

In many ways, this is the nature of faith. We don't always know what obedience will require of us or what difficulties we may encounter along the way. But we have been invited into a life with God, and our confidence rests not in our ability to predict the journey, but in the faithfulness of the One who has made the promise.
