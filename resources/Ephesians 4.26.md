## Commentary
“‘Be angry, and do not sin’: do not let the sun go down on your wrath.”

Someone hurt you for no reason. You know you’re innocent, and as far as you’re concerned, your anger is justified. What are you supposed to do with that? Paul doesn’t say “never feel anger”—he says “be angry, and do not sin.” Bob Gladstone writes in *Marks of the Cross*, “The natural, initial response to unjust treatment may be anger. The Bible acknowledges that and does not call the anger sin at this point. But neither does the Bible give us license to let that anger shift into angry attitudes, words, or actions. Scripture accepts that anger may be our natural, first response, but then it instructs us to deal with it before it becomes sin.”

The question is what you do next—and how fast. Paul puts a deadline on it: don’t let the sun go down on it. Anger is only safe in transit; parked overnight, it becomes lodging for something else—“nor give place to the devil” (==Ephesians 4:27==). And in case we imagine our anger deserves an exemption, watch what Paul does five verses later: “Let all bitterness, wrath, *anger*, clamor, and evil speaking be put away from you” (==Ephesians 4:31==). The anger he permitted in verse 26 is the same anger he orders removed in verse 31. Permission to feel it was never permission to keep it. We simply don’t have the right to do anything with our initial anger except put it away—and replace it with something: “be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave you” (==Ephesians 4:32==).

That’s the full path, and it’s walkable in a single day: feel it honestly, refuse to act from it, put it away before dark, and fill the empty space with forgiveness. Anger acknowledged and released becomes a scar that healed. Anger kept becomes an open door.
