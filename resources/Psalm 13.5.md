## Commentary
“But I have trusted in Your mercy; my heart shall rejoice in Your salvation.”

Psalm 13 turns on the word “But.” For four verses David has been asking how long God will forget him and pleading for an answer before he dies or his enemy wins. Then he says, “But I have trusted in Your mercy.”

Nothing in the psalm says his situation has improved. There's no report that the enemy has backed off or that the sorrow has lifted. Verse 4 is fear and verse 5 is trust, and the difference is simply what David is looking at. In verses 1–4 he's looking at how long it has been, at his own thoughts, and at his enemy. In verse 5 he looks at God's mercy.

David uses three tenses in verses 5 and 6. “I have trusted” is past. He put his weight on God's mercy before this trouble started, and he's reminding himself of that. “My heart shall rejoice” is future. He isn't rejoicing yet, and he doesn't pretend to be, but he's sure he will. “He has dealt bountifully with me” (==Psalm 13:6==) is past again. He looks back at what God has already done and counts it as evidence. This is how faith in a hard season works. We remember whom we've trusted, we look at what He has already done, and on that basis we expect to rejoice again.

What David trusts in is specific. He doesn't say he trusts that things will work out, or that his circumstances will change by a certain date. He trusts in God's “mercy,” which is His covenant love. David's feelings in verse 1 told him God had forgotten him. God's character says He doesn't forget the people He has bound Himself to. David chooses to stand on God's character.

For the believer, the proof of that mercy is the cross. “He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?” (==Romans 8:32==). The Hebrew word for “salvation” in this verse is *yeshu'ah*, where Jesus' name comes from (==Matthew 1:21==). David expected to rejoice in God's salvation, and we know who that salvation is. Paul reaches the same conclusion David did: nothing “shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord” (==Romans 8:39==).

## Word Study
**Mercy** is the Hebrew *chesed* (חֶסֶד)—loyal love, steadfast love, covenant faithfulness. It's love that keeps its commitments. God uses it to describe Himself: “abounding in goodness and truth, keeping mercy for thousands” (==Exodus 34:6-7==). Psalm 136 repeats it in every verse: “His mercy endures forever.” Jeremiah held on to it in the ruins of Jerusalem: “Through the LORD's mercies we are not consumed” (==Lamentations 3:22==). Summary: *chesed* is God's commitment to His people, and it doesn't rise and fall with their circumstances or their feelings. That's why David could trust it while he still felt forgotten.
