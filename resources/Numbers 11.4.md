## Commentary
When Israel departed from Egypt, they were accompanied by a large group of people who were not necessarily Israelites (==Exodus 12:38==). These people had witnessed God's extraordinary acts of deliverance and chose to leave Egypt alongside His people. Numbers 11:4 refers to a group called the *asafsuf*, often identified with that mixed multitude.

But the mixed multitude began craving the foods of Egypt, and soon the Israelites joined in their dissatisfaction. The text specifically says, “the children of Israel also wept again.” What began as discontentment among one group spread throughout the people of God. Their cravings became Israel's cravings, and soon the entire congregation was questioning the goodness of God's provision.

The people we allow to influence us will inevitably affect what we desire, how we think, and what we come to believe is important. Israel had been miraculously delivered from Egypt, but the influence of those longing for Egypt was now stirring up the same desires among God's covenant people. They had physically left Egypt behind, but their hearts were being drawn back to what God had delivered them from.

Throughout Scripture, God repeatedly warns His people against mixture—not the presence of people from different backgrounds, but the mixing of devotion to Him with the values, desires, and practices of a world that does not know Him. Paul warns that “evil company corrupts good habits” (==1 Corinthians 15:33==), and that “a little leaven leavens the whole lump” (==Galatians 5:9==). It does not take much compromise to begin influencing the spiritual condition of an entire community.

This does not mean we are to withdraw from the world or avoid relationships with unbelievers. Jesus was known as a friend of sinners (==Matthew 11:19==), and He specifically prayed that the Father would not take His disciples out of the world, but protect them from the evil one (==John 17:15–18==). We are called to engage the world around us with the gospel, demonstrating the love, goodness, and transforming power of God's kingdom.

But there is a difference between being present in the world and allowing the spirit of the age to shape the life of the church. We are called to influence the world with the values of the kingdom, not allow the values of the world to redefine the kingdom among us. Paul makes this distinction clear when he instructs believers not to be conformed to this world, but to be transformed by the renewing of their minds (==Romans 12:2==).

There is a sobering lesson in the speed with which Israel's desires were redirected. They had witnessed the plagues, crossed the Red Sea, experienced God's presence at Sinai, and were being sustained daily by bread from heaven. Yet the cravings of those around them were enough to make them dissatisfied with what God was providing.

Paul specifically refers to Israel's experience in the wilderness when he warns the Corinthian believers, “Now these things became our examples, to the intent that we should not lust after evil things as they also lusted” (==1 Corinthians 10:6==). 

## Application
This should cause us to examine the influences we permit to shape our own lives and churches. Are we cultivating a hunger for the things of God, or have we allowed the appetites of the surrounding culture to determine what we pursue? Are we influencing those around us toward greater faith and devotion to Christ, or are their values gradually reshaping our own?

We cannot fulfill our calling to reach the world by becoming indistinguishable from it. We must love people without allowing the leaven of this age to spread among us. Our calling is not isolation, but transformation—remaining fully engaged with the world while being firmly established in the values and life of God's kingdom.
