## Commentary
Jesus has just multiplied five loaves and two fish for thousands of people, and the crowd draws the obvious conclusion: “Therefore when Jesus perceived that they were about to come and take Him by force *to make Him king*, He departed again to the mountain by Himself alone.”

Stop and feel the strangeness of that. The King of kings ran from a coronation. Why? Because you don’t *make* Jesus King. He already is King. The crowd’s offer exposed their whole way of thinking: “He’s not our king—but how amazing would it be to have a king who can do stuff like this? Let’s make Him ours.” They weren’t submitting to His royalty; they were recruiting His power. They wanted to install Him in a throne that served their appetites—the day after, they chased Him around the lake, and He told them exactly why: “you seek Me… because you ate of the loaves and were filled” (==John 6:26==). A king you crown because he feeds you is a king you’ll un-crown when he stops.

The issue is never His royalty. Just because He’s not *your* King doesn’t mean He’s not King (==Psalm 2:6==; ==Revelation 19:16==). The only question is your surrender.

Now, there’s a real tension here, because Scripture also tells us to “forget not all His benefits” (==Psalm 103:2==). Kingdom people absolutely believe He is the Healer, the Provider, our peace, our rest—the One who satisfies us with good things. We don’t pretend the benefits away; we thank Him for every one. But here’s the difference between the crowd and the kingdom: if none of those benefits ever came our way again, kingdom people would still follow Him wherever He goes—simply because He is the King. The multitudes follow the bread. Disciples follow the King, and receive the bread as grace, not as the terms of their loyalty.
