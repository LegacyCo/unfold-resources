## Commentary
The Philistines had captured the ark of the covenant in battle, believing they had secured a great victory over Israel and its God. But what they initially regarded as a trophy of war had become a source of terror. Everywhere the ark was taken, the people experienced the judgment of God. First in Ashdod, where Dagon fell before the ark and the people were afflicted with tumors (==1 Samuel 5:1–6==), then in Gath, and finally in Ekron. By the time we arrive at verse 11, the Philistine leaders have reached a unanimous conclusion: the ark must go.

They had witnessed undeniable evidence of the power and authority of Israel's God. Their own god had fallen before Him, their cities had suffered under His hand, and their leaders were powerless to stop what was happening. Yet rather than turning to the Lord in repentance, they demanded that His ark be removed.

They wanted relief from His judgments without surrendering to His authority.

It is possible to acknowledge the power of God without submitting to Him. A person can recognize that God is real, that His Word is true, and even that His hand is at work in their circumstances, while still refusing to yield their life to Him.

Something similar happens in ==Mark 5:1–17==. Jesus delivers a demon-possessed man in the country of the Gadarenes, demonstrating extraordinary authority over the powers of darkness. But when the people of the region see what has happened, they beg Jesus to leave their territory. Rather than welcoming the One who had just demonstrated His power to deliver, they ask Him to go away.

In both accounts, the presence of God confronts people with a decision. They can respond by humbling themselves before Him, or they can attempt to remove the uncomfortable reality of His presence from their lives.

There is also a significant connection to the preceding chapter. When the ark was captured, Eli's daughter-in-law named her child Ichabod, declaring, "The glory has departed from Israel" (==1 Samuel 4:21==). The Hebrew word for glory is kabod, which carries the idea of weight or heaviness. Now, in ==1 Samuel 5:11==, we are told that God's hand was "very heavy" upon the Philistines. The Hebrew word translated heavy comes from the same root as kabod.

The glory that Israel believed had departed was now demonstrating its weight among the Philistines. God had not been defeated when His ark was captured. He had not lost His authority when Israel lost the battle. And He certainly did not need Israel to rescue Him from the Philistines.

The entire account reminds us that God's presence cannot be manipulated, possessed, or controlled. Israel had tried to use the ark to secure victory without obedience, while the Philistines had attempted to possess it as a trophy of their conquest. Neither understood that the God of Israel cannot be reduced to something people use for their own purposes.

## Application
There is a practical warning here for us in this passage. When the Lord confronts something in our lives that is inconsistent with His nature, our first instinct may be to distance ourselves from the discomfort rather than surrender what He is confronting. But the proper response to the conviction of God is never to push Him away. It is to humble ourselves, acknowledge what He is revealing, and allow His presence to accomplish its intended work in us.
