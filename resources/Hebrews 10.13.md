## Commentary
“But this Man, after He had offered one sacrifice for sins forever, sat down at the right hand of God, from that time *waiting* till His enemies are made His footstool” (==Hebrews 10:12-13==).

Hold both halves of that sentence, because together they name the tension of the age we live in. Jesus is *seated*—the work is finished, the sacrifice offered once and forever, the throne occupied above every power and every name that is named (==Ephesians 1:21==). And Jesus is *waiting*—because His enemies, though defeated, have not yet been made His footstool. The kingdom has come, and the kingdom has not yet come in its fullness. Victory accomplished; kingdom not yet fully established. Our enemies are defeated, but they aren’t yet imprisoned. Our King is on the throne—but that throne is in heaven, not yet in a new Jerusalem.

The footstool image itself is worth unpacking. In the ancient Near East, when a king conquered his enemies, the vanquished were brought low so the victor could rest his feet on their necks—Joshua did exactly this with the five kings at Makkedah: “Come near, put your feet on the necks of these kings” (==Joshua 10:24==). A footstool isn’t a battlefield image. It’s a *throne room* image. The fighting is over; what remains is the formal, public placement of the defeated under the feet of the King. Hebrews is telling us which stage of the war we live in: after the decisive battle, before the ceremony.

So, if the work is finished, what exactly is Jesus waiting *for*? Go back to the psalm this verse quotes. Psalm 110 doesn’t just say “sit at My right hand till I make Your enemies Your footstool”—it tells us how the footstool gets built: “The LORD shall send the rod of Your strength out of Zion. *Rule in the midst of Your enemies*. Your people shall be volunteers in the day of Your power” (==Psalm 110:2-3==). The King reigns in the midst of enemies through a volunteer people. Paul closes the loop with one of the most stunning sentences in the New Testament: “the God of peace will crush Satan under *your* feet shortly” (==Romans 16:20==). Under whose feet? Yours. Christ is the head, and the church is His body (==Ephesians 1:22-23==)—and where does a body keep its feet? The enemies of the Head go under the feet of the Body. He isn’t waiting because something is missing from His victory. He’s waiting on a people to walk His victory out—and, Peter adds, His patience has a merciful motive: He is “longsuffering toward us, not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance” (==2 Peter 3:9==). Every day the ceremony is delayed is another day the door of mercy stays open.

Israel lived in this same tension. When Pharaoh and his army went under the Red Sea, Israel’s enemy was decisively defeated—but they weren’t instantly standing in the Promised Land. There was a wilderness between the victory and the inheritance: a season of shedding the old life and learning the new one. And the wilderness wasn’t wasted time—God led them there “to humble you and test you, to know what was in your heart” (==Deuteronomy 8:2==). Slaves don’t become sons overnight. The defeat of Pharaoh was God’s work alone; the possession of the land required a people who would walk it out. Both were true. Neither canceled the other.

That’s where the church lives now—between the empty tomb and the enemies-turned-footstool. And the in-between is a dangerous place to get comfortable. Kingdom life is costly, so instead of crossing the wilderness to possess what the victory purchased, many believers pitch a permanent camp on the shore of the Red Sea. They stay at the place where the victory happened—grateful Pharaoh is dead—but they never move toward the inheritance the victory was *for*. Paul refused that campsite: “I press on toward the goal for the prize of the upward call of God in Christ Jesus” (==Philippians 3:14==). The seated King is not anxious, and He is not in doubt—He’s waiting. And what He’s waiting on, in part, is a people who implement His victory until the kingdoms of this world become the kingdoms of our God and His Christ (==Revelation 11:15==).

## Application
Living in the tension means refusing two opposing errors. The first is triumphalism—pretending the fullness is already here, so every sickness should vanish on the first prayer and every wilderness is a sign of failure. It isn’t; there’s still a “not yet,” and Jesus Himself is still waiting. 

The second is defeatism—living as if the victory hasn’t happened, praying *for* a win the cross already secured, treating a defeated enemy like a reigning one. 

So check yourself this week: when you hit something contrary to the kingdom—sickness, temptation, discouragement, attack—ask whether you’re responding like someone waiting to win or someone enforcing a win. Then volunteer. Psalm 110 says the King’s power moves through a volunteer people; be one, in the specific place where the enemy is still pretending he didn’t lose.

## Cross References
The verse Jesus’ throne-room posture comes from is ==Psalm 110:1==—“Sit at My right hand, till I make Your enemies Your footstool”—the Old Testament verse most quoted in the New. See also ==1 Corinthians 15:25==, “For He must reign till He has put all enemies under His feet,” and ==Acts 3:21==, where heaven receives Him “until the times of restoration of all things.”
