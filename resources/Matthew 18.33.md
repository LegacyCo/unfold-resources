## Commentary
“Should you not also have had compassion on your fellow servant, just as I had pity on you?”

The first servant owed the master ten thousand talents—in modern terms, something like fourteen billion dollars. An absurd, unpayable sum, which is exactly the point: that’s the debt we owed God (==Matthew 18:24==). The master forgave all of it. Then that debt-free servant found a fellow servant who owed him about four thousand dollars—and had him thrown into prison over it (==Matthew 18:28-30==).

Here’s what the parable does *not* say: it never says the second debt was fake. Four thousand dollars is real money. And this is where the teaching gets honest about your situation—the person who hurt you really does owe you. They took something: your trust, your joy, your reputation, maybe your plan for the future. The debt is legitimate. Forgiveness has never meant pretending otherwise. It means looking at a real debt, held by someone who genuinely owes it, and releasing them from it—just as you were released from an unpayable one. The gospel is unbending here precisely because the math is so lopsided: fourteen billion forgiven cannot coexist with four thousand demanded.

And Jesus ends the parable by pointing out that the unforgiving servant is handed to the torturers, “so My heavenly Father also will do to you if each of you, from his heart, does not forgive his brother his trespasses” (==Matthew 18:34-35==). Unforgiveness is its own torture chamber—ask anyone who has spent years replaying a wound. And forgiving others is how received forgiveness proves it actually landed (==Matthew 6:14-15==). So take out the ledger you’ve been keeping, look honestly at what they owe—and then do with it what the Master did with yours. Nail it to the cross, where a certificate of debt has been canceled before (==Colossians 2:14==).
