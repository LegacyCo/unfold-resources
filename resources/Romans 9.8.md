## Commentary
Paul is addressing a difficult question in Romans 9: If Israel was God's chosen people, and God had made extraordinary promises to Abraham and his descendants, how could so many Israelites now be rejecting their own Messiah? Had God's promises somehow failed?

Paul answers this question by making an important distinction between physical descent and participation in the promise of God. In verse 6, he writes, "For they are not all Israel who are of Israel," and then explains in verse 8, "Those who are the children of the flesh, these are not the children of God; but the children of the promise are counted as the seed."

To understand what Paul means, we have to return to the story of Abraham.

God had promised Abraham that He would make him into a great nation and that through his descendants all the families of the earth would be blessed (==Genesis 12:2–3==). Yet Abraham and Sarah remained childless for decades. Eventually, Sarah gave her servant Hagar to Abraham, and Ishmael was born. But Ishmael was not the son through whom God had determined to establish His covenant. God told Abraham, "Sarah your wife shall bear you a son, and you shall call his name Isaac; I will establish My covenant with him" (==Genesis 17:19==).

Both Ishmael and Isaac were biological descendants of Abraham. Both could legitimately claim Abraham as their father. And God even promised to bless Ishmael and make him into a great nation (==Genesis 17:20==). But it was Isaac who would carry forward the particular covenant promise God had made.

This is the distinction Paul is making, and two words he uses in verse 8 help us understand it: *flesh* and *regard*.

When Paul refers to the "children of the flesh," the Greek word is _sarx_, which in this context refers to natural, physical descent. He is not describing people who are living sinful or immoral lives, as he does elsewhere in Romans. He is simply referring to those who are descendants of Abraham by biological birth. Ishmael was just as much Abraham's son as Isaac was. But being born into Abraham's family did not automatically make someone an heir of the covenant God had established with him.

Then Paul says that the children of the promise are "regarded as descendents." The Greek word translated "regarded" is _logizomai_, which means to reckon, recognize, or count someone as belonging to a particular category. It's the same word Paul uses in Romans 4 when explaining how Abraham's faith was accounted to him for righteousness (==Romans 4:3==). Here, Paul is distinguishing between those who can trace their ancestry to Abraham and those whom God recognizes as heirs of His covenant promise.

In other words, _physical ancestry and covenant identity are not the same thing._ A person could be descended from Abraham without automatically being counted among the heirs of the promise. This is why Paul says, "In Isaac your seed shall be called" (==Genesis 21:12==). Isaac's place in the covenant rested upon what God had spoken, not on who his father was.

Consider also the circumstances surrounding Isaac's birth. Abraham was nearly one hundred years old, and Sarah was well beyond the natural age of childbearing. There was nothing about their physical condition that could have produced the child God had promised. Isaac existed because God had spoken, and God was faithful to His word.

The people of promise owe their existence to the faithfulness of God, and they respond by submitting their lives to this God who gives life to the dead.

This becomes especially significant when we consider what Paul has already explained about Abraham in Romans 4. God's intention was never to produce an ethnic nation through Abraham. He had promised to make him "a father of many nations" (==Romans 4:17==). The blessing that began with Abraham was always intended to reach beyond Abraham's physical descendants.

This is why Paul can later write, "And if you are Christ's, then you are Abraham's seed, and heirs according to the promise" (==Galatians 3:29==).

The promise ultimately finds its fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Through faith in Him, people from every nation are brought into the family of Abraham and made participants in the inheritance God promised. They do not have to possess Abraham's bloodline to become heirs of Abraham's blessing. They belong to Christ, and therefore they are counted among the children of promise.

This does not mean God has abandoned Israel or revoked His faithfulness to the Jewish people. Paul will explicitly reject that conclusion in ==Romans 11:1–2==. Rather, he is demonstrating that God's promises have always operated according to His grace and purpose, not according to human ancestry.

## Application
There is a practical implication here for anyone who has grown up surrounded by Christianity.

It is possible to inherit the traditions of faith without personally participating in the life those traditions were meant to reveal. Someone can grow up in a Christian home, attend church for decades, possess considerable biblical knowledge, and still misunderstand what it means to belong to God. Just as physical descent from Abraham was not sufficient to make someone a child of promise, proximity to the things of God is not the same as a surrendered life of repentance and faith.

Our confidence before God cannot rest in our religious history, family background, personal accomplishments, or even our efforts to live faithfully. It must rest in the One who made the promise and has fulfilled it in His Son.

The same God who brought life out of Sarah's barrenness has brought us into His family through the death and resurrection of Jesus Christ. Our place in that family is not something we have produced for ourselves. It is something God has accomplished by His grace, and which we receive through faith.

We are children of promise because God has been faithful to His word.
