## Commentary
“As Jesus passed on from there, He saw a man named Matthew sitting at the tax office. And He said to him, ‘Follow Me.’ So he arose and followed Him.”

Matthew introduces himself with remarkable simplicity: “a man named Matthew.”

The name Matthew comes from the Hebrew _Mattityahu_, meaning “gift of Yahweh” or “gift of the LORD.” Mark and Luke call him “Levi” in their accounts of the same event (==Mark 2:14==; ==Luke 5:27–28==), and Mark identifies him more specifically as “Levi the son of Alphaeus.” This suggests that Matthew was probably known by both names, something not unusual in the first-century Jewish world.

Before becoming one of Jesus’ disciples, Matthew was a tax collector.

Matthew says Jesus found him “sitting at the tax office.” The Greek word is _telōnion_, referring to a tax or customs booth. Because Jesus was ministering around Capernaum at this point (==Matthew 9:1==), Matthew was likely collecting tolls and customs duties connected with the movement of goods through the region.

Capernaum sat near important trade routes around the Sea of Galilee, making it a natural place for collecting tolls and customs duties. Matthew likely worked within the taxation system of Herod Antipas, Rome’s client ruler in Galilee.

Tax collectors were widely despised. The system was vulnerable to abuse, and collectors were often viewed as collaborators who profited from the burdens placed on their own people. This is why the Gospels repeatedly group “tax collectors” with “sinners” (==Matthew 9:10–11==; ==Matthew 11:19==; ==Matthew 18:17==).

We are never told that Matthew personally defrauded anyone. But his profession alone would have made him socially suspect and hated.

And this is the man Jesus calls.

There is no recorded interview. No probationary period. No demand that Matthew first repair his reputation. Jesus just walks to the tax booth and says two words:

“Follow Me.”

Luke emphasizes the cost of Matthew’s response: “So he left all, rose up, and followed Him” (==Luke 5:28==).

For fishermen like Peter, Andrew, James, and John, returning to their boats remained at least theoretically possible. Matthew’s decision carried a different kind of finality. Walking away from an appointed position like his would mean surrendering a livelihood that would not be waiting for him if following Jesus didn't pan out.

Matthew got up anyway.

He soon used his home and relationships to introduce others to Jesus. Luke tells us that Levi “gave Him a great feast in his own house,” attended by “a great number of tax collectors and others” (==Luke 5:29==). Matthew's first recorded act after following Jesus was to place Jesus in the middle of the community he already knew.

Beyond the New Testament, traditions about Matthew’s later missionary work differ considerably. Various ancient sources place his ministry in regions including Ethiopia, Persia, and areas east of the Roman Empire, but these traditions developed later and cannot be established with certainty. The circumstances of his death are also unknown. 

What we know with certainty is something more powerful than where Matthew lived and died.

Matthew was a tax collector.

Jesus saw him.

Jesus called him.

Matthew got up.

And from that moment forward, the man who had spent his life collecting from Israel became one of twelve men sent to give the good news of Israel's Messiah to the world.
