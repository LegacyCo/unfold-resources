## Commentary
“And the disciples were first called Christians in Antioch.”

The early church had what we might call a brand—but look at what it was. They were known as “The Way” (==Acts 19:23==)—people identified by how they lived, following the way of Jesus. And at Antioch they picked up the name *Christians*: literally, Messiah-people. Under persecution, they even had a logo of sorts—the simple fish that marked meeting places and tombs and distinguished friend from foe. But notice what all of it did: the name gave definition to what they believed, purpose to what they were doing, and boundary to what was acceptable. “Why won’t you burn incense in Caesar’s temple?” “Because we aren’t Caesar people. We’re Messiah people.” The name told the world whose they were—and told the family what they would and wouldn’t do.

That’s the right order, and it’s worth checking in our day of church branding: does the name serve the family, or does the family serve the name? A clear identity is good—the church needs to know who she is and what she’s for. But the moment the brand becomes the thing being built—protected, promoted, and polished while the people become the audience for it—the order has flipped, and we’ve started building something in man’s image instead of God’s (==Ephesians 2:19-22==).

And notice that the Antioch believers didn’t name themselves. The city named them—because Messiah was evidently all they talked about, all they lived, all they were. The best identity a church family can have is the one that gets forced on it by a watching community: “those people belong to Jesus.” One author described the fellowship God designs us for as a people “large enough to dare, but small enough to care.” That’s a family worth being named after Him. When the world looks at your church and needs a word for what it sees, may the only word that works be His.
