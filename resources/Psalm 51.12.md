## Commentary
“Restore to me the joy of Your salvation, and uphold me by Your generous Spirit.”

This is David after Nathan called out his sin—after the adultery, the cover-up, and the murder. Read the whole prayer and notice something what's missing: David never says, “I guess my sin proves I was never really a man after God's heart.” He does not allow what he did to re-identify him. He prays for mercy according to God's lovingkindness (==Psalm 51:1==), asks for a clean heart and a steadfast spirit (==Psalm 51:10==), pleads not to be cast from God's presence (==Psalm 51:11==)—and then says *restore to me*. You can only ask for something to be restored if it is rightfully yours. At his lowest moment, David runs to God as a man who belongs to God, asking for the return of what sin had interrupted, not for readmission to an identity he assumed he had forfeited.

That distinction is the difference between conviction and condemnation. Condemnation says the sin is now your name; conviction drives you home as a son asking for what is yours to be restored (==Luke 15:21-22==). And God answered David the way He answers that kind of repentance—immediately: “The LORD also has put away your sin; you shall not die” (==2 Samuel 12:13==). There were consequences that followed David, but there was no re-identification. He remained the man after God's own heart, and the psalm he wrote from the wreckage became the church's prayer of repentance for three thousand years. When a believer sins, the way back is David's way: refuse the false name, run toward the presence rather than away from it (==Genesis 3:8==), and ask the Father to restore the joy that belongs to His children.
