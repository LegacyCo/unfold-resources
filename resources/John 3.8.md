## Commentary
“The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear the sound of it, but cannot tell where it comes from and where it goes. So is everyone who is born of the Spirit.”

Jesus is making a play on words that works in both biblical languages. The Greek word for spirit, *pneuma*, and the Hebrew word, *ruach*, both mean the same three things: breath, wind, spirit. So when Jesus explains being born of the Spirit, He points out the window: think about wind.

Wind has no boundaries. It isn’t contained by any defined space, and it can’t be fenced. And wind does something solid things can’t do: it merges. When the wind coming from the north meets the wind coming from the south, where is the north wind and where is the south wind? Are there two winds blowing through town? No—they become one wind, one breeze moving through the community. They don’t sit side by side; they join, without either being destroyed. That’s the picture Jesus chose for the born-again life, and it tells us something wonderful about what the new birth actually is: spirit can be joined to Spirit with no separation. When you are born of the Spirit, God’s ruach and your spirit aren’t stacked next to each other like bricks—they’re joined like winds: “he who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with Him” (==1 Corinthians 6:17==).

And notice the last thing Jesus says: “so is *everyone* who is born of the Spirit.” Not only is the Spirit like the wind—the Spirit-born person becomes wind-like too. You hear the sound of their life; you see the effects—the peace that doesn’t match their circumstances, the love that doesn’t match their history—but you can’t explain them by natural categories, because their source isn’t visible. Where did that come from? Where is it taking them? You can’t tell. That’s not a flaw in the born-again life. That’s the family resemblance.
