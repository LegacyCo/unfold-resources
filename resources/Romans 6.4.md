## Commentary
“Therefore we were buried with Him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, even so we also should walk in newness of life.”

Most of us have been taught that baptism is a symbol—a picture of the death, burial, and resurrection that happened when we believed. I used to be okay with that. Not anymore. You don’t have to agree with me, but hear me out and take it to the Lord in prayer.

Look at Paul’s actual language. He doesn’t say baptism *illustrates* being buried with Christ. He says we were buried with Him *through* baptism. The word baptize itself means to immerse, to submerge—and Paul says we have been immersed into Christ Jesus, immersed into His death (==Romans 6:3==). This is the same apostle who treats the Lord’s table as far more than a memory aid—Corinthians were weak, sick, and dying because they took communion in an unworthy manner (==1 Corinthians 11:29-30==), and symbols don’t have the power to do that. The church has historically recognized two sacred acts given to her: communion and baptism. If something real happens at the table, something real happens in the water. An exchange takes place between the natural world and the spiritual world. Baptism is a defining moment in our walk with Jesus.

And what happens is glorious: an ending and a beginning. Our old self was crucified with Him, that the body of sin might be done away with (==Romans 6:6==)—“for he who has died has been freed from sin” (==Romans 6:7==). The water is a grave for everything you inherited from Adam. But nobody stays in a grave that belongs to Jesus. Just as the Father’s glory raised Christ, you come up out of that water to *walk in newness of life*—resurrection life, now, not someday.

## Word Study
- *united together* : **symphytos** (Greek, ==Romans 6:5==) — literally “grown together”; a botanical word used for plants grafted into one another until they share one life and can no longer be separated. Paul says we have become symphytos with Christ in the likeness of His death—and therefore certainly in the likeness of His resurrection.

Summary: baptism language isn’t spectator language. A picture hangs on a wall next to its subject; a graft grows into the tree until there’s only one life in it. Paul chose the graft.
