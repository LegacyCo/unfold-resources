## Commentary
“And when the cloud departed from above the tabernacle, suddenly Miriam became leprous, as white as snow. Then Aaron turned toward Miriam, and there she was, a leper.”

The cloud lifts, and Miriam is a leper. In Israel, that meant you were unclean, and everyone had to know it. The law required a leper to tear his clothes, leave his head bare, cover his mouth, and cry out, “Unclean! Unclean!” (==Leviticus 13:45==). Those are the signs of mourning. A leper lived as though he were at his own funeral. Aaron says as much when he pleads for her: “Please do not let her be as one dead” (==Numbers 12:12==).

It also meant you lived alone. “He shall dwell alone; his dwelling shall be outside the camp” (==Leviticus 13:46==). No home, no family table, no place in the community. Anyone who touched you became unclean too. That's why the ten lepers who met Jesus “stood afar off” (==Luke 17:12==).

But most of all, it meant you were cut off from the presence of God. Lepers were sent out of the camp “that they may not defile their camps in the midst of which I dwell” (==Numbers 5:3==). The tabernacle stood at the center of the camp, and a leper couldn't come near it. When King Uzziah was struck with leprosy, Scripture says: “he was cut off from the house of the LORD” (==2 Chronicles 26:21==).

And there was nothing anyone could do about it. The priest could examine a leper and pronounce him unclean or clean, but he couldn't heal him. When the king of Israel was asked to cure Naaman, he said, “Am I God, to kill and make alive?” (==2 Kings 5:7==). Healing a leper was something only God could do.

Now put Miriam in that situation. She wanted a higher place in the camp, and suddenly she had no place in it at all. She'd claimed to be a voice for God, and now the only thing she'd be calling out was “Unclean!” And Aaron, the high priest, the man whose job was to examine lepers and declare them unclean (==Leviticus 13:2-3==), turns around and finds himself looking at his own leprous sister. Scripture doesn't say why Aaron wasn't struck too. But he understood what he was seeing. His next words are a confession: “we have done foolishly and… we have sinned” (==Numbers 12:11==).

Not every leper in the Bible was under judgment. Naaman wasn't, and neither were the men Jesus healed. But three times leprosy falls on someone who tried to take what wasn't theirs: Miriam here, Gehazi when he ran after Naaman's silver (==2 Kings 5:27==), and Uzziah when he walked into the temple to do the priests' work (==2 Chronicles 26:16-21==). And Israel was told never to forget this instance: “Remember what the LORD your God did to Miriam on the way when you came out of Egypt!” (==Deuteronomy 24:9==).

All of this is the background for what happens when a leper comes to Jesus. “Jesus put out His hand and touched him, saying, ‘I am willing; be cleansed’” (==Matthew 8:3==). He touched the one nobody could touch, and He didn't become unclean. The leper became clean. And in the end Jesus took the leper's place: “Jesus also, that He might sanctify the people with His own blood, suffered outside the gate” (==Hebrews 13:12==).

## Word Study
**Leprous** is the Hebrew *tsara'at* (צָרַעַת). It's a broader term than the disease we call leprosy today. It covers a range of serious skin conditions, and Leviticus even uses it for a kind of mold or decay in clothing and in the walls of a house (==Leviticus 13:47==; ==Leviticus 14:34==). What they have in common is that they made a person or a thing unclean and unfit to be near God's dwelling. Summary: the Old Testament treats *tsara'at* less as a sickness to be cured and more as an uncleanness to be cleansed, which is why the Gospels speak of lepers being “cleansed.”
