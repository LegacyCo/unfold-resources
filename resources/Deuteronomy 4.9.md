## Commentary
The encouragement to “keep yourself” is a consistent charge in Scripture. The Hebrew word here means to watch over, guard, pay careful attention to. The first time this word is used in Scripture is when God commissions Adam to tend the garden. Humanity was placed in Eden not just to enjoy the garden, but to guard it, cultivate it, and keep watch over what God had entrusted to them.

That same language now gets turned inward.

Moses is telling Israel to tend the garden of the heart with all diligence. “Only take care, and keep your soul diligently…” In other words, do not live carelessly with what God has shown you. Do not treat revelation as something casual. Do not let the memory of God’s works become overgrown with neglect.

This is the danger Moses is addressing. Israel had seen the fire. They had heard the voice (5x Moses reiterates this point in this passage). They had stood at Sinai while God revealed Himself in power and holiness. But even sacred encounters can fade from memory if they are not tended to. 

That's why Moses says, “lest you forget the things that your eyes have seen, and lest they depart from your heart all the days of your life.”

Forgetting in Scripture is not just a failure to remember. It is often a failure of worship. It's what happens when the soul stops recalling the faithfulness of God. The eyes saw clearly, but the heart stopped beholding. What was once holy becomes familiar. What was once treasured becomes assumed.

We find this language in the New Testament, too. Jude writes, “Build yourselves up on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit, keep yourselves in the love of God…” (Jude 20–21). The charge remains the same: pay attention to the condition of your soul. Stay awake and responsive to the love of God.

But this encouragement to “keep yourself” is not an exercise in legalism or self-effort. It’s a participation in grace initiated by God.

It is God who first keeps *us*, and only in *His* keeping are we given the grace to keep ourselves. Jude ends his letter by saying, “To Him who is able to keep you from stumbling…” (Jude 24). Paul says it this way: “I press on, that I may lay hold of that for which Christ Jesus has also laid hold of me” (Philippians 3:12).

That's the order of grace.

Christ lays hold of us, and because He has laid hold of us, we press on to lay hold of Him. God keeps us, and because He keeps us, we learn to keep our souls. Indeed, we love because He first loved us (1 John 4:19). 

Moses also connects this keeping to generational faithfulness: “Make them known to your children and your children’s children.” The soul that keeps watch over the works of God becomes a witness to the next generation. What is treasured in the heart is eventually passed through the mouth.

This means spiritual forgetfulness is never a private failure. When one generation stops rehearsing the works of God, the next generation is left with commandments detached from testimony. They inherit rules without remembrance and receive traditions without wonder.

## Application
This call from Moses is not just for Israel. It is for all God's people: watch over the garden of your heart, remember what your eyes have seen, and teach these things to your children.

There are things God has shown you, spoken to you, delivered you from, and carried you through that must not be left unattended. Recall them. Speak of them. Tell them to those around you. Let remembrance become a form of worship.

Your heart will not stay tender by accident. The garden must be tended. So keep yourself.
