## Commentary
“Love never fails.” We’ve put that on so many greeting cards that we can miss what Paul actually claims here. This chapter isn’t first a description of how we should love—it’s a description of how God *does* love, because God is love (==1 John 4:8==). So when Paul says love never fails, he’s telling you something about the love of your Father: it bears all things, endures all things, is not provoked, suffers long—and it never, ever fails.

Religion wants you to believe that your sin can have the same effect on God’s love that the Babylonian army had on Solomon’s temple—that your disobedience tears His love down into ruins, and then it’s your faith and your good works that slowly rebuild what your failure destroyed. But the Father’s love for you never needs to be rebuilt, because it never falls apart.

Other loves in your life may have failed. A parent’s love, a spouse’s love, a friend’s love—people you counted on may have lost their way with you. That wound is real. But the Father’s love is not one more human love, just bigger. It’s a different kind altogether: a love that has never once lost its way with you and never will (==Romans 8:38-39==). What could possibly separate you from a love like that?

## Word Study
- *fails* : **pipto** (Greek) — to fall, to collapse, to lose one’s way, to abandon. Paul isn’t saying love always gets the outcome it wants; he’s saying love never falls down on you.

Where else Scripture uses pipto:

- ==Luke 10:18== — “I saw Satan fall (*pipto*) from heaven like lightning.”
- ==Acts 15:16== — “I will rebuild the tabernacle of David, which has fallen (*pipto*) down.”

Satan pipto-ed. David’s tabernacle pipto-ed and had to be rebuilt. But in the same breath that Paul says prophecies will fail, tongues will cease, and knowledge will vanish away, he says love never pipto-s. Everything else in the verse comes to an end. The love of the Father is the one thing in the list that cannot fall.
