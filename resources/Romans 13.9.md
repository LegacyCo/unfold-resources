## Commentary
"For the commandments, 'You shall not commit adultery,' 'You shall not murder,' 'You shall not steal,' 'You shall not bear false witness,' 'You shall not covet,' and if there is any other commandment, are all summed up in this saying, namely, 'You shall love your neighbor as yourself.'"

Paul has just told the believers in Rome to pay what they owe: taxes, customs, respect, honor (==Romans 13:7==). Then he names one debt that never gets paid off. "Owe no one anything except to love one another, for he who loves another has fulfilled the law" (==Romans 13:8==). You can pay your taxes in full. You'll never be finished loving your neighbor. 

Paul's list comes from the second half of the Ten Commandments, the ones about how we treat each other. Each of them protects something that belongs to your neighbor. Adultery takes his marriage. Murder takes his life. Stealing takes his property. False witness takes his good name. Every one of them is a way of using another person to get something for yourself.

But the last commandment is different. "You shall not covet" doesn't describe anything you do with your hands. It's about what you desire. No court in Israel could convict a man for coveting, and Paul was very familiar with this commandment: "I would not have known covetousness unless the law had said, 'You shall not covet'" (==Romans 7:7==). 

Coveting is usually where the other four sins begin. One evening King David walked out on his roof and "saw a woman bathing, and the woman was very beautiful to behold" (==2 Samuel 11:2==). He wanted her, and he took her. When she became pregnant, he tried to cover his mistake with deception, and when that failed, he arranged for her husband Uriah to die in battle (==2 Samuel 11:6–17==). When the prophet Nathan confronted him, he told it as a story about theft: a rich man with flocks of his own who took a poor man's only lamb (==2 Samuel 12:1–4==). Adultery, murder, deceit, stealing. Every one of them grew out of something David wanted that belonged to his neighbor. As James says, "You lust and do not have. You murder and covet and cannot obtain" (==James 4:2==).

The commandment against coveting tells you what *not* to want. The command Paul is pointing at in this section tells you *what* to want: "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" (==Leviticus 19:18==). Coveting and love both start with a desire. Coveting looks at your neighbor and wants what he has. Love looks at him and wants what's good for him. "As yourself" takes the care we naturally give ourselves and turns it toward someone else. As Paul told the Philippians, "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others. Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus" (==Philippians 2:4–5==).

## Word Study
**Summed up** is *anakephalaioō* (ἀνακεφαλαιόω), to bring many things together under one head, the way a long argument is gathered into its main point. The New Testament uses it only twice. The other is ==Ephesians 1:10==, where God's plan is "that He might gather together in one all things in Christ." Summary: every commandment about our neighbor is gathered under love, the same way all creation will one day be gathered under Christ.

## Application
Notice what you've caught yourself wanting lately that belongs to someone else: his success, her marriage, their house, his reputation. Then pray for that person by name, and ask God to bless them in the very thing you've envied.
