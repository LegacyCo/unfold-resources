## Commentary
Paul uses a specific verb in this verse when he describes the Christian's relationship with hospitality — _diōkō_. It means to run after, pursue, even hunt for. 

Most translations say something like, “practice hospitality,” which is not wrong, but it can sound too passive. It can make hospitality feel like something we do when the opportunity happens to appear in front of us. Someone needs a meal, so we make one. Someone asks for help, so we respond. Someone comes over, so we open the door.

But Paul’s word is more urgent than that.

He is not merely saying, “Be open to hospitality if the moment comes.” He is saying, “Pursue it. Look for it. Chase it down. Make it part of the way you move through the world.”

Hospitality is easy to admire and hard to practice. We like the idea of an open table, an open home, an open life. But the actual practice of hospitality is an interruption. It costs time. It rearranges schedules. It touches our sense of privacy, our convenience, our money, our energy, and often our desire to keep life manageable.

Which is exactly why Paul tells the church to pursue it.

Hospitality doesn't usually happen by accident. It has to be determined.

And Paul is not talking about entertaining.

Entertainment is often about presentation. Hospitality is about welcome.

Entertainment says, “Look at what I have prepared.” Hospitality says, “There is room for you here.”

Entertainment can become a performance. Hospitality is an act of love.

Entertainment often requires everything to be clean, impressive, and controlled. Hospitality can happen with paper plates, leftovers, tired eyes, and a house that looks like people actually live in it.

The point is not the perfection of the environment. The point is the posture of the heart.

This is why hospitality belongs at the center of Christian life. The gospel itself is the story of divine hospitality. God did not wait for us to make ourselves presentable before He welcomed us — while we were still sinners, Christ died for us (Romans 5:8). He did not open His table to us because we were impressive guests. He received us by grace. 

So when Christians practice hospitality, we are not just being nice. We are bearing witness to the God who welcomed us.

We are saying with our homes, tables, schedules, and lives: “What God has done for me, I am eager to extend to you.”

That is why Paul says to *pursue* hospitality. It's not a spiritual gift reserved for extroverts with large dining rooms. It is a Christian virtue. It may look different from person to person, but it belongs to all of us.

For one person, it may mean inviting someone into their home.

For another, it may mean buying coffee and giving someone an hour of undistracted attention.

For another, it may mean making space at the lunch table, checking on the lonely, hosting a small group, welcoming the new family at church, or refusing to let people remain invisible.

The form can vary. The pursuit cannot.

Paul is calling the church to become the kind of people who notice the stranger, the outsider, the overlooked, the weary, and the displaced — and then move toward them with the welcome of Christ.

Hospitality is not something we extend only when it is convenient. True hospitality is a grace the Lord has shown us; therefore, we become eager to show it to others. As Peter says in 1 Peter 4:9, "Offer hospitality to one another without grumbling."

## Application
Don't wait until for things to feel manageable before you practice hospitality. That day will probably never come.

Start smaller, but start intentionally.

Ask the Lord to show you one person this week who needs to be welcomed, noticed, fed, encouraged, or brought near. Then pursue them. Send the text. Make the invitation. Set another plate at the table. Give someone access to your life in a way that costs you something.

Hospitality begins when we stop asking, “Do I have room for this?” and begin asking, “Who has God placed near me that needs to experience His welcome through me?”
