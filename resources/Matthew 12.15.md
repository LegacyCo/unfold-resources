## Commentary
Jesus didn't withdraw because He was afraid of the Pharisees or unwilling to confront their hypocrisy. He had just confronted them publicly, exposing the hardness of their hearts and their distorted understanding of the Sabbath. Rather, His withdrawal reveals something about the way Jesus understood His assignment. He wasn't allowing the hostility of others to determine the timing or direction of His ministry.

Jesus knew that His death was necessary, but He also knew that His hour had not yet come (==John 7:30==; ==John 8:20==). He would eventually surrender Himself to those who wanted to kill Him, but He would do so according to the Father's will, not because His enemies had managed to force His hand. Even His death would be an act of willing obedience rather than an unfortunate consequence of religious opposition (==John 10:17–18==).

Matthew makes this even more significant by connecting Jesus' withdrawal to Isaiah's prophecy concerning the Servant of the Lord (==Isaiah 42:1–4==). In the verses that follow, Matthew explains that Jesus' conduct fulfilled the words, "He will not quarrel nor cry out, nor will anyone hear His voice in the streets" (==Matthew 12:19==). Jesus wasn't interested in defending His reputation. He was the Spirit-anointed Servant who would faithfully accomplish the purposes of God without resorting to the methods of those who opposed Him.

Yet notice that His withdrawal wasn't a withdrawal from people. Matthew immediately tells us that great multitudes followed Him, "and He healed them all." He withdrew from the hostility of the Pharisees while continuing to minister to those who came to Him. His refusal to engage in unnecessary conflict didn't diminish His compassion for those around Him.

## Application
Not every confrontation is ours to engage, and not every accusation deserves a response. There are times when faithfulness requires us to stand and speak, and there are other times when that same faithfulness requires us to withdraw. Jesus demonstrated both. 

We can become so occupied with defending ourselves, correcting others, or answering our critics that we lose sight of the very work God has entrusted to us. Jesus refused to let His enemies become the center of His attention. He remained occupied with His Father's business, and He trusted the Father with the timing and outcome of His obedience.
