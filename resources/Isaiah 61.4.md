## Commentary
In February 1945, near the end of WWII, Allied bombing devastated the city of Dresden. One of the city’s great landmarks, a church called the Frauenkirche, collapsed in the bombing.

For decades, it remained a massive heap of ruin in the middle of the city. The pile of stones sat there for nearly fifty years. A testimony of destruction. 

But after German reunification in 1990, the people of Dresden began the work of restoring the church.

The thing is, they didn’t throw the old stones away. They carefully sorted through the rubble. Using old plans, photographs, and modern technology, they put as many of the original stones as possible back into the restored church. 

Of course, the original stones were charred by fire and even disfigured by the bombs. So when the restoration was complete, the marks of war were clearly seen in the church. 

The people of Dresden didn’t erase the scars of the past. 

Those scars were incorporated into the restoration and became a testimony of healing.

That’s a picture of Kingdom restoration.

God doesn’t restore us by pretending the past never happened. He restores by taking what was broken and placing it within a testimony greater than the pain.

Wounds become scars and scars become testimonies. 

And just like the scars on Jesus, our scars tell a story too. But rather than only telling stories of brokenness and pain, they now tell the story of restoration.

The ones who have been restored become living stones in the house God is building — witnesses to the world of what the kingdom of God can do with broken things (see 1 Peter 2:4-5).
