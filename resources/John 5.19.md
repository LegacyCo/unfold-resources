## Commentary
“Then Jesus answered and said to them, ‘Most assuredly, I say to you, the Son can do nothing of Himself, but what He sees the Father do; for whatever He does, the Son also does in like manner.’”

Jesus says this to religious leaders demanding an explanation for what He did at Bethesda—a pool surrounded by a multitude of the sick, where He healed one man and left (==John 5:3==; ==John 5:6-9==). His defense is not an argument about the Sabbath. It is a description of how He operates: the Son does nothing of Himself, only what He sees the Father doing. The eternal Son, with power to meet every need at that pool, voluntarily bound His actions to the Father's initiative. He did not see the need and assume the response. He was not led by the crisis; He was led by His Father—and that made Him a man at rest in the middle of overwhelming need.

The same principle keeps Him still when Lazarus is dying: He loved Lazarus, and He stayed two more days in the place where He was, because the Father was not yet moving (==John 11:5-6==). Nothing moved Jesus but obedience—not need, not pressure, not even the grief of people He loved. His eyes stayed vertical, and the vertical gaze always produced a horizontal response: He *did* heal the man at the pool, and He *did* go to Lazarus. But the horizontal response was the secondary consequence. What was ultimate was watching the Father. This is the pattern for everyone who serves in His name. Without it, a compassionate believer sees a great need, adopts the need as a calling, and asks God to bless it. He becomes reactionary and worn down by whatever problem is loudest. With it, a servant of God does what the Father is doing, and finds that His assignments come with His rest (==John 15:5==).
