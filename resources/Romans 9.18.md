## Commentary
“Therefore He has mercy on whom He wills, and whom He wills He hardens.”

This is one of the more difficult statements in Paul's letter to the Romans. On the surface, it seems to suggest that God arbitrarily decides whom He will show mercy and whom He will harden, raising difficult questions about human responsibility, free will, and the character of God. If God is the One hardening someone's heart, how can that person be held responsible for resisting Him?

To understand what Paul is saying, we need to return to the story he has just referenced in the preceding verse: God's dealings with Pharaoh during the Exodus.

When Moses first appeared before Pharaoh and commanded him to release the Israelites, Pharaoh responded, "Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, nor will I let Israel go" (==Exodus 5:2==). Pharaoh was not an innocent man who simply happened to become the object of divine judgment. He was a powerful ruler who had enslaved God's people, participated in their oppression, and repeatedly refused God's command to release them.

As the plagues unfolded, the book of Exodus describes the hardening of Pharaoh's heart in two different ways. Sometimes Pharaoh is said to harden his own heart (==Exodus 8:15==; ==Exodus 8:32==; ==Exodus 9:34==), while at other times we are told that the Lord hardened his heart (==Exodus 9:12==; ==Exodus 10:20==).

This distinction is important.

Although God had announced beforehand that He would harden Pharaoh's heart (==Exodus 4:21==), the narrative initially emphasizes Pharaoh's own resistance. He repeatedly refuses to listen, even when confronted with unmistakable demonstrations of God's power. It is not until the sixth plague that the text explicitly states that the Lord hardened Pharaoh's heart.

The Greek word Paul uses for "hardens" is _sklērynō_, which means to make hard, rigid, or stubborn. When applied to the heart, it describes a condition of obstinacy in which a person becomes increasingly resistant to correction or persuasion. Paul is not simply describing someone who struggles to believe or has difficulty understanding God's will. He is describing a heart that has become resistant to God.

But notice what happens in the Exodus account. God does not appear to force Pharaoh to desire something contrary to what he already wanted. Pharaoh had repeatedly demonstrated his unwillingness to obey, and God's hardening confirmed and strengthened that resistance. One way many interpreters have understood this is as an act of judicial hardening, in which God gives someone over to the direction they have persistently chosen.

We find a similar principle earlier in Paul's letter. In ==Romans 1:24–28==, Paul repeatedly says that God "gave them up" or "gave them over" to the desires and thinking they had embraced. His judgment was expressed, at least in part, by allowing people to continue down the path they had chosen.

This does not mean that God's involvement in Pharaoh's hardening was merely passive. Paul explicitly attributes the hardening to God's will, and we should be careful not to explain away the force of his statement. God was actively governing these circumstances to accomplish His purposes. But neither should we ignore the repeated testimony of Exodus that Pharaoh was responsible for his own rebellion.

Both truths are present in the biblical account.

And this brings us to something particularly important about God's purpose in hardening Pharaoh's heart.

In the preceding verse, Paul quotes God's declaration to Pharaoh: "For this very purpose I have raised you up, that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth" (==Romans 9:17==).

God's purpose was not simply to punish Pharaoh. Through the events of the Exodus, He was revealing His power, demonstrating His authority over the gods of Egypt, delivering His covenant people, and making His name known among the nations.

Pharaoh's resistance did not prevent God's purposes from being accomplished. Even his rebellion was ultimately made to serve the revelation of God's glory.

This is part of the larger argument Paul is making throughout Romans 9. He is answering the question of whether God's promises have failed because so many Israelites have rejected their Messiah. Just as God was able to accomplish His purposes through Pharaoh's resistance, He has not lost control of His redemptive plan because of Israel's present unbelief.

In fact, Paul will later describe a hardening that has occurred within Israel itself (==Romans 11:7==; ==Romans 11:25==). Yet remarkably, he does not describe this hardening as the end of God's dealings with Israel. He explains that Israel's stumbling has opened the way for salvation to come to the Gentiles, and that God's purposes of mercy for Israel have not been exhausted (==Romans 11:11–12==).

This is why we need to be careful about drawing conclusions from Romans 9:18 that go beyond Paul's argument. The verse certainly affirms God's sovereignty in showing mercy and hardening hearts. But it does not, by itself, explain every dimension of how divine sovereignty relates to human freedom or settle the eternal destiny of every person who experiences hardening. These are questions Christians have wrestled with for centuries, and different theological traditions have understood Paul's language differently.

What Paul is making unmistakably clear is that God is not at the mercy of human resistance. His purposes are not dependent upon human cooperation, and His faithfulness cannot be overturned by human unbelief.

There is also a sobering personal warning in Pharaoh's story.

Every time God confronted Pharaoh, he had an opportunity to humble himself. But rather than surrendering, he repeatedly resisted. Even when he temporarily acknowledged his sin and asked Moses to pray for relief, he returned to his previous rebellion once the pressure was removed (==Exodus 9:27–35==).

It is possible to become so accustomed to resisting God that our hearts grow increasingly insensitive to His voice. What begins as a refusal to obey in one area of life can eventually become a settled posture of resistance toward Him.

This is why the writer of Hebrews warns, "Today, if you will hear His voice, do not harden your hearts" (==Hebrews 3:15==).

Notice that the warning is addressed to people who are hearing His voice. The danger is not simply ignorance of what God has said, but the refusal to respond to what He has revealed.

When the Lord confronts us through His Word, convicts us by His Spirit, or reveals something in our lives that needs to change, we should not treat those moments casually. They are opportunities to respond to His grace.

The proper response to a passage about God's sovereignty is not to become preoccupied with whether God might harden our hearts, but to humble ourselves before the One who is presently speaking.

Pharaoh heard the word of the Lord repeatedly and refused to yield. Moses heard the word of the Lord and, despite his initial fears and objections, ultimately obeyed.

Both encountered the same God. Both were confronted by His authority. But their responses were profoundly different.

We cannot control the purposes of God, nor should we imagine that He is obligated to act according to our understanding. But we are responsible for how we respond to the light He has given us.
