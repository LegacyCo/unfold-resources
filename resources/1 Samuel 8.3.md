## Commentary
“But his sons did not walk in his ways; they turned aside after dishonest gain, took bribes, and perverted justice.”

Samuel had seen this situation before. He grew up in Eli's house, where Eli's sons were stealing from the sacrifices and “did not know the LORD” (==1 Samuel 2:12==). As a boy, he was the one who had to deliver God's word of judgment on that family (==1 Samuel 3:11-14==). Now he's an old man, and it's his own sons who have gone astray.

Their names make the situation even more sad. Joel means “the LORD is God.” Abijah means “the LORD is my father.” Samuel named them in faith. And his own record was spotless. At the end of his life he could stand in front of the whole nation and ask, “whose ox have I taken… or from whose hand have I received any bribe?” and nobody could name a thing (==1 Samuel 12:3-4==). So this verse isn't about a father who modeled corruption. It's about sons who had a godly example right in front of them and chose another path. One of the lessons of this passage is that faith can be taught and modeled for the next generation, but it can't be handed down. Every generation has to choose the path of faith for themselves (==Judges 2:10==).

This verse names three things Samuel’s sons did, and God’s law had clearly forbidden each one for anyone who was to be a judge of God's people. First, they “turned aside after dishonest gain.” When judges were first appointed in Israel, they had to be men “hating covetousness” (==Exodus 18:21==). Joel and Abijah loved the thing a judge was required to hate. Second, they “took bribes.” Third, they “perverted justice.” Moses had ruled out both for Israel's judges: “You shall not pervert justice; you shall not show partiality, nor take a bribe, for a bribe blinds the eyes of the wise” (==Deuteronomy 16:19==). The three happen in that order. A judge who wants money will accept it from the people whose cases he’s hearing, and a judge who has been paid will rule for the one who paid him.

Scripture doesn't blame Samuel the way it blamed Eli, who “did not restrain” his sons (==1 Samuel 3:13==). But verse 1 says Samuel “made his sons judges,” and that hadn't been done before. Judges were raised up by God (==Judges 2:16==), and when Israel tried to make Gideon's family a dynasty, he refused: “I will not rule over you, nor shall my son rule over you; the LORD shall rule over you” (==Judges 8:23==). A calling from God can't be passed down like a family business. And the failure of these two sons is what gives Israel's elders their opening to ask for a king (==1 Samuel 8:5==).

## Word Study
**Turned aside** and **perverted** are the same Hebrew verb, *natah* (נָטָה)—to bend, to lean, to turn. The sons bent toward money, and then they bent justice. **Dishonest gain** is *betsa'* (בֶּצַע), profit taken by cutting corners or by force. It's the word in Jethro's requirement that judges be men “hating covetousness” (==Exodus 18:21==), and in the proverb, “He who is greedy for gain troubles his own house, but he who hates bribes will live” (==Proverbs 15:27==). Both words come together again in a prayer: “Incline my heart to Your testimonies, and not to covetousness” (==Psalm 119:36==). Summary: a heart is always leaning somewhere. The psalmist asks God to lean his toward the Word, because a heart that leans toward gain will eventually bend everything else with it.
