## Commentary
When Matthew tells us that the people of Nazareth were “offended at Him,” he is describing something more significant than their simply being upset with Jesus. The Greek word translated “offended” is skandalizō, which means to stumble, take offense, or encounter something that becomes an obstacle to belief. The people of Nazareth were confronted with the wisdom and authority of Jesus, but rather than recognizing who He was, they stumbled over what they thought they already knew about Him.

The people who knew Jesus the longest weren't questioning whether He possessed extraordinary wisdom or whether remarkable miracles had taken place. They were questioning how someone so familiar to them could possibly possess such authority.

These were people who had watched Jesus grow up. They knew His family, His occupation, and the ordinary circumstances of His life. Yet their familiarity with Him had become an obstacle to recognizing the extraordinary revelation of God standing before them.

This is what makes Jesus' response so significant: “A prophet is not without honor except in his own country and in his own house.”

Throughout Israel's history, the prophets were frequently rejected by the very people to whom God had sent them. Their rejection wasn't necessarily because the people lacked evidence that God was speaking. Often, the problem was that God's word came through people they were unwilling to receive. Jeremiah was opposed by the people of his own hometown (==Jeremiah 11:21==), and Jesus would later lament Jerusalem's history of killing the prophets and rejecting those sent to her (==Matthew 23:37==).

Now the same pattern was being repeated in Nazareth.

But there's an even deeper issue here concerning the incarnation. The eternal Son of God had entered the world through the ordinary circumstances of human life. He was born into a family, raised in a small town, and worked with His hands. Nothing about His outward appearance demanded that people recognize Him as the Messiah. As Isaiah had prophesied concerning the Servant of the Lord, “He has no form or comeliness; and when we see Him, there is no beauty that we should desire Him” (==Isaiah 53:2==).

The people of Nazareth couldn't reconcile the humanity of Jesus with the authority He demonstrated. They expected someone extraordinary to arrive in an extraordinary way. And because Jesus didn't fit their expectations, they rejected the very One God had sent.

Faith requires us to receive God's revelation on His terms, even when the way He chooses to reveal Himself challenges our expectations. 

The passage concludes by telling us that Jesus “did not do many mighty works there because of their unbelief” (==Matthew 13:58==). This doesn't mean their unbelief somehow diminished His power. Jesus wasn't dependent upon their faith in order to possess the authority of God. Rather, their refusal to receive Him meant that they did not experience the fullness of what He had come to make available to them. In the midst of their unbelief, Jesus couldn't be Himself.

It's a sobering conclusion. The people who had enjoyed the greatest familiarity with Jesus were among those least willing to receive Him.
