## Commentary
It may be the strangest turn in the Gospels: Peter rebukes the Man he just crowned. Moments after confessing “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God,” Peter hears Jesus explain that the Christ must go to Jerusalem, suffer many things, and be killed (==Matthew 16:21==)—and he takes the King aside to correct Him: “Far be it from You, Lord; this shall not happen to You!”

It’s hard to understand on this side of the cross, but a *suffering* Messiah was unthinkable to Israel. They were anticipating the Son of David on a white horse—a deliverer who would overthrow Rome the way David felled his enemies. And there’s the problem: Israel saw their greatest enemy as something external that needed to be defeated by force. But their real enemy wasn’t external. Israel’s greatest enemy was not Rome. It was sin.

And we are no different from Peter. Our biggest problem is not out there—it’s in here. That doesn’t mean we ignore the issues in our world; it means that until the interior enemy is dealt with, we have no authority over the exterior ones. Our enemy is not poverty; it’s pride. It’s not what others say about us; it’s our need to defend ourselves. Our enemy is not injustice, immigration, corrupt politicians, the cost of living, artificial intelligence, or any of the causes we build our outrage around. Those are external issues that distract from the true enemy—and all our relentless moat-digging around our little castles does nothing to keep that enemy out, because the enemy isn’t outside the walls. He’s within them.

This is why the Messiah came to die rather than to conquer. Internal enemies can’t be defeated by strength; they can only be defeated by sacrifice. A sword can topple Rome, but it cannot touch sin. So Jesus set His face toward Jerusalem—not the throne room Peter wanted, but the cross Peter feared—because that was the only battlefield where humanity’s actual enemy could be destroyed (==Colossians 2:14-15==).

## Application
Name your Rome—the external thing you’ve decided is the real problem: the politics, the person, the circumstance, the culture war. Now ask the harder question: what's the internal enemy that all that outrage is distracting you from? Pride, fear, unforgiveness, the need to be right?

Peter wanted victory without a cross, and Jesus called that thinking satanic (==Matthew 16:23==)—the things of men, not the things of God. Let the Messiah fight the actual war: surrender the inner enemy to the only weapon that defeats it, His sacrifice. Deal with the enemy within first. Then you'll have authority worth carrying out there.
