## Commentary
The glory of the Lord, which had once filled the temple as a sign of God's dwelling among His people, is now beginning to depart. The same glory that filled the tabernacle in ==Exodus 40:34–35== and Solomon's temple in ==1 Kings 8:10–11== is moving toward the threshold, preparing to leave the place where God had chosen to make His presence known.

What makes this moment particularly tragic is that the temple was still standing. The priests were still functioning, the religious structures remained in place, and Jerusalem was still regarded as the dwelling place of God. Yet the people had filled His sanctuary with idolatry and their lives with injustice. They had come to presume upon God's presence while continually rejecting His ways. They believed that because they possessed the temple, they were secure from judgment. But the temple itself could not guarantee God's protection when the people had abandoned the covenant relationship it represented.

Notice, however, that God's glory does not depart all at once. Throughout these chapters, Ezekiel watches as the glory moves in stages. It rises from the cherubim to the threshold (==Ezekiel 9:3; 10:4==), later moves from the threshold to the cherubim (==Ezekiel 10:18==), and eventually departs the city altogether, resting on the mountain east of Jerusalem (==Ezekiel 11:23==). God had warned His people repeatedly through the prophets, calling them to repentance and offering opportunities to return. His departure was not impulsive. It was the culmination of generations of persistent rebellion against His goodness.

## Application
This passage raises an important question about the difference between maintaining the outward structures of our relationship with God and actually living in communion with Him. It is possible to preserve religious practices, maintain ministry activity, and continue gathering for worship while our hearts grow increasingly distant from the One for whom all these things exist.

Under the new covenant, we should not confuse this vision with the idea that God's Spirit abandons His children whenever they fail. Rather, Ezekiel reminds us that God's presence is the greatest gift we have been given, and that we should never become so accustomed to the activities of worship that we lose sight of the One we have gathered to worship.
