## Commentary
The most famous sculpture in the world may be Michelangelo’s *David*. What most people don’t know is that the massive block of marble Michelangelo used had already been rejected—by two other sculptors, who found the grain of the stone too flawed to work with. For twenty-five years, that eighteen-foot block sat in a church courtyard in Florence: exposed to the weather, unfinished, unwanted.

Then Michelangelo saw it. And where others saw a defective block, he saw something hidden inside—something worth years of hammer and chisel to set free.

That’s Ephesians 2:10: “For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them.” We too sat outside the church, separated from the life of God, passed over, written off (==Ephesians 2:12==). But God took the rejected block. He sees a masterpiece inside each of us—it’s just covered by things that need to be removed. And our obedient yes to Him is what places us under the chisel of the loving Master.

Which means the chips flying off your life right now may not be evidence of destruction. Sculpting and shattering can look similar from the outside; the difference is whose hands hold the hammer. The Master never strikes to break the stone—only to release what He already sees in it. And He’s specific about the finished form: there are good works *prepared beforehand* with your name on them, waiting for the sculpture to walk out of the block.

## Word Study
- *workmanship* : **poiēma** — a thing made; a work of craftsmanship or artistry. It’s the root from which we get our word *poem*. This word appears only twice in the New Testament:

- ==Romans 1:20== — creation itself: “the things that are *made*.” The first *poiēma* is the universe.
- ==Ephesians 2:10== — the church: “we are His *workmanship*.” The second *poiēma* is you.

Summary: God has signed exactly two masterworks—the cosmos, and His redeemed people. The heavens declare His glory by what they are; His people declare it by what He is making of them. You’re not a salvage project. You’re the second poem.
