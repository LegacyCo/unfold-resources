## Commentary
“I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us.”

Paul does not say suffering is insignificant. That would be insulting and dishonest.

Paul had been beaten, imprisoned, rejected, betrayed, stoned, shipwrecked, hungry, sleepless, and repeatedly brought face-to-face with death (==2 Corinthians 11:23–28==). When Paul writes about “the sufferings of this present time,” he is not writing from the safety of a theological classroom. His theology of suffering was written into his body.

He writes, “I consider…”

The Greek word is _logizomai_, meaning to reckon, calculate, reason, or come to a conclusion after consideration. Paul has considered whether the very real suffering he has experienced for the name of Christ compares with the hopeful expectation this gospel promises.

He has placed suffering on one side of the scale and the promise of eternal glory on the other, and his conclusion is that there is no comparison.

This does not diminish suffering at all. It magnifies the promise of glory.

Sometimes we read this verse as though Paul is saying, “What you are going through really isn’t that bad.” That is not his point at all. Some suffering _is_ that bad. There are losses that change you permanently. There are wounds you carry for the rest of your life. There are prayers that seem to go unanswered, people who do not come back, seasons you would never willingly walk through again. The gospel does not require us to pretend those things do not hurt.

Paul's claim is far more audacious: however great the suffering is, the promises of God in Christ Jesus are infinitely greater.

Backing up to the previous verse, we can follow Paul's train of thought and identify a pattern:

“...if indeed we suffer with Him, that we may also be glorified together” (==Romans 8:17==).

Suffering, then glory.

Cross, then resurrection.

Humiliation, then exaltation.

Death, then life.

This is the pattern we see most clearly in Jesus. Paul tells the Philippians that Jesus “humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross. Therefore God also has highly exalted Him” (==Philippians 2:8–9==).

In the kingdom of God, the way to the crown is always _through_ the cross. Before Jesus stepped into glory, His path went the way of suffering.

And the servant is not greater than his Master.

This is one of the places where our expectations about following Jesus often need to be corrected. We tend to assume that obedience should move us progressively away from difficulty. If God is pleased with us, things should get easier. Doors should open. Opposition should disappear. The road should straighten itself out beneath our feet.

But Scripture repeatedly tells another story.

Noah obeyed, and then spent decades building.

Abraham obeyed, and then waited.

Joseph received a promise, and then entered a prison.

Israel left Egypt, and then entered a wilderness.

David was anointed, and then lived in caves.

Jesus obeyed, and then carried a cross.

Paul obeyed the heavenly vision, and then discovered just how much he would suffer for the name of Jesus (==Acts 9:15–16==; ==Acts 26:19–21==).

Sometimes suffering is what obedience looks like while the reward is still coming.

But Paul goes even further than saying suffering comes before glory. Elsewhere he says suffering can actually *produce something:*

“Our light affliction, which is but for a moment, is working for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory” (==2 Corinthians 4:17==).

Suffering is not neutral. It is *working*. In the hands of God, suffering becomes productive.

It produces endurance (==Romans 5:3–4==). It produces maturity (==James 1:2–4==). It deepens fellowship with Christ (==Philippians 3:10==). It strips away false sources of strength and teaches us to depend upon the God who raises the dead (==2 Corinthians 1:8–9==).

This does not mean every tragedy comes with an explanation we will receive in this life. Scripture never requires us to manufacture a reason for someone's pain. There are mysteries we may carry all the way into resurrection.

But there is an enormous difference between saying, “I understand why this happened,” and saying, “God will not waste what happened.”

Paul gives us permission to say the second even when we cannot say the first.

This is why Christian hope is fundamentally different from optimism. Optimism looks at present circumstances and says, “Things will probably get better.”

Christian hope looks directly at suffering and says, “Even if they don't, resurrection is coming.”

Our hope is that there is an age coming in which every grave opens, every tear is wiped away, every enemy of God is placed beneath the feet of Jesus, creation is liberated from corruption, and the children of God finally stand within creation clothed with resurrection glory (==Romans 8:19–23==; ==1 Corinthians 15:42–49==; ==Revelation 21:4–5==).

The Christian does not endure suffering because suffering is beautiful. We endure because Jesus is beautiful, because resurrection is real, and because glory is coming.

There will be a day when you will look back upon every tear, every wilderness, every unanswered question, every costly act of obedience, and every scar carried faithfully in following Jesus—and the weight of what God has prepared will make the suffering seem impossibly small by comparison.
