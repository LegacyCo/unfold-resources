## Commentary
Theologians refer to the importance of the first time a word is used as the "law of first mentions." This means the initial occurrence of a word, doctrine, or theme in Scripture establishes a foundational meaning that continues throughout the Bible. Abraham Heschel points out in his book *The Sabbath* that this is the first time the word "holy" (qadosh) is used in Scripture. "Holy" is a word that represents the mystery and majesty of the divine. And what does God first distinguish in creation as holy? A mountain...an altar?

No. A day.

Not an object. Not a sacred space. God first gives the quality of holiness to a moment in time.

Heschel goes on to point out that it was only *after* Israel had worshipped a *thing*, the golden calf, that the holiness of a space/thing was commanded; the tabernacle. The holiness of time came first, the holiness of man came second, the holiness of space last.

We treat places as holy—buildings, sanctuaries, mountains—and time as something to spend. God's first act of sanctification says the opposite: before there was ever a temple to enter, there was a day to enter. Heschel called the Sabbath "a palace in time," and wrote that "the Sabbaths are our great cathedrals." You cannot buy a ticket to this cathedral or drive to it. You can only *stop*—and find that God has been waiting inside the hours you were rushing through.

And notice *why* God makes the day holy: "because in it He rested from all His work." Not because He was tired—the Creator of the ends of the earth neither faints nor grows weary (==Isaiah 40:28==). God rested the way an artist steps back from a finished canvas. The work was complete, and He stopped to delight in it. Holiness enters creation not through effort but through *ceasing*.

There's something tender here for Adam, too. He was created on the sixth day—which means his first full day of life was not a workday. Before he ever dressed the garden or named a single animal's offspring, he woke into God's rest (==Genesis 2:15==). Man's first day was spent enjoying a finished work he had contributed nothing to. That's the order of the kingdom: we don't work our way toward rest; we work *from* it.

This is the rest Jesus stands inside when He says, "The Sabbath was made for man, and not man for the Sabbath" (==Mark 2:27==)—and the rest He now *is*: "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (==Matthew 11:28==). Hebrews reaches all the way back to this verse to describe our salvation: "there remains a rest for the people of God," and whoever enters it "has himself also ceased from his works as God did from His" (==Hebrews 4:9-10==). The seventh day was pointing at something all along. Rest is not just a day God blessed. In Christ, it's an inheritance we live in.

## Application
Sanctify time before you sanctify anything else—that's God's own order. Pick the hours this week and mark them the way you'd mark ground for an altar: on the calendar, defended, non-negotiable. Then practice ceasing, not just stopping: no producing, no proving, no catching up. Put the phone in another room. Set the table. Linger with your people and your God inside a finished work. If the golden calf teaches anything, it's that whoever will not worship God in time will eventually worship things—so enter the cathedral He built first.
