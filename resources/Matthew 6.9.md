## Commentary
“In this manner, therefore, pray: Our Father in heaven, hallowed be Your name.”

The first word Jesus puts in His disciples’ mouths is *Father*. Not “Almighty God,” not “Judge of all the earth,” not “Master”—titles that are true, and that a first-century Jew would have reached for first. Jesus opens the model prayer with the family word. And if He is Father, then the one praying is a son or a daughter. You are approaching Him as a child—not as a servant, not as a sinner, not as a beggar negotiating for scraps.

This is why prayer is so often misunderstood and neglected: we don’t know who we are when we come to God in prayer. If a person sees himself as a servant, prayer becomes work—effort and striving to get it right, to say the correct things with the correct verses for long enough to convince God of his seriousness. Prayer becomes a duty that produces guilt when neglected and exhaustion when performed. But a child climbing into his father’s lap isn’t performing anything. Jesus explicitly removed the performance elements before giving the prayer: not for show like the hypocrites (==Matthew 6:5==), not with vain repetitions like the pagans who “think that they will be heard for their many words”—“for your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him” (==Matthew 6:7-8==). The Father already knows. The praying is not information delivery; it is communion.

## Application
Your prayer life reveals what you actually believe about your identity. A believer can affirm sonship in doctrine and still pray like a servant. “You did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry out, ‘Abba, Father’” (==Romans 8:15==). Come to Him as a son who knows his inheritance, not a servant trying to earn something from a master. Come in rest. He taught us to say Father because that is who He is to us—and He wants prayer to feel like it.
