## Commentary
“My soul, wait silently for God alone, for my expectation is from Him.”

David is talking to himself in this verse—commanding his own soul to do something that doesn’t come naturally: wait, silently, for God *alone*. The silence is not emptiness; it is focus. And the last clause explains why the posture makes sense: “my expectation is from Him.” A soul convinced that everything it hopes for comes from one source can afford to be still before that source. A soul that has spread its expectations across many sources will always be too busy managing them to wait on any.

There is a real difference between praying and waiting, and most of us have far more experience with the first. Andrew Murray draws the line in *Waiting on God*: “If the question be asked, whether waiting on God be anything different from what we normally do when we pray, the answer is, that there may be much praying but very little waiting on God. In praying we are often occupied with ourselves, with our own needs, and our own efforts in the presentation of these needs. In waiting upon God, the first thought is of the God upon whom we wait. We enter His presence, and feel we need just to be quiet, so that He, as God, can overshadow us with Himself. God longs to reveal Himself, to fill us with Himself. Waiting on God gives Him time in His own way and divine power to come to us.”

That is the distinction this verse teaches. Prayer occupied with our needs keeps us at the center of our own praying; waiting puts God at the center of it. Both belong in a believer’s life—Scripture commands petition (==Philippians 4:6==)—but a prayer life that is all asking and no waiting never gives God what Murray describes: time to come to us in His own way. So build the silence in deliberately. Fix your attention on Him rather than the list. Ask, “Lord, what are You doing right now?”—and then be quiet long enough for the answer to matter. The soul that waits for God alone is the soul that finds its expectation was placed correctly (==Psalm 62:1-2==).
