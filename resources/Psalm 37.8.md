## Commentary
This psalm is about refusing agitation in a world where the wicked seem to prosper. David is addressing his inner turbulence that rises because evil appeared to be winning. “Do not fret” is repeated because fretting feels small at first, but it's not small. It's the inward heating of the soul. It's that churning irritation and anxiety that comes when we look at men, circumstances, injustice, delay, or opposition and lose our peace.

The English word fret comes from the Old English _fretan_, meaning to devour, to eat, to gnaw into something. The Hebrew word David actually used as he wrote in his original language is _charah_ which has, at its root, the idea of growing warm and blazing up.

Let's put these two pictures together. Think of worry as a rat inside your soul, gnawing away. Think of Satan as an arsonist, setting little blazes of distress inside your heart. King David is saying something like this in Psalm 37: “I have been young, and now I’m old (see v25). I’ve seen many things, suffered many burdens, and learned many lessons. Based on a lifetime of experience, my advice is: ‘Kill off the rats and put out the fires. Do not fret, it only causes harm.’"

That is why verse 8 is so sharp: "Cease from anger and abandon wrath; Do not fret (or "get upset"); it only leads to evildoing." David does not treat fretfulness as harmless emotion. He treats it as a spiritual condition that, if not checked, will carry a man into sin. The troubled soul, untended to, begins speaking out of its own frustration, reacting from its own confusion, resenting others, envying the wicked, and moving outside the fear of the Lord.

This is important because many people excuse the inward life, as though sin only begins when the outward act appears. Our interior world is the seedbed for either righteous living or sinful behavior ("Out of the mouth the heart speaks" - Luke 6:45). Sin often starts an internal agitation that hasn't been surrendered to God. A heart that is unsettled by fear, anger, envy, or frustration becomes vulnerable to doing evil because it's no longer resting in the Lord. Once peace is forfeited, the flesh begins offering its own solutions.

## Application
David’s instruction is not to simply, “Calm down.” It's a call back into rest. The whole psalm moves in that direction: trust in the Lord, delight yourself in the Lord, commit your way to the Lord, rest in the Lord, wait patiently for Him. Because only trust can keep the soul from becoming inflamed. Only rest can keep irritation from turning into rebellion. Only confidence in God can prevent inward pressure from becoming outward sin.

Fear, frustration, and inward agitation are not safe places to live.

A troubled soul eventually reaches for a fleshly answer. That's the warning of this verse. If I stand in fear, if I nurse irritation. If I let agitation remain, then I'm already leaning toward evil. I may not have acted yet, but I've stepped onto the soil where sin grows.

So when fear rises, don't tolerate it. When agitation comes, don't justify it. When your soul begins to boil, don't treat it like a minor thing. Get out of it quickly. Repent. Bring your heart back under the rule of the Prince of Peace. "Return to your rest, O my soul" (Psalm 116:7).
