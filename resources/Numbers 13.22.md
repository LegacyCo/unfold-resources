## Commentary
The Anakim were an ancient people known for their extraordinary height and intimidating physical stature. Their name comes from Anak, whose ancestor Arba was apparently a prominent figure among them. In fact, Hebron was originally known as Kiriath Arba, meaning "City of Arba" (==Joshua 14:15==; ==Joshua 15:13==). 

There's also an interesting connection between the Anakim and the Nephilim mentioned in ==Genesis 6:4==. In ==Numbers 13:33==, the spies explicitly identify the descendants of Anak with the Nephilim, the mysterious figures described before the flood as mighty men of old. This has led some to conclude that the Anakim were literal descendants of the Nephilim. However, the passage doesn't provide enough information to establish that genealogy with certainty. The statement comes from the spies' fearful report, and it may reflect their understanding of the Anakim as belonging to an ancient class of unusually powerful men. Elsewhere, the Anakim are associated with the Rephaim, another group remembered for their great stature (==Deuteronomy 2:10–11==).

But the most significant detail of this verse is where these men were living.

Hebron wasn't just another Canaanite city. It was one of the most significant locations in Israel's ancestral history (roughly 20 miles south of what would become Jerusalem). Abraham had settled there after separating from Lot and built an altar to the Lord (==Genesis 13:18==). It was also where Sarah died and where Abraham purchased the cave of Machpelah as a burial place for his family (==Genesis 23:17–20==). Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebekah, Jacob, and Leah would eventually be buried there.

In other words, the spies were standing in a place deeply connected to the covenant promises God had made to Abraham. The land God had promised to give his descendants was now occupied by some of the most intimidating people they had ever encountered.

Forty-five years after the spies go through the land, Caleb, who had been one of the twelve spies, specifically requested Hebron as his inheritance. At eighty-five years old, he was still determined to take possession of the land God had promised. He knew the Anakim were there, but he was *still* confident that the Lord would enable him to drive them out (==Joshua 14:6–12==).

And ==Joshua 15:14== tells us that Caleb drove out the very three descendants of Anak mentioned in ==Numbers 13:22==: Sheshai, Ahiman, and Talmai.

The men who had terrified Israel's first generation were eventually defeated by the man who had refused to surrender to their fear.

According to ==Joshua 11:21–22==, although the Anakim were driven out of Israel's hill country, some remained in the Philistine cities of Gaza, Gath, and Ashdod. This becomes particularly interesting when we remember that Goliath, the giant David defeated, came from Gath (==1 Samuel 17:4==). Scripture also identifies other unusually large Philistine warriors from Gath as descendants of Rapha, a name associated with the Rephaim (==2 Samuel 21:15–22==).

Although Scripture doesn't explicitly identify Goliath as a descendant of Anak, the geographical and ancestral connections make it a reasonable possibility.

Consider the contrast between these two generations of God's people. When Israel first encountered the Anakim, the spies became convinced that their enormous size made taking the land impossible. Yet centuries later, David stood before a giant from one of the very cities where the Anakim had survived and declared, "The battle is the Lord's" (==1 Samuel 17:47==).

The presence of the Anakim in Hebron didn't invalidate God's promise to Abraham. It revealed whether Israel would trust the God who had made that promise.

Caleb understood this when the rest of his generation did not. He never denied the strength of the people occupying the land. He simply refused to allow their strength to become greater in his estimation than the faithfulness of God.

And the fact that Hebron eventually became Caleb's inheritance is a remarkable testimony to God's covenant faithfulness. The very place that had once represented an insurmountable obstacle to Israel's unbelieving generation became the possession of a man who had believed God would do exactly what He promised.
