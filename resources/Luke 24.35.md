## Commentary
“And they told about the things that had happened on the road, and how He was known to them in the breaking of bread.”

Jesus had walked with them for miles. He had opened the Scriptures to them until their hearts burned (==Luke 24:32==). And still they didn’t recognize Him—until He took bread, blessed it, broke it, and gave it to them. The Word explained on the road was made visible at the table. Augustine called communion exactly that: “the Word made visible.”

There are great mysteries in this gospel of ours—profoundly deep things even angels long to look into (==1 Peter 1:12==). But at the center of it all sits a fact so simple that its best ritual is marked by bread and wine. God uses common things as channels of His revelation. We bring bread and wine, and we get back Christ. The Bible describes the gospel. Sermons talk about the gospel. But the table brings the gospel to life—it’s a reenactment we take into our hands and mouths.

And there are mysteries Jesus still wants to show us that are broken open the same way they were at Emmaus: in the breaking of bread. He reveals Himself at His table—because how can you really know someone until you’ve shared a meal with them?

We never come to the end of the mystery of bread and wine. So let’s move beyond mere symbolism and embrace it: where bread is flesh and wine is blood, where servants are kings and crosses are thrones, where water is living and wind is Spirit, where God’s people, gathered around a table in faith, gather around Christ Himself.
