## Commentary
“Let him who stole steal no longer, but rather let him labor, working with his hands what is good, that he may have something to give him who has need.”

Paul skips the step we expect. We’d expect: stop stealing, get a job, provide for yourself. But that’s not where the sentence lands. The reason the thief goes to work is “that he may have something *to give* him who has need.” The kingdom mentality of work is not working to provide for yourself—it’s working to provide for the one who has a need. Your own needs being met is given and implied; the man who doesn’t provide for his own household is worse than an unbeliever (==1 Timothy 5:8==). But in the kingdom, provision for yourself is the floor of work, not the point of it.

The thief and the ordinary self-focused worker actually share an assumption: my hands exist to serve my consumption. Paul converts the hands entirely—from taking, past merely earning, all the way to giving. This is why kingdom people practice discipline in their consumption: not because money is evil, but because a person who spends everything on himself has no surplus, and a person with no surplus has nothing in his hands when he meets a need. The poor will always be among us (==Deuteronomy 15:11==), and doing good—especially to the household of faith (==Galatians 6:10==)—requires margin.

## Application
Ask yourself a different question about your work this week. Not “Is this job providing for me?” but “Who is my labor positioning me to bless?” Then build the margin on purpose: leave room in the budget the way a farmer leaves the edges of his field unharvested for the ones who need it (==Leviticus 19:9-10==). When your hands produce more than your household consumes, you’ve stopped working like the world—you've started working like a citizen of the kingdom.
