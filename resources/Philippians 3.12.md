## Commentary
Many of us want God the way we want exercise. We want the results—we just don’t want the cost.

The saints whose biographies we read didn’t shape history that way. None of them were passively waiting for God to move in their generation. They grabbed hold of the promise that God is found by those who seek Him, dared to believe the gospel offered more than their current reality, and read “eye has not seen, nor ear heard… the things God has prepared for those who love Him” (==1 Corinthians 2:9==) as a word for *their* day. Then they made a conscious decision to surrender everything in their pursuit of God—and whatever it cost was whatever it cost. For Rees Howells, the Welsh intercessor, it cost his reputation. For Hudson Taylor, the pioneer missionary to China, it cost his homeland, two wives, and four children. For Amy Carmichael in India, it cost the approval of the people she admired most, plus poverty, legal battles, and decades of physical affliction.

And behind them all stands Paul: “I have suffered the loss of all things… that I may know Him” (==Philippians 3:8-10==). Was he describing his conversion on the Damascus road? No—listen to the verse tense here in verse 12: “Not that I have already attained… but I *press on*, that I may lay hold of that for which Christ Jesus has also laid hold of me.” This is the ongoing rhythm of his life, decades in: forgetting what’s behind, reaching forward, pressing toward the prize. And then the line that won’t let us file this under “extreme Christianity”: “Therefore let us, as many as are *mature*, have this mind” (==Philippians 3:15==). Paul calls this mindset maturity. Pressing on isn’t the radical fringe of the faith. It’s normal.

So here’s the question that sorts our desires from our decisions: who’s willing to suffer the loss of temporal things to gain a measure of Christ that transforms cities and nations? God intends to restore all things through Jesus (==Acts 3:21==)—and He does that work through a people who refuse to be satisfied when what’s around them disagrees with the victory of the cross.
