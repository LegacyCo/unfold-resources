## Commentary
“And Abraham came near and said, ‘Would You also destroy the righteous with the wicked?’”

God had just disclosed His plans to Abraham—“Shall I hide from Abraham what I am doing?” (==Genesis 18:17==)—and told him that the outcry against Sodom had come up before Him. Now look at Abraham’s response to the revelation. He doesn’t announce the coming judgment. He doesn’t distance himself from the city under it. He *comes near*—steps closer to God—and begins to intercede for people who had no idea a conversation about them was happening.

That movement is the heart of intercession. Revelation drew Abraham toward God, and toward the people the revelation concerned. He pressed his appeal six times—fifty righteous, forty-five, forty, thirty, twenty, ten (==Genesis 18:24-32==)—each time leaning on what he knew of God’s character: “Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?” (==Genesis 18:25==). Abraham wasn’t trying to talk God out of being just. He was appealing to God’s justice on behalf of a city, standing in the gap between what he had heard and the people who hadn’t heard it (==Ezekiel 22:30==).

This is why God tells His friends what He is about to do (==James 2:23==; ==Amos 3:7==). He isn’t giving them information to hold—He’s giving them a place to stand. When God shows a person something, the revelation is an invitation to come near, not a report to file. And it’s worth remembering how the story ended: God did remember Abraham when He destroyed the cities, and Lot was brought out (==Genesis 19:29==). The intercession of one man on a dusty road reached into Sodom before the fire did. When God lets you see what He sees—a person, a family, a city—do what Abraham did with it. Come near.

## Application
Turn what God shows you into intercession the way Abraham did. When a person keeps coming to mind, when a situation grips you in prayer, when you learn something heavy about someone’s life—treat it as disclosure with a purpose. Come near: pray for them specifically, by name, before you talk to anyone else about it. Persist: Abraham asked six times, narrowing his request each round, and God answered every one—so don’t stop at the first asking. And anchor your appeals in God’s character the way Abraham did (“Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?”): pray back to God what you know is true of Him. The people you’re standing before God for may never know the conversation happened. Sodom didn’t. Pray anyway.
