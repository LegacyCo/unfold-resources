## Commentary
Put two verses from the same sermon side by side. “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father in heaven” (==Matthew 5:16==). And then, twenty-nine sentences later: “Take heed that you do not do your charitable deeds before men, to be seen by them.” If you'd been sitting on that hill taking notes, you might have raised your hand: “Rabbi—are people supposed to see a difference in us, or not?”

It’s not a contradiction. Both verses assume your righteousness will be seen. The difference is what your righteousness *produces* in the people who see it. In Matthew 5:16, they see your good works and glorify *the Father*. In Matthew 6:2, the hypocrites give “that they may have glory from men”—the glory stops at the giver. Think of it this way: a window and a mirror are made of the same glass. Righteousness aimed at the Father is a window—people watch what you do, and they see *Him* through it. Righteousness aimed at yourself is a mirror—same act, same glass, but now all anybody sees is you. So the kingdom response to self-righteousness is not to hide your light. Shine. Just make sure you’re shining in the right direction. The measuring stick isn’t whether people see your righteousness; it’s who they end up glorifying.

Two cautions. First, this is not a rule that all giving must be secret—Barnabas laid his gift at the apostles’ feet in front of everyone, and Scripture tells it with approval (==Acts 4:36-37==). The issue is the aim. Second, if you’re doing the math on your own motives right now and concluding you should stop giving until your heart is pure—don’t. Your motives won’t be completely pure in this life, and the poor can’t eat your introspection. Mixed motives are a reason to keep your eyes on the Father while you give. They are never a reason to withhold mercy.

## Word Study
- *take heed* : **prosechō** — be on the alert; watchman language, used when a danger is present that others won’t see on their own. Jesus opens with a warning label because this particular sin sneaks up on you—the person committing it is almost always the last to know.
- *hypocrites* (v2) : **hypokritēs** — the Greek word for a stage actor. We hear “hypocrite” and picture a fraud who knows it’s all an act. But what defines an actor isn’t insincerity—it’s the *audience*. A hypocrite is someone whose righteousness has to be seen in order to have value. That’s why hypocrites rarely feel fake: the money was real, the help was real. But the giving was a performance, and the applause became the reward. Nobody wakes up and decides to become one—it happens slowly, one noticed act of obedience at a time, until you no longer know how to practice righteousness without an audience.
