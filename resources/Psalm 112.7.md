## Commentary
Psalm 112 is a portrait of the righteous man, and verse 7 makes a claim most of us would love to have written over our lives: “He will not be afraid of evil tidings; his heart is steadfast, trusting in the LORD.” No fear of bad news. But look at what surrounds that promise, because the psalmist placed it deliberately: right before it, “a good man deals graciously and *lends*; he will guide his affairs with discretion” (==Psalm 112:5==). Right after it, “he has *dispersed abroad*, he has *given to the poor*; his righteousness endures forever” (==Psalm 112:9==).

The steadfast heart is sandwiched between two descriptions of generosity. That’s not an accident of poetry—it’s a diagnosis of how fear works. The person whose security is in what they’re holding will always be afraid of news that threatens the pile. But the person who has learned to open their hands has moved their security somewhere bad news can’t reach. Generosity isn’t just kindness to others; it’s a declaration about where your trust lives. Every act of giving preaches a small sermon to your own soul: *the LORD is my source, not the barn.*

And Scripture insists this open-handed life doesn’t end in lack—it ends in increase. “There is one who scatters, yet increases more… the generous soul will be made rich, and he who waters will also be watered himself” (==Proverbs 11:24-25==). “Honor the LORD with your possessions, and with the firstfruits of all your increase; so your barns will be filled with plenty” (==Proverbs 3:9-10==). Notice the kingdom order in that verse: God gets the *first*, not the leftovers. In the world’s economy, we give out of our abundance—whatever’s left after everything else is handled. In the kingdom, we give first and live off what remains. That’s what made the widow’s two coins worth more than all the offerings of the rich: they gave out of their abundance; she gave first, out of her poverty, because her trust was in the LORD and not the pile (==Luke 21:2-4==).

So if bad news rattles you more than it used to, don’t start by working on your courage. Check your hands. Fear and clenched fists tend to live in the same house—and so do steadfast hearts and open ones.
