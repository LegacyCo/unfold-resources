## Commentary
“For freedom Christ has set us free; stand fast therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery.”

Here’s one of the subtler ways believers end up back under the yoke: we let the spirit of religion speak old covenant realities over a new covenant people. We hear Isaiah cry, “Woe is me, for I am unclean” (==Isaiah 6:5==), and we take on his perspective as our own. We read “we are all unclean, and our righteousness is like filthy rags” (==Isaiah 64:6==), and because it’s between the covers of the Bible, we assume Isaiah speaks for us.

But we have to understand the full counsel of God. Isaiah lived under an inferior covenant—a shadow of the good things to come (==Hebrews 10:1==). He knew nothing yet of the redemptive blood of Emmanuel. He didn’t know that the same God he saw high and lifted up would wrap Himself in flesh, descend into the depths of our sin, and become unclean *for us*. For anyone on this side of the cross to take on the self-assessment of a man under the old covenant is like a slave who has been legally freed but keeps living in the quarters, flinching at a master who no longer owns him.

That’s what legalism actually is: old covenant language enforced as a weapon of control over people the cross has already freed. The vocabulary tells you which covenant is being preached. The language of the old is “woe is me, for I am unclean.” The language of the Spirit is, “Do you not know that you are the temple of God, and the Spirit of God dwells in you?” (==1 Corinthians 3:16==). Jesus didn’t just rinse us off—He made us new: “If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away” (==2 Corinthians 5:17==).

So stand fast. When a sermon, a system, or a voice in your own head hands you Isaiah’s confession as your identity, check the covenant it came from. You don’t honor the cross by talking like it never happened.
