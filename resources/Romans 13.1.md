## Commentary
"Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist are appointed by God."

Paul wrote these words around AD 57. The emperor was Nero.

The church in Rome already knew what an emperor's decree could do to them. About eight years earlier, Claudius had expelled the Jews from the city. The Roman historian Suetonius says the Jews were thrown out because of constant disturbances over someone he calls "Chrestus." Most historians think he meant Christ. It seems the message about Jesus had stirred up heated arguments in Rome's Jewish community, and Claudius settled it by expelling them all. Luke mentions something similar. Paul met Aquila and Priscilla in Corinth because they had "recently come from Italy... because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome" (==Acts 18:2==). Jewish believers lost their homes and their livelihoods overnight. When Claudius died in AD 54, many of them came back, and by the time this letter arrived, Priscilla and Aquila were hosting a church in their home again (==Romans 16:3–5==).

So these weren't people who had only known a friendly government. Many were Jewish believers who had been driven out of the city by an emperor. Many of the names Paul greets in chapter 16 were common among slaves and freedmen, people with very little power and no say in how they were governed. Around the same time complaints about greedy tax collectors grew so loud in Rome that Nero considered abolishing some taxes altogether. Paul's instruction to pay "taxes to whom taxes are due, customs to whom customs" (==Romans 13:7==) landed on people who felt that burden.

And they had no idea what was coming. Seven years after Paul wrote this letter, a fire destroyed much of Rome, and Nero blamed the Christians. Tacitus describes the fallout: Christians wrapped in animal skins and torn apart by dogs, crucified, and burned as torches to light Nero's gardens. Early church tradition says Paul himself was beheaded in Rome under the same emperor. The man who wrote "the authorities that exist are appointed by God" would eventually be executed by one of them.

Paul isn't naive about Rome. He's making a claim about God. Nero didn't take the throne behind God's back. "The Most High rules in the kingdom of men, gives it to whomever He will, and sets over it the lowest of men" (==Daniel 4:17==). Jesus said the same thing to the Roman governor who sentenced Him: "You could have no power at all against Me unless it had been given you from above" (==John 19:11==). Every ruler holds borrowed authority, and every ruler will answer to the One who lent it.

A few sentences before this, Paul told these same believers, "Repay no one evil for evil... do not avenge yourselves... overcome evil with good" (==Romans 12:17, 19, 21==). Romans 13 continues that thought. A people whose King had been crucified by a Roman governor could easily have seen Rome as the enemy to be resisted. But Paul calls them higher. They were to live as quiet, honest, tax-paying citizens of Rome while their deepest loyalty belonged to another kingdom (==Philippians 3:20==).

"Be subject" is *hupotassō*, a word with a military background. It means to arrange yourself under, to take your place in an order. It describes a posture more than a list of commands. Being subject to an authority isn't the same as doing whatever it says. When the Sanhedrin ordered the apostles to stop preaching, Peter answered, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (==Acts 5:29==). Then they took the beating and left rejoicing (==Acts 5:40–41==). Daniel kept praying when it was outlawed, and he went to the lions rather than lead a revolt. Christians have long discussed where the limits of this command lie, but Scripture itself is clear that subjection never means obeying an order to sin. When a Christian has to refuse, he refuses without contempt, and he accepts the consequences.

This verse has been badly misused. In the 1930s, many church leaders in Germany pointed to Romans 13 to call Christians to loyalty to Hitler. Others, in what became the Confessing Church, refused, insisting that Jesus Christ alone is Lord of His church and that no ruler could claim the obedience that belongs to Him. Paul never meant "be subject" to mean "go along with whatever the state does." Scripture never asks God's people to take part in evil because a ruler commands it, and three verses later Paul tells us exactly what a ruler's authority is for (==Romans 13:4==).

This wasn't a teaching Paul gave only to Rome. He told Titus to "remind them to be subject to rulers and authorities, to obey, to be ready for every good work" (==Titus 3:1==). Peter, probably writing from Rome itself in the early 60s as hostility toward Christians was rising (==1 Peter 5:13==), uses the same language: "submit yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord's sake, whether to the king as supreme" (==1 Peter 2:13==). The king was Nero. *For the Lord's sake.* A Christian's submission isn't fear of Caesar. It's another way of serving Christ.

## Application
Paul also wrote, "I exhort first of all that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks be made for all men, for kings and all who are in authority" (==1 Timothy 2:1–2==). He was asking believers to pray for Nero. Whoever governs you, whether you voted for them or not, is in that seat under God's authority and will answer to Him. You're free to disagree, to speak, and when it comes to it, to refuse to sin. But you're not free to hold them in contempt. Pray for the people who govern you by name this week.
