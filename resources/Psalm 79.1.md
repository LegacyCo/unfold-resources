## Commentary
Psalm 79 likely comes from the devastation of Jerusalem in 586 BC, when Babylon invaded the city, destroyed the temple, broke down the walls, and carried many of the survivors into exile. This verse is the cry of a people standing in the ruins of what they believed could never fall.

Notice the language: “Your inheritance,” “Your holy temple,” “Jerusalem.” The psalmist doesn't say, “They invaded our city.” He says, “They have come into *Your* inheritance.” This is not just a national tragedy. This is a covenant crisis. The land belonged to Yahweh. The temple was the place where His name dwelled. Jerusalem was the city bound up with His promises.

That's why the destruction cuts so deeply. The psalmist is trying to process how the holy city could become a ruin. The nations have defiled what belonged to God.

But there is also a confession hidden in this verse. If the land is “Your inheritance,” then Israel’s hope is not ultimately in the city, the temple, or their own strength. Their hope is that even in judgment, everything still belongs to Yahweh. Babylon may have burned the temple, but Babylon did not become Lord. Jerusalem may lie in ruins, but the covenant God of Israel is still the One being addressed: “O God…”

## Application
This psalm shows us where lament begins: with the confidence to say — even in weakness and confusion — “O God…” while standing in the middle of the wreckage.

There are moments when the most important thing you can do is simply turn toward God with the ruins of your situation in your hands.

Lament is not unbelief. Lament is what faith sounds like when bewilderment honestly presents itself to God — when the wounded heart chooses to turn toward God instead of away from Him.
