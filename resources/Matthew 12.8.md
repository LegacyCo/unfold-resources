## Commentary
When Jesus declares, “For the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath,” He is identifying Himself as the One to whom the Sabbath has been pointing since the beginning of creation.

To understand the significance of this statement, we need to go back to ==Genesis 2:1–3==. After six days of creating, God finished His work and rested on the seventh day. The Hebrew word translated “rested” is *shabat* — to cease or stop. God didn't rest (stop) because He was tired from creating for six days. He stopped because His work was complete. There was nothing left to add.

Now consider Adam. He was created on the sixth day, which means his first full day of life was the seventh. Before Adam accomplished anything, fulfilled a responsibility, or lifted a hand to work, he was invited into God's finished work and a creation that was at rest with its Creator. Adam didn't work six days to earn a seventh day of rest. He began his life in God's rest, and from that place he was given work to do.

This is the order God established at creation. God worked and then rested. Adam rested and then worked. Humanity was never intended to work its way into rest, but to live and work from the rest God had already provided.

When God later established the Sabbath as a covenant sign with Israel (==Exodus 31:13–17==), the weekly day of rest became a reminder of this original design. But the Sabbath also pointed beyond itself to a greater reality. Paul explains in ==Colossians 2:16–17== that the Sabbath was a shadow of things to come, but the substance was Christ.

A shadow tells us something true about the object casting it, but it is not the object itself. For centuries, the Sabbath reminded Israel that God finishes what He begins, that His people are not slaves to endless labor, and that their lives are meant to be lived in fellowship with Him. Then Jesus came and announced, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (==Matthew 11:28==).

Notice where that invitation to come to God's rest is in relation to this confrontation with the Pharisees — a mere ten verses earlier. Matthew deliberately places these stories beside each other. Jesus has just invited the weary to find rest in Him, and now He declares Himself Lord of the Sabbath.

The Pharisees were defending the regulations surrounding a day that was intended to reveal the very Person standing in front of them. They were so concerned with protecting the Sabbath that they were willing to condemn His disciples for satisfying their hunger. They understood the restrictions of the day but never understood the freedom of the day...of living from a life of union and fellowship with the God who has ceased His labors.

This is why ==Hebrews 4:9–10== is so important. The writer declares that a Sabbath rest remains for the people of God, and that those who enter God's rest cease from their works as God did from His. The invitation of the gospel is not to work harder until we have finally earned God's acceptance. It is to enter, by faith, into what Christ has already accomplished.
