## Commentary
Genesis tells us that Terah took his son Abram, his grandson Lot, and Sarai, Abram's wife, and departed from Ur of the Chaldeans with the intention of reaching Canaan. Yet rather than continuing to Canaan, they stopped in Haran and settled there.

The ancient city of Haran is located in modern-day southeastern Turkey, near the Syrian border. It was an important commercial center situated along major trade routes connecting Mesopotamia with the Mediterranean world. Interestingly, Haran was also a significant center for the worship of the Mesopotamian moon god Sin, as was Ur, the city Abram had originally came from. There is something worth considering in the fact that Abram had left his homeland, yet settled in another city with a similar religious environment. He had traveled a considerable distance geographically, but had not yet reached the place God was calling him to.

What makes this interesting is what we discover in the following chapter. God had instructed Abram to “Get out of your country, from your family and from your father's house, to a land that I will show you” (==Genesis 12:1==). We might assume that God gave this instruction after Abram arrived in Haran, but Stephen provides some important background in ==Acts 7:2–4==. He explains that God appeared to Abram while he was still living in Mesopotamia, before he dwelt in Haran, and commanded him to leave his country and relatives. Genesis 12, then, gives us the divine calling behind the journey already described in Genesis 11.

Notice the tension between what God instructed Abram to do and what actually happened. God called Abram to leave his father's household, yet Abram traveled with his father and members of his father's family. God was leading him toward Canaan, but the family settled in Haran. What began as a journey of faith became a prolonged stay somewhere short of the destination God had appointed.

The text does not explicitly tell us why they stopped in Haran or whether Abram's decision to travel with his family was the cause of the delay. Nevertheless, Abram had received a personal calling from God, but his journey was still closely connected to the household he had been called to leave. According to Stephen, it was only *after* his father's death that Abram departed from Haran and continued toward the promised land (==Acts 7:4==).

Then there's Lot. Even after Abram leaves Haran, we read that “Lot went with him” (==Genesis 12:4==). Lot was Abram's nephew, the son of his deceased brother Haran, and therefore another member of the extended family Abram had been instructed to leave.

As the story unfolds, Lot's presence becomes the occasion for several difficult circumstances. The increasing wealth of both households creates conflict between their herdsmen, eventually requiring Abram and Lot to separate (==Genesis 13:5–12==). Lot's decision to settle near Sodom exposes him to the dangers of that city, and Abram must later intervene to rescue him after he is taken captive (==Genesis 14:12–16==). Eventually, Lot's association with Sodom results in further tragedy (==Genesis 19==). Although Scripture does not directly attribute these events to Abram's disobedience, it is difficult to overlook how much of the subsequent narrative involves complications with Abram's nephew.

There is an important lesson here concerning obedience to God. Sometimes the difficulty is not that we refuse to move when God speaks, but that we attempt to obey while still holding on to things He has asked us to leave behind. We take steps in the right direction, but we bring along relationships, dependencies, expectations, or attachments that complicate the journey.

Abram's story is also encouraging because God did not abandon him in Haran. The Lord continued leading him toward what He had promised. His faith was not perfected at the beginning of the journey, and his obedience would continue to develop throughout his life.

## Application
Are there areas of your life where you have begun obeying God but stopped short of what He actually instructed you to do? Have you settled in a place He only intended you to pass through? Have you carried something into the next season that He asked you to leave behind? Abram's story asks us to consider whether our obedience is being shaped by the fullness of God's instruction or just by what makes sense to us.
