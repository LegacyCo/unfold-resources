## Commentary
“For unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given; and the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace.”

These words were spoken into one of the darkest periods of Judah’s history. The Assyrian Empire was expanding, the surrounding nations were threatening war, and King Ahaz had chosen to place his confidence in political alliances rather than in the Lord. The people were living under the weight of fear, uncertainty, and the consequences of their own rebellion. Yet in the middle of all this darkness, Isaiah announces that God is going to do something extraordinary. He is going to give them a child.

This is the remarkable contrast of the passage. God’s answer to the darkness of the nations is not another army, a more powerful political alliance, or a stronger earthly king. His answer is a Son.

Isaiah had already announced that the people walking in darkness would see a great light (==Isaiah 9:2==). Now he reveals that this light will come through a child born to the people of Israel, a descendant of David who will establish a kingdom unlike any they have ever known.

Notice the language: “Unto us a Child is born, unto us a Son is given.” The child is _born_, emphasizing His genuine humanity, and the Son is _given_, emphasizing that His coming is ultimately God's gift to His people. From the perspective of the New Testament, we recognize the fullness of this promise in the incarnation. The eternal Son of God entered into our humanity (==John 1:14==).

Isaiah then declares that “the government will be upon His shoulder.” In the ancient world, the shoulder was associated with bearing responsibility and authority. This child would carry the responsibility of governing God's people. But unlike the kings who had failed Israel, His reign would be characterized by righteousness, justice, and peace. The following verse tells us that His kingdom will have no end (==Isaiah 9:7==).

What follows is a remarkable collection of royal titles that reveal the character of this promised King.

_Wonderful Counselor._ The Hebrew expression _pele yo'etz_ describes one whose counsel is extraordinary, beyond ordinary human wisdom. The word _pele_ is frequently associated with the marvelous works of God. This King will not govern by the limited wisdom of men. He will possess the wisdom of God Himself. We see this fulfilled in Jesus, whose words repeatedly astonished those who heard Him, revealing the wisdom and purposes of the Father (==John 12:49–50==).

_Mighty God._ The Hebrew is _El Gibbor_, a title Isaiah uses again in ==Isaiah 10:21== when speaking explicitly of the Lord. This is one of the most extraordinary features of the prophecy. The promised Davidic King is described using language associated with God Himself. The New Testament reveals how this can be true: the promised Messiah is not a man empowered by God, but God who has come to dwell among men (==Colossians 2:9==).

_Everlasting Father._ This title requires some clarification. Isaiah is not suggesting that the Son and the Father are the same person. Rather, he is describing the character of the Messiah's reign. The Hebrew _avi-ad_ can be understood as “Father of Eternity” or “Everlasting Father.” In the ancient world, kings could be described as fathers to their people, emphasizing their responsibility to protect, provide for, and care for those entrusted to them (==Isaiah 22:21==). Unlike earthly kings whose reigns eventually end, this King will care for His people forever. His fatherly rule will never be interrupted by death, failure, or the passing of generations.

_Prince of Peace._ The Hebrew word for peace is _shalom_, and its meaning extends far beyond the absence of conflict. It speaks of wholeness, well-being, harmony, and things being brought into their proper order. This Messiah will establish the conditions in which creation can flourish according to God's original intention. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus has already begun this work, reconciling us to God and making peace through the blood of His cross (==Colossians 1:19–20==). His kingdom will ultimately bring the restoration of all things.

What makes this prophecy so extraordinary is that all these titles belong to one person. The child born in Bethlehem is the King whose wisdom is perfect, whose power is divine, whose care is everlasting, and whose kingdom brings peace.

And yet, perhaps the most beautiful words in this verse are the ones that introduce the entire promise: _“Unto us.”_

Twice Isaiah repeats them. This child is born _unto us_. This Son is given _unto us_. The promised King is not presented as someone who has come merely to demonstrate His greatness from a distance. He is God's gift to humanity.

The God who could have remained distant from our darkness chose instead to enter into it. He came as one of us, shared in our humanity, bore our suffering, and established His kingdom through His own self-giving love.

Isaiah's announcement is therefore more than a prediction about the birth of Jesus. It is a revelation of the kind of King God has always intended to give His people. And it invites us to consider whether we are looking to Jesus for what He can do for us, or whether we have actually entrusted the government of our lives to Him.

The One upon whose shoulders the government rests is worthy of being trusted with the whole of our lives.
