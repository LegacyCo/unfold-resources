## Commentary
“Then Abram moved his tent, and went and dwelt by the terebinth trees of Mamre, which are in Hebron, and built an altar there to the LORD.”

One of the meanings of the name Hebron is “communion.” After all of Abram’s wanderings and missteps—the famine, Egypt, the strife with Lot—he finally settles in the place of communion, and the first thing he does there is build an altar to the LORD. The order of the chapter is significant: before Abram could arrive at the place of communion, he had to pass the test of preferring someone else above himself (==Genesis 13:9==).

The selfish are not seated at the table of communion. Communion is intimate fellowship—with God and with God’s people—and it is a place of trust, love, humility, and considering the ones beside you more important than yourself (==Philippians 2:3==). Abram had learned to trust that God rewards the meek with a seat at that table. Hebron would go on to carry that meaning through Israel’s story: it was from Hebron that the spies brought back the fruit of the land (==Numbers 13:22-23==), Hebron that Caleb claimed as his inheritance (==Joshua 14:13-14==), and Hebron where David was first anointed king (==2 Samuel 2:4==). The place of communion is where inheritance and kingship take root.

## Word Study
**Hebron** (חֶבְרוֹן, *chevron*) comes from the root *chabar*, “to join, to unite, to bind together”—the same root behind *chaver*, “companion.” The root describes the curtains of the tabernacle being “coupled together” into one dwelling (==Exodus 26:6==), and the psalmist uses *chaver* to say, “I am a companion of all who fear You” (==Psalm 119:63==). Hebron is literally the place of joining—of being bound in fellowship. Summary: Abram’s journey ends this chapter at a city whose very name means joined together in companionship; communion with God is the settled destination of the surrendered life.
