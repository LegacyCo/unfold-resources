## Commentary
“I will also spread My net over him, and he shall be caught in My snare. I will bring him to Babylon, to the land of the Chaldeans; yet he shall not see it, though he shall die there.”

Ezekiel is in Babylon when he receives this word, living among the Jews who were deported in 597 BC. Jerusalem is still standing, and King Zedekiah is still on the throne. The exiles expect to go home soon. So God has Ezekiel act out what's about to happen. He packs a bag in broad daylight, digs a hole through the wall of his house at evening, and climbs out at twilight with the bag on his shoulder and his face covered (==Ezekiel 12:3-7==). The phrase “in their sight” appears seven times in those five verses. God wants the exiles to watch.

Then God explains it. The man with the bag is “the prince in Jerusalem,” Zedekiah (==Ezekiel 12:10==). He'll leave the city at twilight through a hole in the wall, and he'll cover his face “so that he cannot see the ground with his eyes” (==Ezekiel 12:12==). Verse 13 adds a detail that must have sounded impossible: God will bring him to Babylon, he will die there, and he will never see it.

About five years later, it happened exactly that way. When the Babylonians broke through Jerusalem's wall, Zedekiah and his soldiers fled at night through a gate between the two walls. The Babylonian army caught him in the plains of Jericho and took him to Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah. “Then they killed the sons of Zedekiah before his eyes, put out the eyes of Zedekiah, bound him with bronze fetters, and took him to Babylon” (==2 Kings 25:4-7==). He was a prisoner there until the day he died (==Jeremiah 52:11==). He was brought to Babylon, he died in Babylon, and he never saw it.

Jeremiah had given Zedekiah a prophecy that seemed to say the opposite: “your eyes shall see the eyes of the king of Babylon… and you shall go to Babylon” (==Jeremiah 34:3==). The Jewish historian Josephus records a tradition that Zedekiah noticed the difference between the two prophets and used it as a reason to believe neither. But both were right. Zedekiah looked Nebuchadnezzar in the eye at Riblah, and that was one of the last things he ever saw.

God says “My net” and “My snare.” Babylon's army did the chasing, but God was the one catching him. Zedekiah had sworn an oath of loyalty to Nebuchadnezzar in God's name and then broken it (==2 Chronicles 36:13==). He'd also been told plainly, more than once, that if he surrendered he would live and the city would be spared (==Jeremiah 38:17-18==). He had every warning a man could have. The chapter opens by describing people like him: they have “eyes to see but (do) not see” (==Ezekiel 12:2==). The king who refused to see what God was showing him ended his life unable to see anything.

This prophecy shows how exact God's word is. He doesn't speak in generalities that can be made to fit whatever happens. He named the time of day, the hole in the wall, the covered face, the city, and the blindness, years ahead of time. 

When two things God has said seem impossible to hold together, the problem is with what we can see from where we're standing. Joshua's testimony at the end of his life applies to every word God has spoken: “not one thing has failed of all the good things which the LORD your God spoke concerning you” (==Joshua 23:14==).
