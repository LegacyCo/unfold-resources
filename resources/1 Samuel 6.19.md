## Commentary
The return of the ark to Israel should have been an occasion of celebration, and initially, it was. The people of Beth Shemesh rejoiced when they saw the ark approaching, offered sacrifices, and worshiped the Lord. Yet what began as a celebration ended in mourning when God struck down seventy men for looking into the ark.

There is some ambiguity in the Hebrew concerning precisely what these men did. Some translations say they "looked into" the ark, while others say they "looked upon" it. Since the people had already seen the ark approaching and celebrated its arrival (==1 Samuel 6:13==), the offense was apparently not simply seeing it. Rather, the language suggests an inappropriate or unauthorized inspection of something God had declared holy. 

Beth Shemesh was a Levitical city assigned to the descendants of Aaron (==Joshua 21:13–16==). These were people who should have understood the requirements surrounding the ark. God had explicitly warned that even those Levites responsible for transporting the holy objects were not permitted to look upon them when uncovered, lest they die (==Numbers 4:20==). The people of Beth Shemesh were not ignorant of the God of Israel or His instructions concerning the things devoted to Him.

This also continues the theme we have seen throughout the ark narrative. The Philistines had discovered that possessing the ark did not mean they could control the God whose presence it represented. Now Israel was being reminded that belonging to God did not give them permission to disregard His holiness. The same God who judged the Philistines for their irreverence held His own people accountable for how they approached Him.

Under the new covenant, we have been given something extraordinary. Through the blood of Jesus, we are invited to draw near to God with confidence, entering into a relationship and intimacy that the people of the old covenant could scarcely have imagined (==Hebrews 10:19–22==). But this access does not mean that God has somehow become less holy. The God who dwells within us by His Spirit is the same God whose presence caused Israel to tremble at Sinai.

The writer of Hebrews holds these two realities together beautifully. We can approach God boldly because of Christ, and yet we are instructed to worship Him "with reverence and godly fear. For our God is a consuming fire" (==Hebrews 12:28–29==).

Our confidence in approaching God should never become an excuse for treating Him casually. The intimacy we have been given through Christ ought to deepen our reverence for Him, not diminish it. The more we come to know Him, the more we should recognize the extraordinary privilege of being welcomed into the presence of the One who is holy.

Textual note: Some older translations report that 50,070 people were struck down, while many modern translations give the number as seventy. The traditional Hebrew text contains both numbers, but there are significant textual difficulties with the larger figure, leading many scholars to regard seventy as the more likely original reading.
