## Commentary
“He who does not love does not know God, for God is love.”

“God is love” may be the most quoted sentence in this letter—and the most dangerous one to misread. Here’s why. The Bible uses at least six different words to convey love. English has one, and we’ve worn it out—we love God, love our spouse, love tacos, love a good parking spot. So when we come to Scripture and read that God *is* love, we inevitably pour our own experiences into that word. We filter God through what love has been to us. And if love, as we experienced it, was distorted, then the danger isn’t just a wrong idea about love. It’s a wrong idea about *God*.

If the person who taught you love was manipulative and controlling, you'll read “God is love” and approach Him the way you approached the manipulator: *if I don’t act the way He wants, He’s going to get angry.* If the person you loved most betrayed you and left, you’ll read “God is love” and conclude, *I don’t know that I can trust this God.* We rarely think these things consciously—but filter His love through your experience and you end up with a god made in your own image, wearing the face of everyone who ever hurt you in the name of love.

So kingdom people take a firm stance: they reject any idea about love that is not seen in the life of Jesus. He is “the image of the invisible God” (==Colossians 1:15==); in Him all the fullness of who God is dwells and is put on display (==Colossians 1:19==; ==John 14:9==). Do you want to know what “God is love” actually means? Don’t consult your history. Watch Jesus—touching lepers, feeding the ones who would abandon Him, washing the feet of His betrayer, forgiving His executioners. That is the love that God *is*. Let Him define the word, and then let the definition heal what the counterfeit versions broke.
