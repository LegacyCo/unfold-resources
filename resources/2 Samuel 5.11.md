## Commentary
"Then Hiram king of Tyre sent messengers to David, and cedar trees, and carpenters and masons. And they built David a house."

Look at everything that has already happened to David by this point. He's been anointed by Samuel (==1 Samuel 16:13==). Anointed by the men of Judah (==2 Samuel 2:4==). Anointed by all the tribes of Israel (==2 Samuel 5:3==). He's conquered Jerusalem and taken the stronghold. Three anointings and a capital city—and in none of those stories does David say his kingdom is established.

Then Hiram builds him a house. And the very next verse: "So David knew that the LORD had established him as king over Israel" (==2 Samuel 5:12==). It wasn't the anointing that told him. It wasn't the throne. It was the *house*. Something about the house being built confirmed that the throne was secure.

Now watch how the pattern lands on Jesus. He has been anointed King of kings. The Father has exalted Him to the right hand of the Majesty on high (==Hebrews 1:3==). All authority in heaven and on earth is His (==Matthew 28:18==). And He is waiting—not for more authority, but for someone to build His house. "On this rock I will build My church" (==Matthew 16:18==)—a house of living stones, a family fitted together for a dwelling place of God (==Ephesians 2:19-22==).

God has never been looking for a monument. He has been looking for a *dwelling*—a place to fill with Himself. It's the ache running through the whole story. In the garden He walked with man in the cool of the day (==Genesis 3:8==). At Sinai He said, "Let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may *dwell among them*" (==Exodus 25:8==). When Solomon finished the temple, God didn't send approval; He moved in—the glory filled the house so thick the priests couldn't stand to minister (==2 Chronicles 5:13-14==). And the end of the story is the same longing finally satisfied: "Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them" (==Revelation 21:3==). From Eden to the New Jerusalem, God is a Father wanting to come home to His family. A throne without a house is a King with nowhere to live among His people. That's why the house confirms the throne—because the house is what the throne was *for*.

God will not fill just any house. When He gave Moses the plans for the first one, He said it plainly: "See to it that you make them according to the pattern shown you on the mountain" (==Exodus 25:40==). Not a suggestion. A requirement, repeated (==Exodus 25:9==, ==Hebrews 8:5==). God fills what He designs. He does not endorse our blueprints with His presence; He inhabits *His* blueprint when we're humble enough to build to it. And His pattern is so contrary to our natural way of building that it has to be revealed—it can't be guessed at, borrowed from the last generation, or reverse-engineered from whatever seems to be working somewhere else. He builds with people, not programs. He builds family, not organizations. He builds slowly, out of broken living stones fitted together in covenant, not quickly out of whatever's efficient (==1 Peter 2:5==). We can build something impressive and call it His house. But "unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it" (==Psalm 127:1==).

So how important is it that we build God's house? Exactly as important as it is that His throne be established in our city, our nation, the earth. If we want His righteous rule and reign to sweep our streets, we don't need better strategies for influence. We need to build Him a house—His way, according to the pattern. When *that* house stands, the kingdom is established, because it's hard to be yourself when you're a guest in someone else's house.

And notice one more thing: David didn't build his own house. Hiram did—a Gentile king who loved David and sent cedar, carpenters, and masons. God can bring materials from unexpected places to build His house. Our job isn't to control the supply. It's to be the kind of people He can build with.
