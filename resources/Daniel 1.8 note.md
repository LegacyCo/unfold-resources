## Commentary
Daniel had no problem with his name being changed to one that honored the pagan gods of Babylon (==Daniel 1:7==). He had no problem being enrolled in three years of schooling in the language and literature of the very empire that had ransacked Jerusalem (==Daniel 1:5==). A young exile could absorb Babylon’s education and answer to Babylon’s names. But when it came to eating from the king’s table, that’s where he drew the line: “Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the portion of the king’s delicacies, nor with the wine which he drank.”

Why the table? Because Daniel understood something significant: there is something spiritually binding about the table you eat from. In the ancient world, to eat from a king’s table was to enter his fellowship, live off his provision, and come under his patronage—it announced whose you were. Daniel could carry a Babylonian name without belonging to Babylon’s gods. But he knew he couldn’t eat from the king's table without communion.

This isn’t a statement about dietary restrictions so much as a statement about the spiritual significance of tables. Scripture takes eating seriously as a covenental act of joining: “Are not those who eat of the sacrifices partakers of the altar?” (==1 Corinthians 10:18==). Which is exactly why Paul warns that you cannot partake of the Lord’s table and the table of demons (==1 Corinthians 10:21==)—not because God is territorial about menus, but because every table joins you to its host.

Daniel’s conviction is a question aimed at us: we guard our reputations, our schedules, and our associations—but do we guard our tables? What we routinely feast on—what feeds our minds, our appetites, our imaginations—is never neutral. Every table has a host. Daniel purposed in his heart to eat at only one.
