## Commentary
Every other tribe in this list is described by its soldiers—how many came, what weapons they carried, how well they could fight. Issachar gets described by what its leaders *understood*. 

When the Chronicler describes the sons of Issachar as men who “had understanding of the times, to know what Israel ought to do,” he is identifying a particular kind of wisdom that was necessary during a significant transition in Israel's history. Saul had died, David had already reigned over Judah for approximately seven and a half years, and the tribes of Israel were now gathering at Hebron to establish David's reign over the entire nation (==1 Chronicles 12:23–40==; ==2 Samuel 5:1–5==).

What makes the men of Issachar remarkable is that they understood the significance of what was happening and knew how Israel needed to respond. They weren't simply aware of the political circumstances surrounding them. They recognized that a new season had arrived in God's dealings with His people, and they understood what faithfulness required of them in that moment.

God had already revealed His intention to establish David as king (==1 Samuel 16:12–13==). The men of Issachar recognized that the period of division following Saul's death needed to come to an end. God had already chosen David to shepherd His people, and these men understood that the time had come for Israel to unite under his leadership. Their discernment wasn't simply an awareness of changing political circumstances, but an understanding of what obedience to God's revealed purposes required of them.

Biblical discernment isn't simply the ability to recognize what's happening around us. It's the ability to interpret our circumstances in light of what God has revealed and understand what obedience demands.

There are people who are remarkably informed about the world around them. They understand political developments, cultural movements, economic conditions, and the challenges facing the Church. Yet being informed doesn't necessarily mean they're discerning. It's possible to have an accurate understanding of the circumstances surrounding us and still arrive at the wrong conclusion about what we ought to do.

The men of Issachar possessed both understanding and direction. They knew what was happening, but more importantly, they knew how Israel needed to respond.

Notice also that Scripture mentions there were two hundred chiefs among them, “and all their brethren were at their command.” Their discernment wasn't just for their own benefit. They were leaders whose understanding provided direction for others. At a time when Israel could have been divided by conflicting loyalties and competing ambitions, these men helped bring their tribe into alignment with God's purposes.

Those entrusted with leading God's people need more than biblical knowledge, administrative ability, or an understanding of contemporary culture. They need the wisdom to recognize what God is requiring of His people in a particular moment and the courage to lead them accordingly.

Paul addresses something similar when he instructs the believers in Ephesus to walk carefully, “not as fools but as wise, redeeming the time, because the days are evil,” and then adds, “Therefore do not be unwise, but understand what the will of the Lord is” (==Ephesians 5:15–17==).

Wisdom isn't just knowing what is true. It's knowing how to faithfully respond to the truth within the circumstances in which God has placed us.

This kind of discernment requires a life grounded in Scripture, shaped by prayer, and submitted to the Holy Spirit. Otherwise, we can become so preoccupied with the circumstances around us that we allow those circumstances to dictate our response rather than the revealed will of God.

## Word Study
**Understanding** is the Hebrew *binah* (בִּינָה)—discernment, insight, the ability to tell things apart. It comes from the verb *bin*, which means to separate mentally, to perceive the difference. Solomon asked for this when he prayed for a heart to “discern between good and evil” (==1 Kings 3:9==). Proverbs says, “in all your getting, get understanding” (==Proverbs 4:7==). Daniel “understood by the books” that the seventy years of exile were almost over, and he responded by setting his face toward God in prayer (==Daniel 9:2-3==). Summary: *binah* is more than knowing facts. It's the ability to know what the moment calls for.

## Application
Paul gives us the New Testament version of Issachar's wisdom: “knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of sleep” (==Romans 13:11==), and “do not be unwise, but understand what the will of the Lord is” (==Ephesians 5:17==). Understanding the times starts with Scripture, like it did for Daniel, more than it starts with the news. So ask what season you're in. Is it a season to build, to wait, to repent, to speak, to stay? Then ask what obedience to Jesus looks like in it, and do that. If discernment never gets around to obedience, it isn't the kind this verse is commending.
