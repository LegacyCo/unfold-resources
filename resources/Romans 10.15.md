## Commentary
Paul brings us to one of the great responsibilities of the Church: the proclamation of the gospel. In the preceding verses, he has explained that everyone who calls upon the name of the Lord will be saved (==Romans 10:13==). But he immediately raises a series of questions that reveal the practical implications of that promise. How can people call upon someone they have not believed in? How can they believe in someone they have never heard about? How can they hear unless someone tells them? And how can anyone preach unless they are sent?

Paul is describing the necessity of gospel proclamation. God has made salvation available to all through Jesus Christ, but the good news of what He has accomplished must be communicated. People cannot respond in faith to a message they have never heard. This is why the Church has been entrusted with the responsibility of making Christ known.

Paul reinforces this point by quoting ==Isaiah 52:7==: “How beautiful are the feet of those who preach the gospel of peace, who bring glad tidings of good things!” In its original context, Isaiah was announcing the good news of God's deliverance and the restoration of His people. The messenger came with the announcement that God was reigning and that salvation was coming to Zion. Paul recognizes that this prophetic picture finds its ultimate fulfillment in the gospel of Jesus Christ. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus has accomplished a deliverance far greater than Israel's return from exile. He has reconciled humanity to God, defeated the powers of sin and death, and inaugurated His kingdom.

There is something significant about Isaiah's description of the messenger's feet as beautiful. In the ancient world, messengers often traveled considerable distances on foot, across difficult terrain, to deliver important news. Their feet would have been dusty, worn, and dirty. Yet Isaiah calls them beautiful, not because of their appearance, but because of the message they carried. The arrival of the messenger meant that good news had come.

This is how God regards those who carry the gospel. There is beauty in the willingness of a person to go, to cross boundaries, to endure inconvenience and hardship, and to make Christ known to people who have not yet heard.

We should also recognize that Paul's emphasis on being "sent" is not limited to a small group of professional ministers or missionaries. While God certainly calls and commissions individuals to specific works of ministry, the responsibility of participating in His mission belongs to the entire Church. Jesus told His disciples, “As the Father has sent Me, I also send you” (==John 20:21==). And before His ascension, He commissioned them to make disciples of all nations (==Matthew 28:18–20==). The Church was never intended to become a community concerned only with its own spiritual growth and well-being. We have been gathered to Christ, but we have also been sent into the world as witnesses of what He has accomplished.

This means that being a people on mission cannot be treated as an optional expression of Christianity reserved for those who feel particularly called to evangelism. The specific ways we participate will differ. Some will cross oceans, learn languages, and devote their lives to proclaiming Christ among people who have never heard His name. Others will faithfully witness to their neighbors, coworkers, friends, and families. Still others will help send and sustain those who go through prayer, generosity, and practical support. But all of us have been invited into the work of making Christ known.

And our motivation for this mission must remain rooted in the love of God. We are not attempting to fulfill a religious obligation so that God will be pleased with us. We have encountered the extraordinary love of God revealed in Jesus Christ, and we desire for others to know Him as we have come to know Him. The gospel is good news that has transformed our lives, and it is worth sharing with those who have not experienced that transformation for themselves.

There are still people in the world who have little or no meaningful opportunity to hear the gospel. And there are people living within a few miles of our homes who have never encountered a genuine witness of the love and goodness of Jesus. Paul's questions should challenge us to consider our responsibility toward both.

If they are to believe, they must hear. And if they are to hear, someone must be willing to go.
