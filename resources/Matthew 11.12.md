## Commentary
Matthew 11:12 is one of the more difficult statements of Jesus to interpret because the Greek language allows for more than one understanding. Jesus says, “And from the days of John the Baptist until now the kingdom of heaven suffers violence, and the violent take it by force.”

The Greek word translated “suffers violence” is *biazetai*, which can be understood as the kingdom being subjected to violence or as the kingdom advancing forcefully. The related word *biastēs*, translated “violent,” describes someone who uses force. The question is whether Jesus is describing people violently opposing the kingdom or people forcefully pressing into it.

The context is important. Jesus has just explained that John the Baptist was the promised messenger sent to prepare the way for the coming King. John marked a turning point in redemptive history. The kingdom that Israel had anticipated for generations was now breaking into the world through Jesus. But its arrival was not being met with universal celebration. John was already in prison, the religious leaders were resisting Jesus, and the kingdom was encountering increasing hostility.

This makes the interpretation of violence against the kingdom particularly compelling. From the beginning of John's ministry, the announcement of God's kingdom provoked opposition. John would eventually be executed, and Jesus Himself would be rejected and crucified. The arrival of the kingdom was confronting the powers and structures of the present age, and those who felt threatened by it were responding with force.

Yet there is another interpretation worth considering. Some understand Jesus to be describing the urgency with which people were responding to the kingdom's arrival. This reading finds support in ==Luke 16:16==, where Jesus says that since John, the kingdom of God has been preached and people are pressing into it. On this reading, Jesus is describing the determination of those who recognize the significance of the kingdom and refuse to remain indifferent to its arrival. The Greek allows for this possibility, although the negative language of violence and forceful seizure makes the interpretation less straightforward. 

There is an important application here. We should not assume that opposition to the work of God means His kingdom is failing to advance. John's imprisonment did not invalidate his calling, and the rejection and eventual crucifixion of Jesus did not mean that God's purposes had been defeated. In fact, it was through the very death of Jesus that the new covenant would be established.

The kingdom of God has come into a world that does not always welcome its King. And yet, despite the violence and resistance directed against it, God's purposes continue to unfold.

## Application
There is also a personal application worth considering from the second interpretation. If Jesus is describing people forcefully pressing into the kingdom, then there is something to be said about the posture with which we pursue the things of God. We are not called to approach the kingdom passively. There ought to be a hunger in us, a holy determination to lay hold of everything Christ has made available.

Paul describes this posture in ==Philippians 3:12–14==. He freely admits that he has not yet attained or been perfected, but says that he presses on to take hold of that for which Christ Jesus took hold of him. The word translated "press on" is the Greek diōkō, a word expressing deliberate, strenuous pursuit. Paul is not trying to earn a place in Christ; he is pursuing the fullness of the life for which Christ has already made him His own. There is a significant difference between striving to become accepted by God and pressing forward because we already belong to Him.

Paul uses similar language when he charges Timothy to "fight the good fight of faith" and "lay hold on eternal life" (==1 Timothy 6:12==). The Greek verb translated "lay hold" is *epilambanomai*, meaning to grasp forcefully, seize, or take hold of something. It can describe taking someone by the hand, but the force of Paul's exhortation is not simply to acknowledge eternal life as a doctrine. Timothy is to embrace the life to which he has been called with conviction and perseverance. Paul repeats the exhortation in ==1 Timothy 6:19==. Likewise, ==Hebrews 4:16== invites us to come boldly to the throne of grace, and ==Hosea 6:3== calls God's people to press on to know the Lord.

This is not an attempt to force God's hand. It is a response of faith to what He has already promised. Jesus taught us to ask, seek, and knock (==Matthew 7:7–8==), to seek first the kingdom (==Matthew 6:33==), and to pray, "Your kingdom come. Your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" (==Matthew 6:10==). The early church took that invitation seriously. When opposition arose, they gathered to pray for boldness, and God answered by filling them afresh with the Holy Spirit (==Acts 4:29–31==).

We should believe there is still more of God's kingdom to be made visible in our lives, our churches, and our communities. Prayer, faith, obedience, hunger, and the pursuit of the formation of Jesus in our individual lives all have a direct impact on the kingdom of God advancing around us. God has invited His people to participate in His purposes. Paul reminds us that what no eye has seen and no ear has heard, God has prepared for those who love Him—and, in the very next verse, says that God has revealed these things through His Spirit (==1 Corinthians 2:9–10==). Our confidence is not in our intensity but in the God who makes Himself known through His people.

We pursue because He has first laid hold of us. And because the King has come, we have every reason to keep asking, seeking, pressing on, and expecting His kingdom to be revealed more fully among us.
