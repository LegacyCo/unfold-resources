## Commentary
“And a traveler came to the rich man, who refused to take from his own flock and from his own herd to prepare one for the wayfaring man who had come to him; but he took the poor man's lamb and prepared it for the man who had come to him.”

The rich man had flocks and herds of his own, and he *took* the poor man's one lamb. This is the same verb Scripture used for David's sin: he saw Bathsheba, inquired about her, and “*took* her” (==2 Samuel 11:4==). Nathan chose his story with precision—David had become a taker. And that is exactly what God had warned Israel a human king would be. When they demanded a king like the other nations, God said it six times in seven verses: he will *take* your sons, *take* your daughters, *take* your fields, *take* your vineyards, *take* your servants, *take* your flocks (==1 Samuel 8:11-17==). Kings of this world take.

Underneath David's story is a principle that governs every heart: we become like the kings we serve. Whatever sits on the throne of a life shapes that life into its own image—we are conformed to what we behold and obey (==2 Corinthians 3:18==; ==Romans 6:16==). And we were designed for one King. We were made to serve King Jesus, and King Jesus never takes—He only gives. He gives rest to the weary (==Matthew 11:28==), gives His flesh as bread for the life of the world (==John 6:51==), gives the overcomer a seat on His own throne (==Revelation 3:21==); the whole gospel hangs on this verb—God so loved the world that He *gave* (==John 3:16==). A man ruled by that King becomes a giving man. But when we allow ourselves to be ruled by imposter kings—fear, ambition, lust, the need to be right—we come under inferior kings, and inferior kings take. 

That's what happened to David. For one season, with an imposter occupying the throne of his heart, the man after God's own heart became precisely what 1 Samuel 8 described. And the parable's power is that David, hearing of a taker, burned with anger before he realized he was hearing about himself (==2 Samuel 12:5-7==). “You are the man” lands on anyone whose king is anything other than the true King who gives—and the way back is not trying harder to be generous. It is returning the throne to the King whose likeness we were designed to bear.
