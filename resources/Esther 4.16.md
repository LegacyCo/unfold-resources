## Commentary
“Go, gather all the Jews who are present in Shushan, and fast for me; neither eat nor drink for three days, night or day. My maids and I will fast likewise. And so I will go to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish!”

Esther stood before an impossible situation: an irrevocable decree of death over her people, and an approach to the king that could cost her life. Her first act was not strategy. It was a fast. This is one of the special occasions that has always called God's people to fasting—the moment of crisis, when a people knows it cannot survive on its own resources and says so with its body. Ezra proclaimed a fast before leading the exiles home through bandit country, “to seek from Him the right way for us” rather than requesting a royal escort (==Ezra 8:21-23==). Nehemiah fasted when he heard the walls of Jerusalem were broken down (==Nehemiah 1:4==). Jehoshaphat proclaimed a fast throughout all Judah when three armies came against him (==2 Chronicles 20:3==).

In each case the fast was the confession that words alone could not make: *we have no power against this, nor do we know what to do, but our eyes are upon You* (==2 Chronicles 20:12==). Anyone can say “God, we depend on You” over a full plate. The crisis fast says it with the body—every skipped meal a declaration that deliverance will not come from our own resources. And the pattern of these stories is that God answers that embodied humility: Esther found favor, Ezra's company was protected, the walls were rebuilt, and Judah watched an enemy defeat itself. When the situation is beyond you, the fast is how a believer's whole self—body included—casts itself on God.
