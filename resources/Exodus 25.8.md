## Commentary
“And let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.”

This is the first time in Scripture God ever asked for a house—and the reason He gives sets the trajectory for the rest of your Bible. Not “that they may worship Me properly,” not “that religion may have a headquarters.” *That I may dwell among them.* Before God ever showed Moses the pattern, He gave Moses the reason: God wants to dwell with His people. Everything else about the tabernacle—the furnishings, the priesthood, the sacrifices—serves that one desire.

And this desire didn’t begin at Sinai. It began in a garden. Eden was the original dwelling place of God with man—Adam and Eve knew “the sound of the LORD God walking in the garden in the cool of the day” (==Genesis 3:8==), and the reason they recognized the sound is that it was familiar. Walking with them is what He *did*. Sin drove humanity out of that fellowship, but it did not remove the desire from God’s heart. So when God speaks at Sinai, He isn’t introducing something new—He’s resuming something that was interrupted. All of redemptive history is God restoring His dwelling with His people.

This is the God Solomon would later confess no building could hold: “heaven and the heaven of heavens cannot contain You” (==1 Kings 8:27==). And He requests a tent—acacia wood, goat hair, ram skins—in a desert. He doesn’t need shelter; He wants nearness. And notice *where* the tent goes: not on a distant mountain that the people visit, but in the center of the camp, with the twelve tribes arranged on every side around it (==Numbers 2:2==). The nation was ordered around the presence of God in their midst. “I will dwell among the children of Israel and will be their God. And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them up out of the land of Egypt, *that I may dwell among them*” (==Exodus 29:45-46==). The exodus was not only deliverance from Egypt. Its purpose was God dwelling among His people.

Trace the thread from this verse through the rest of Scripture. The tabernacle in the wilderness became the temple in Jerusalem, and the glory filled it (==1 Kings 8:10-11==). The temple became the Word made flesh, who “dwelt”—literally *tabernacled*—among us (==John 1:14==). The Word who tabernacled among us became a people He dwells *in* by the Spirit: “you are the temple of God” (==1 Corinthians 3:16==). And the people He dwells in will become a city where the desire of Exodus 25:8 is satisfied forever: “Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them, and they shall be His people” (==Revelation 21:3==). From Eden to Sinai to the New Jerusalem, one desire is driving the whole story—*let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.*

This is why God’s servants aren’t the point—not Moses, not the apostles, not any leader or ministry or movement. The point is God having the desire of His heart. Everything we build in the Body of Christ must serve a purpose infinitely greater than ourselves, or we’ve misread it. You weren’t saved just to escape judgment; you were saved because God has wanted to dwell with His people since the garden—and in Christ, you became part of the house He’s been building all along (==Ephesians 2:22==).

## Word Study
- *dwell* : **shakan** — to settle down, abide, take up residence; the word for a nomad pitching his tent to stay. From this root comes *mishkan*, the Hebrew word for the tabernacle itself—literally “the dwelling place”—and the later term *Shekinah*, the settled, resident glory of God.

Other places the word carries the story:

- ==Exodus 40:35== — “the cloud *settled* (shakan) upon it, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.” The request of 25:8, answered.
- ==John 1:14== — “the Word became flesh and *dwelt* (eskēnōsen—pitched His tent) among us, and we beheld His glory.” John deliberately reaches for the Greek echo of shakan: in Jesus, the mishkan became a Man.

Summary: shakan is the language of settled dwelling, not visitation. God did not ask Israel for a place to appear occasionally; He asked for a place to remain. That desire has never changed.
