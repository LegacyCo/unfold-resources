## Commentary
The golden plate fastened to Aaron’s forehead declared, “Holiness unto the Lord.” It wasn't hidden in his pocket or left in his tent. It rested on his forehead—over the place of thought, intention, judgment, and imagination. Before Aaron did anything for God, he bore the reminder that he belonged entirely to God.

Art Katz writes in _Apostolic Foundations_:

“With every step and every gesture, that plate would have registered its presence against his skin. We need that constant reminder, because our head is always wanting opportunity, if we would grant it, to have its own excursions, its own delights, its own activities and its own thoughts. The mind continually needs to be brought back to the knowledge of a holy God. There would be much less brash and soulish ministry, and much less of a rush to come to the place of speaking, if that golden weight were resting squarely between our eyes.”

The priest could never forget whose presence he was entering or whose work he was performing. With every movement, the weight of that plate reminded him that ministry was not an opportunity for self-expression, ambition, or the display of human ability. His thoughts, words, and actions were to be governed by the holiness of the One before whom he stood.

We are often eager to speak before we have allowed the holiness of God to weigh upon our minds. We rush toward activity because we see an opportunity, feel a need, or believe we have something useful to contribute. But the fact that something can be said does not mean that God has called us to say it. The fact that something can be done does not mean that it has been born from His presence.

“Holiness unto the Lord” must rest over the whole life, but especially over the mind. Our thoughts cannot be permitted to wander wherever they desire and then be brought into ministry as though everything originating within us is suitable for God. The mind must continually be brought back under the awareness of His holiness. 

## Application
Peter carries this priestly image into the life of every believer when he writes, “Gird up the loins of your mind, be sober,” and then commands us, “As He who called you is holy, you also be holy in all your conduct” (1 Peter 1:13–16). Holiness begins by gathering in the loose and wandering thoughts of the mind and bringing them under the lordship of Christ. Before we speak, act, lead, or minister, we should allow the weight of God’s holiness to rest upon us. We must ask whether what we are about to do has come from His presence or merely from our own urgency, enthusiasm, and desire to be heard.
