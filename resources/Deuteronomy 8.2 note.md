## Commentary
“And you shall remember that the LORD your God led you all the way these forty years in the wilderness, to humble you and test you, to know what was in your heart.”

Read that carefully: God led them, and God tested them. But the testing wasn’t so *God* could discover who Israel was—He already knew. It was so *Israel* could discover who Israel was. And who they really were was a stiff-necked, self-centered, undeveloped people who had known nothing but slavery. They were 100% saved from Egypt the day they crossed the Red Sea—Pharaoh would never be their problem again. But saved is not the same as mature. Before they could handle houses they didn’t build and vineyards they didn’t plant (==Deuteronomy 6:10-11==), they had to be transformed from slaves into sons. The wilderness was where that transformation was intended to happen.

This is why salvation does not equal character. Have you ever met someone who is truly saved but doesn’t act like it? They’ve been delivered from their old slavery, but they haven’t let God take them through the wilderness that matures them. Salvation is the door—it gives you access to the process of transformation, where you leave who you were and become who God says you are. Think of it this way: when I was born into my parents’ family, I didn’t receive a little bit of sonship. I was a son—completely, from day one. That was my identity. But I still had to learn the culture of my father’s house: what he loved, what wasn’t permitted, how a son of that house lives. Learning those things wasn’t me *growing in sonship*—it was me growing in maturity. And honestly, a lot of those lessons felt like suffering at the time. They were actually my father loving me enough not to give me what I wanted, because what he was building in me was better.

The wilderness shows you how much of Egypt is still in you. We are experts at convincing ourselves we’re something we’re not—and we can keep the act up as long as life stays predictable. But take away the comforts, the distractions, the things we lean on to prop ourselves up, and who we really are comes to the surface: the grumbling, the fear, the ways of reacting that look more like Pharaoh than Jesus. Israel wanted to kill Moses and go back to Egypt. They complained about the manna. They refused to fight for the promise. The wilderness didn’t create any of that—it *exposed* it, so God could deal with it. He doesn’t reveal what’s in us to condemn us. He reveals it so it can be crucified and replaced with the character of Christ (==Galatians 2:20==).

These are God’s ways—and as Dr. Robert Gladstone said, “If we don’t understand His ways, we’re going to be offended when He acts like Himself.” Leading His people into the wilderness to show them who they really are—so who they really are can be buried there—is simply God acting like Himself. He’s done it with me. He’s walked me through the valley of the shadow of death, sat me down at a table in the presence of my enemies (==Psalm 23:4-5==), and we’ve had some long conversations about how much of me is still in me. It never once decreased His goodness. It *was* His goodness.
