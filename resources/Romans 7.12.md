## Commentary
“Therefore the law is holy, and the commandment holy and just and good.”

Paul has just spent five verses describing how the commandment exposed his sin, how sin seized the commandment as a base of operations, and how the arrival of the law ended in his death (==Romans 7:7-11==)—and his conclusion is not that the law is the villain of the story. The law is *holy*: it came from God and carries the character of the God who gave it (==Psalm 19:7-8==). It is *just*: everything it demands is right, and everything it condemns deserves condemnation. And it is *good*: it was given for our benefit, not our harm. The problem was never the law. The problem is what the law reveals in us, and what sin does with it (==Romans 7:13==).

This is the reason Jesus didn't abolish the law. “Do not think that I came to destroy the Law or the Prophets. I did not come to destroy but to fulfill” (==Matthew 5:17==). Jesus' announcement when He came preaching the gospel of a new covenant was not, “Good news—all that difficult stuff in the Old Testament is now irrelevant.” The law was given by God for a purpose, and that purpose is not finished: its work of exposing unrighteousness is what reveals every person's need for a Savior—the greatest gift the law can give anyone—and Jesus will not take that gift away. The law remains the tutor that brings us to Christ, that we might be justified by faith (==Galatians 3:24==). A generation that discards the law loses the very thing that shows it why the gospel is good news; a mirror that shows a man his true face is not cruel for being accurate (==James 1:23-25==). So the law stands—holy, just, and good—still doing the job God gave it: not saving anyone, but faithfully driving everyone to the One who can (==Romans 10:4==).
