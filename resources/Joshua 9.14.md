## Commentary
The Gibeonites, terrified by what God had done at Jericho, resorted to deception. They dressed in worn-out clothes, packed dry and moldy provisions, and presented themselves at Israel’s camp as travelers from a distant country asking for a covenant. Verse 14 records Israel’s failure in a single line: the men examined the evidence—they “took some of their provisions”—“but they did not ask counsel of the LORD.” On the strength of what they could see and taste, they swore an oath with the very people God had commanded them to drive out.

Three days later the deception was exposed. And here the story becomes stunning: the oath stood. Israel had sworn by the name of the LORD, and though the covenant had been obtained by fraud, they kept their word.

Centuries later the weight of that oath was still felt. During David’s reign a famine persisted for three years, and when David inquired of the Lord, the answer reached back generations: Saul had violated the ancient oath by putting Gibeonites to death (==2 Samuel 21:1==). God was still honoring a sworn word given to liars—long after everyone who had spoken it was gone.

The verse carries a double warning. First, decisions made on plausible evidence but without seeking God can bind us far longer than we imagine. Second, heaven’s memory of our words is longer than ours. Once a vow leaves the mouth, God hears it and expects it to be performed (==Numbers 30:2==). The righteous man of Psalm 15 “swears to his own hurt and does not change” (==Psalm 15:4==). In God’s economy, a word is not dissolved by time, by inconvenience, or even by the unworthiness of the one to whom it was given.
