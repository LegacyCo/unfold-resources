## Commentary
An oath is scaffolding. When a man says, “I swear I will do it,” he is bracing his word with something outside himself because the word cannot stand on its own. The deeper problem is not the vocabulary but the divided heart beneath it: one part genuinely promises, while another part quietly reserves the right to back out. The oath straps the two halves together just long enough to be believed.

Jesus commands the scaffolding removed—“let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No’”—not because He demands more willpower from divided people, but because He intends to heal the division itself. Behind the command stands the New Covenant promise: “I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you” (==Ezekiel 36:26==). A whole heart speaks a whole word. Simple, reliable speech is not primarily a discipline; it is the overflow of an undivided inner life, formed after the pattern of the One who is Himself God’s Yes (==2 Corinthians 1:20==).

History shows what happens when a community takes this verse at face value. The early Quakers of the 1600s refused all oaths—many accepting prison rather than swearing in court—because they would not maintain two levels of honesty, one for sworn statements and one for everyday talk (==James 5:12==). Every word had to be as true as testimony under oath. Their honesty became legendary: Quaker shopkeepers pioneered the fixed price while other merchants haggled, and Quaker families went on to build some of England’s most trusted institutions. The trust was a byproduct. The aim was simply to speak like Jesus.

This remains God’s intention for His people: a community where words are safe. Where “I’ll pray for you” means prayer actually happens, where a confidence stays a confidence, and where “I was wrong” gets said. When our yes means yes, the world eventually recognizes whose people we are.
