## Commentary
In 1975, an engineer named Steve Sasson built something he knew would revolutionize the world: the first digital camera. He showed his supervisors how he could capture light digitally and display it on a screen. And they buried it. Told him to move on. The problem? Sasson worked for Kodak—and 90% of Kodak’s revenue came from film, darkroom chemicals, and photographic paper, everything his invention threatened to make obsolete. The executives couldn’t stomach being emptied of what had made them successful. By the time Kodak finally pivoted, digital photography had exploded without them. They filed for bankruptcy in 2012—not because they lacked the future, but because they couldn’t release the past.

Israel knew that trap. When they were just a family of seventy, God used Egypt to save them—Pharaoh’s storehouses preserved them through famine (==Genesis 45:7==). But Israel stayed in the blessing too long, and Exodus 1:8 records the turn: “Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.” What had saved them became the very thing that enslaved them. Egypt was provision for a famine, not a foundation to build a life on. That’s what happens when we hold on to what God once *did* instead of holding on to the God who is always moving forward—“of the *increase* of His government there will be no end” (==Isaiah 9:7==).

The same thing happens whenever God wants to move us into a new season: we fear the loss of what we’ve known more than we trust what He wants to do next. Comfort, routine, and predictability are seductive—and when we prioritize them over obedience, we start building homes where God only meant for us to pitch tents (==Hebrews 11:9==).

So hold your Egypts loosely. Honor what God did there—and refuse to build a monument to it. There are times when God will empty us of good things we’ve experienced because they’re keeping us from the ultimate things we were designed for. The blessing had a season. The God who gave it doesn’t stop moving. Go with Him.
