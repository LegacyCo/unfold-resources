## Commentary
“You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons; you cannot partake of the Lord’s table and of the table of demons.”

Eating from an altar joins you to the god of that altar. That’s the premise underneath Paul’s whole argument: “Are not those who eat of the sacrifices partakers of the altar?” (==1 Corinthians 10:18==). The altar is a place of covenant, covenant joins things together, and the meal eaten after the sacrifice celebrates union with the god just worshiped—and with the people who serve him. Believers in Corinth were sitting down to idol-feast meals with pagan friends and thinking nothing of it, and Paul rebukes them for being foolish: that meal isn’t neutral. It joins.

Notice what Paul doesn’t say here. This was the perfect moment to write, “Relax—this is all just symbolic. The idol meat means nothing, and for that matter, so do the bread and the cup of the Lord's table. Just don’t send the wrong message.” This was his chance to soften Jesus’ scandalous words—“unless you eat the flesh of the Son of Man and drink His blood, you have no life in you” (==John 6:53==). Paul does the opposite. He doubles down: both tables are real participation—*koinonia*—one with demons, one with the blood and body of Christ (==1 Corinthians 10:16==). Matthew Henry says, “The Lord’s supper is a feast on the sacrificed body and blood of our Lord. And to eat of the feast is to partake of the sacrifice.”

That’s why the two tables are mutually exclusive. Not because God is territorial about furniture, but because each table forms a union, and you cannot be covenantally joined in both directions at once. You have one seat. Every altar you eat from claims you. So Paul’s command at the top of the passage is not a suggestion—“flee from idolatry” (==1 Corinthians 10:14==)—and his invitation underneath it is glorious: there is a table where the cup joins you to the blood that redeemed you and the bread joins you to a body that will never fail you. Take your one seat there.

## Word Study
- *partake* : **metechō** — literally “to hold with” (meta, with + echō, to hold); to share in something so that you now possess a portion of it. This is stronger than attending or observing—the partaker doesn’t just sit at the table; he takes hold of what the table holds, and it becomes his.

Watch the word work elsewhere:

- ==1 Corinthians 10:17== — “we all *partake* (metechō) of that one bread.” One loaf, held-with by many hands, making the many one body.
- ==Hebrews 2:14== — “as the children have *partaken* (metechō) of flesh and blood, He Himself likewise shared in the same.” Before we ever partook of His table, He partook of ours—taking hold of our flesh and blood so that we could take hold of His.

Summary: metechō is why the two tables in this verse can’t be combined. Partaking isn’t polite attendance; it’s taking hold—and being taken hold of. The Son of God gripped our humanity all the way to death, and His table is where we grip Him back. Hands that are holding-with Christ have no grip to spare for another altar.
