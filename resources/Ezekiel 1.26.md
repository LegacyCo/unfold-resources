## Commentary
Ezekiel’s vision reaches its apex in verse 26. After the wind, the fire, the living creatures, and the wheels full of eyes, Ezekiel finally sees what is resting above it all.

A throne.

And on the throne is “a figure with the appearance of a man.”

Ezekiel is careful with his language. He does not say he saw God in a full or exhaustive way. He continues using words like _likeness_ and _appearance_ because he is describing something beyond ordinary human categories. He is seeing the glory of God, but he is still a creature trying to describe the Creator.

John tells us, “No one has seen God at any time. The only begotten Son…He has declared Him” (John 1:18). Paul says Christ is “the image of the invisible God” (Colossians 1:15). Hebrews says the Son is “the radiance of the glory of God and the exact imprint of His nature” (Hebrews 1:3).

In other words, when God makes Himself visible, understandable, and knowable, He does so through the Son.

Ezekiel does not yet have the language of the incarnation. He is not standing in Bethlehem or looking at the risen Christ. But his vision is moving in that direction. The glory above the throne is a Person. And that glory would one day take on flesh.

This is a consistent them in Scripture. The appearance of a man on the throne in Ezekiel becomes the Son of Man standing before the Ancient of Days in Daniel 7. It becomes the Word made flesh in John 1. It becomes Jesus, crucified and risen, saying, “All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me” (Matthew 28:18). It becomes Stephen, full of the Holy Spirit, seeing Jesus standing at the right hand of God (Acts 7:55–56). It becomes John in Revelation seeing the throne of God and the Lamb.

Ezekiel 1:26 is a window into the way God reveals Himself.

For Ezekiel, this would have been deeply significant. He is in exile. Jerusalem is under judgment. The people of God are displaced. Everything looks unstable. But above it all there is still a throne. 

## Application
In Jesus, we see the fullness of what Ezekiel only saw in a glimpse. But we can learn from Ezekiel's response to this vision. He didn't analyze or casually observe. He fell on his face.

That is the proper response to the glory of God revealed in the Son.

Jesus is not simply another prophet or teacher to learn from. He is not a helper or a doctrine to understand. He is the image of the invisible God, the radiance of God’s glory, and the One seated in authority above all things.

If Ezekiel fell before the appearance of the *likeness* of His glory, how much more should we live in awe before the One in whom that glory has been fully revealed?

To see Christ rightly is to be brought low in worship, surrender, and obedience.
