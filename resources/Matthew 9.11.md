## Commentary
“And when the Pharisees saw it, they said to His disciples, ‘Why does your Teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?’”

In the ancient world, sharing a meal carried social and relational significance. To eat at someone’s table communicated fellowship, welcome, and association. Table fellowship helped define who belonged together.

That is why the Pharisees' question is so pointed: “Why does your Teacher eat with tax collectors and sinners?”

Matthew has just left the tax booth and hosted a meal where “many tax collectors and sinners came and sat down with Him and His disciples” (==Matthew 9:10==). To the Pharisees, this looked like a compromise of holiness. Jesus’ response in the next two verses explains exactly how He understood the scene:

“Those who are well have no need of a physician, but those who are sick” (==Matthew 9:12==).

Jesus does not deny that these people are sinners. But, interestingly, He describes them as sick. This gives us a glimpse into the mind of God and His presence among the lost.

A physician does not avoid sick people because they are sick. Their sickness is the very reason he goes to them. Jesus is saying, in effect, _You are asking why I am sitting with sinners. That is precisely why I am here._

The Pharisees saw the table as evidence that Jesus was becoming too close to sinful people. Jesus saw the table as the place where the Physician had finally reached His patients.

Then He presses the point further:

“Go and learn what this means: ‘I desire mercy and not sacrifice.’ For I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance” (==Matthew 9:13==).

Jesus quotes ==Hosea 6:6==, where God confronts religious activity that has become disconnected from His own heart. Sacrifice was vital for God's people because God commanded it, but sacrifice without mercy missed the heart of God. The Pharisees were deeply concerned with holiness, obedience, and religious faithfulness, yet their version of holiness had produced distance from the very people God desired to restore.

Jesus reveals that holiness and mercy are not opposites.

True holiness moves toward what is broken in order to heal it.

This is what Jesus has already demonstrated throughout Matthew. He touches a leper, and instead of becoming unclean, the leper becomes clean (==Matthew 8:2–3==). He enters the spaces others avoid. He receives the people respectable society has rejected. His holiness does not need to protect itself from sinners. He is so holy that He takes the unclean and transforms it.

Jesus moves toward the sinners because He has come to bring them out of the life that has enslaved them and into the kingdom of God.
