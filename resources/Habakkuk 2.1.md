## Commentary
“I will stand my watch and set myself on the rampart, and watch to see what He will say *in* me.”

Habakkuk gives us one of Scripture’s clearest pictures of the waiting dimension of prayer. He’s wrestled with God, asked his hard questions—and now he climbs the watchtower. Not to talk. To watch. Prayer, for Habakkuk, isn’t finished when he’s done speaking; it moves into a posture of alert expectation, positioned to receive whatever God will answer.

Notice the strange phrase: “what He will say *in* me.” Not just *to* me—in me. God doesn’t only speak to us through His word; He speaks within us, in the renewed conscience and the inward witness of the Spirit (==Romans 8:16==). Which is why the watchtower matters. Those who expect to hear from God must withdraw from the noise of the world, get above it, and set themselves on the watch. You can’t hear an inward voice while living at street level, swept along in the traffic. The psalmist knew this: “My soul, wait silently for God alone, for my expectation is from Him” (==Psalm 62:5==).

Be clear about what this waiting is *not*. It isn’t passivity, and it isn’t killing time until God finally does something. A watchman on the wall is on duty—alert, positioned, scanning the horizon, responsible for what he sees. Biblical waiting is some of the most active work a believer ever does; it just doesn’t produce anything visible while it’s happening. That’s exactly why we skip it. Jesus asked it of His closest friends on His hardest night: “Sit here while I go and pray over there… What! Could you not *watch* with Me one hour? Watch and pray, lest you enter into temptation” (==Matthew 26:38-41==). The disciples slept through it. Most of us schedule over it.

But the watchman sees what the sleeper misses. Habakkuk climbed the tower with his questions still unanswered, and it was there—on watch—that the vision came, with the appointed time attached (==Habakkuk 2:2-3==). And those who wait gain more than answers: “those who *wait* on the LORD shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles” (==Isaiah 40:31==). Some answers, and some strength, God gives only to a person who has taken up a position and stayed in it.

## Application
Some practicals for waiting on God:

Take a position. Habakkuk says “I will *set myself*”—the watchman chooses his post before he sees anything. Pick a time and a place and treat it like a watch assignment, not a mood. Waiting that isn’t scheduled rarely happens.

Bring your questions, then stop talking. Habakkuk carried real complaints up that tower, and then he watched. Say what you need to say to God—honestly, specifically—and then sit in deliberate silence with your Bible open. Don’t fill every minute with words. Let the noise in you settle; the inward voice is quiet, and it usually speaks after yours runs out.

Watch in the word. “What He will say in me” often arrives through what’s in front of your eyes. Read slowly—one psalm, one passage—until something leans toward you. The Spirit loves to speak through the page He inspired.

Write down what you receive. This is God’s own next instruction to Habakkuk: “Write the vision and make it plain” (==Habakkuk 2:2==). Keep a notebook in the watch. An impression written down can be tested, prayed over, and obeyed; an impression unrecorded is usually forgotten by lunch. Test what you write against Scripture—He never contradicts His word.

Obey what He already said. If something He’s spoken is still sitting undone, tend to that first. Obedience to the last word is often the doorway to the next one (==Mark 4:24-25==).

Stay until relieved. A watchman doesn’t abandon his post because the first hour is quiet. The vision came to Habakkuk at its appointed time, and it did not lie. If day one of waiting is silent, come back to the tower on day two.
