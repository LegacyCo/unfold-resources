## Commentary
Look at the bookends of this passage. “So continuing *daily* with one accord in the temple, and breaking bread from house to house… And the Lord added to the church *daily* those who were being saved” (==Acts 2:46-47==). Two dailies. The church gathered daily, and God added daily. I don’t believe those two facts just happen to sit in the same sentence. Why did God add to the church daily? Because the church was together daily—and something is activated when the Body of Christ dwells together in one accord that cannot be activated any other way.

Notice the two settings in the verse, because they’re not the same thing. The early church gathered *at* the temple—Solomon’s Porch, a huge covered colonnade that functioned like a city square, packed with worshipers, religious leaders, beggars, the sick. That’s where they heard the apostles’ teaching, prayed for the sick, and boldly proclaimed the Messiah to the masses. What we do on Sundays is comparable to what the early church did at the temple. But the verse gives a second address: house to house. The temple was their public altar. The home was their private table—the informal, authentic, intimate setting where strangers became friends and friends became family.

And here’s the relationship between the two: house to house is not in place of the temple, and it’s not in addition to the temple. It’s the *catalyst* for what happens in the temple. A catalyst is what ignites the reaction—the spark that makes the engine run. A dozen believers sharing a meal around a table is a catalyst. A living room full of the family of God praying over one another is a catalyst. A Bible conversation on a back porch is a catalyst. A few families pitching in to buy someone’s groceries is a catalyst. Dropping your kids at another believer’s house because you need a break is a catalyst. Heart talks over a sink full of dirty dishes—catalyst. What happens house to house throughout the week is the fuel for what happens when the whole church comes together.

The table is not a program. It isn’t a Bible study or a supper club. It’s normal life becoming communal life. When the everyday things you used to do in isolation become everyday things done with God’s people, everyday things become spiritual things. Sinks full of dirty dishes become altars. And a watching community gets to see something it can’t argue with: a people who actually live like family. The Lord added daily to a church that lived like that daily—and there’s no reason to believe He’s changed His pattern.
