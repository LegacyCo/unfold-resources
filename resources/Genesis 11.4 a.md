## Commentary
“And they said, ‘Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.’”

Most readers focus on the tower. But the people said two things: “let us build ourselves a city, *and* a tower.” The city came first in their words, and the city is what this passage is really about. The tower was the monument; the city was the way of life the monument stood for—a community organized so that its people could take care of themselves without God.

In the fifth century, Augustine wrote *The City of God* to make the point that all of human history is a conflict between two cities. One is the city of man: everything people build to secure, protect, and glorify themselves in this present age. The other is the city of God: what God Himself is building, for the age to come, out of the people who belong to Him. The two cities have different founders, different purposes, and different futures. Every generation is building one or the other. Genesis 11 is the first place in Scripture where the city of man is actually founded—where human self-reliance stops being an individual's sin and becomes corporate sin.

The city was an act of rebellion against the covenant blessing God had just given. God told Noah's family to be fruitful, multiply, and fill the earth (==Genesis 9:1==). These people answered, in effect, “We will not scatter and fill. We will consolidate and build up.” Filling the earth with image-bearers required too much time and dependence on a God they did not understand, so rather than trusting His blessing of multiplication, they organized. Their reasoning has become every generation's reason for not trusting what God says: *if we do what He asks, we will not be as well off as we will be doing what we think is best*. So they built something magnificent, designed for fame and stability—and void of God. “Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it” (==Psalm 127:1==). The tower was the rallying point, the symbol that gave the people a sense of purpose; the city was the substance—a place of self-sufficiency and protection where they would not need God for their needs.

Genesis treats the city this way consistently. The first builder of a city in Scripture is Cain, the first murderer, who gathered others to himself for self-reliance (==Genesis 4:17==). When Lot separated from Abram, he went to the city—Sodom (==Genesis 13:12==). And the people of Genesis 11 are laying the foundations of Babylon. There is nothing wrong with a city, but there is something wrong with *that* city, built on that foundation of pride and self-sufficiency. Babylon is not just a place; it is a civilization organized as a means of living apart from God, and Scripture treats it as a system set against His purpose in the earth (==Revelation 18:2==). 

God has His own city, built His way, according to a heavenly pattern: Jerusalem in this age (==Deuteronomy 11:12==; ==Ezekiel 16:8==), and the New Jerusalem, the holy city, in the age to come—so precious to God that Scripture calls her the Bride, the Lamb's wife (==Revelation 21:9-11==). What Babylon is to the powers of darkness, Jerusalem is to the kingdom of God. If Jerusalem is the bride of the Lamb, Babylon—“the great harlot” (==Revelation 17:1==)—is the bride of the serpent.

## Application
Every person is building one of the two cities. Look at the reasoning behind your plans: are they drawn up so that you will not need God—stability, reputation, and provision secured on your own terms—or are they made under Him, in dependence on Him? Plans made apart from God, however well engineered, are Babel. Choose the harder obedience of scattering and filling—of trusting His blessing over your own consolidation—and build with your life a city whose builder and maker is God (==Hebrews 11:10==).
