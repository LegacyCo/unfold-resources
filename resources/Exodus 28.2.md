## Commentary
“And you shall make holy garments for Aaron your brother, for glory and for beauty.”

Glory implies radiance—the same idea Hebrews reaches for when it calls Jesus “the radiance of God’s glory” (==Hebrews 1:3==). And *holy* garments means that when Aaron wore them, there was none like him on the planet. Before Aaron could step into his calling, he had to be covered in something he could not produce: garments that projected his distinction and radiated glory and beauty. God still clothes His priests this way—and the garments are still woven by hands other than your own.

Look at who God assigned to the loom. He didn’t drop the garments down from heaven ready-made, and He didn’t tell Aaron to sew his own. He said, “Speak to all who are *gifted artisans*, whom I have filled with the spirit of wisdom, that they may make Aaron’s garments, to consecrate him” (==Exodus 28:3==). The garments came from within the camp—from members of Aaron’s own community, ordinary Israelites whom God had filled and anointed for exactly this. Aaron was called by God, anointed by God, commissioned by God—and still utterly dependent on the people around him to be clothed for his calling. The glory Aaron wore was woven by the very people he served among. And so is yours: God has determined that the community of His people will clothe one another in glory and beauty. Someone in the camp carries the anointing to make what you cannot make for yourself.

What are the garments now? The qualities of Jesus. And here’s the thing about those qualities: they can only be woven in real life with real people. Forgiveness is only a concept until you suffer a wound inflicted by another. Humility is never tested until you have the live opportunity to choose yourself over someone—and serve them instead. How can love be proven to be more than sentiment if it never gets the chance to suffer long and bear all things (==1 Corinthians 13:4==; ==1 Corinthians 13:7==)? On your own, God’s word stays theorized. It’s at the intersection of your life with other lives—in the daily grind—that concept becomes garment. That’s why Peter uses this exact imagery: “all of you, *clothe yourselves* with humility toward one another” (==1 Peter 5:5==).

And notice how long the weaving took. Read the rest of Exodus 28: a breastplate forged with four rows of precious stones, an ephod of gold, blue, purple, and scarlet thread, a robe with pomegranates stitched around the hem. No sewing machines. No fabric store down the road with the right shade of purple. Those artisans gave months of skilled, patient labor to clothe one man in glory. Becoming a people who clothe one another will take time too. This isn’t microwave Christianity. 

## Application
Put your thread in someone’s garment this week—not conceptually, practically. Invite people you worship beside every Sunday over for dinner. Take someone to lunch for the sole purpose of hearing their story. Babysit so a mom and dad can go on a date. Make dinner and drop it off so a tired mother gets a night off. When someone’s sick, do their laundry. Show up at someone else’s kid’s game and cheer. None of that looks like priestly garment-making. All of it is—one stitch of glory and beauty at a time, sewn into somebody who can’t clothe themselves.
