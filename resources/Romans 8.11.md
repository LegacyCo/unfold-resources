## Commentary
Throughout Romans 8, Paul is contrasting two realms: flesh and Spirit, death and life, the old creation and the new. Humanity in Adam has come under the reign of death, but those who belong to Christ have received the Spirit of the age to come. The resurrection life that will one day fill the renewed creation has already taken up residence within God’s people.

This is why Paul carefully identifies Him as “the Spirit of Him who raised Jesus from the dead.” The resurrection of Jesus was not simply God reversing something terrible that happened on Good Friday. Resurrection was the beginning of new creation. Jesus emerged from the grave as the firstfruits of the world God intends to make new (==1 Corinthians 15:20–23==). What happened to Him is therefore the beginning and guarantee of what will eventually happen to everything joined to Him.

And now that resurrection Spirit _dwells in you_.

The God whose glory once descended upon the tabernacle and later filled the temple has made His dwelling within human beings. Paul has already told the Corinthians, “you are the temple of God and…the Spirit of God dwells in you” (==1 Corinthians 3:16==). The presence that once rested behind the veil now lives within the people of God.

There may even be an echo here of the larger biblical story of creation. In the beginning, the Spirit of God hovered over a creation waiting to be filled with life (==Genesis 1:2==). In Ezekiel’s vision, the Spirit enters a valley filled with dead bones and they “lived, and stood upon their feet” (==Ezekiel 37:10==). Then God interprets the vision with the promise, “I will put My Spirit in you, and you shall live” (==Ezekiel 37:14==).

Romans 8 announces that this promise has begun.

The Spirit of resurrection has entered people who still inhabit a world marked by death.

That is why Paul continues:

“He who raised Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through His Spirit who dwells in you.”

Paul does not say just that God will give life to our _souls_. The Christian hope is bodily resurrection.

“Mortal” means _subject to death_. These bodies still weaken, age, suffer, and eventually die. Even believers who possess the Spirit remain physically mortal. Paul has already acknowledged this tension: “the body is dead because of sin, but the Spirit is life because of righteousness” (==Romans 8:10==). We live between resurrection begun and resurrection completed.

The Spirit within us is therefore both _presence and promise_.

His presence is evidence that God has already begun His work of new creation in us. His presence is also the guarantee that God will finish what He began. Paul describes the Spirit elsewhere as the “guarantee of our inheritance” (==Ephesians 1:13–14==). The Greek idea is a _down payment_—the first portion of something that guarantees the rest is coming.

We have the life of the coming age breaking into our mortal bodies even now.

This is where miraculous healing belongs within the larger biblical story. Jesus healed sick bodies, restored crippled limbs, opened blind eyes, cleansed lepers, and raised the dead. These miracles were not random demonstrations of divine power. They were signs that the kingdom of God had invaded the present age. They revealed ahead of time what creation will look like when death and corruption are finally removed.

The early church continued to experience these signs through the power of the Spirit. A man unable to walk from birth stood to his feet (==Acts 3:1–10==). The sick were healed as the apostles ministered in Jesus’ name (==Acts 5:12–16==). Paul describes “gifts of healings” among the manifestations of the Spirit within the church (==1 Corinthians 12:9==).

Healing is a foretaste of resurrection.

When God miraculously brings life and restoration to a mortal body, we are seeing in miniature what Romans 8 promises on the largest possible scale. A healed body will still eventually die. Lazarus walked out of his tomb, but he would one day die again. Present healing is therefore not the fullness of resurrection. It is a sign pointing toward it.

Every healing whispers that death does not get the final word. It is evidence that the Spirit who will one day raise the dead is already at work within the present creation. The powers of the coming age are sometimes experienced ahead of time (==Hebrews 6:5==). The future leaks into the present.

This gives the church every reason to pray boldly for healing because the Spirit of resurrection is present, because Jesus taught His disciples to pray for the kingdom to come “on earth as it is in heaven” (==Matthew 6:10==), and because healing is entirely consistent with the character and activity of the kingdom He announced.

We can therefore hold two truths without weakening either one: we expect God to heal, and we know that the complete redemption of our bodies still awaits the resurrection.

The “already” gives us faith to pray.

The “not yet” keeps our hope anchored beyond what happens in this present age.

Notice also Paul's wording: “He who raised Christ…will also give life to your mortal bodies.” Jesus’ resurrection becomes the pattern for ours. God does not abandon His creation and replace it with something unrelated. He redeems it. The body placed into the grave is raised into incorruptibility (==1 Corinthians 15:42–44==). Jesus' resurrection is the evidence that God's answer to the brokenness of creation is not escape from creation, but resurrection.

This becomes enormously important for understanding Romans 8 as a whole. A few verses later Paul says creation itself is waiting to be “delivered from the bondage of corruption” (==Romans 8:21==). Our bodies are part of that creation. They groan beneath corruption just as the world around us groans. This is why Paul says that even believers “groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for the adoption, the redemption of our body” (==Romans 8:23==).

The resurrection of our bodies and the renewal of creation belong to the same story.

God is not rescuing spirits from a doomed physical universe. He is making all things new.

And Romans 8:11 tells us that the power by which He will accomplish that future renewal is already present within us.

There is something almost outrageous about Paul's claim. The believer may presently feel weakness, sickness, suffering, exhaustion, aging, and the frailty of mortality—and yet hidden inside that mortal body is the Spirit of resurrection. Our present weakness does not tell the whole truth about our future.

We do not have to treat weakness, sickness, or decay as though they are ultimate realities. We can lay hands on the sick and pray with expectation. We can ask God to bring the life of the coming kingdom into a mortal body now. We can resist the temptation to make peace with everything death has touched simply because it feels normal in a fallen world.
