## Commentary
When Matthew quotes Isaiah's prophecy concerning the Servant of the Lord, he uses two images to describe the character of Jesus: “A bruised reed He will not break, and smoking flax He will not quench.” Both images describe something that has become weakened or damaged and is in danger of being destroyed.

A reed was a common plant that grew along the banks of rivers and streams. Although useful for a variety of purposes, reeds were relatively fragile. Once bruised or bent, they could easily be broken off. Similarly, the expression “smoking flax” refers to the wick of an oil lamp that has nearly burned out. Instead of producing a steady flame, it is smoldering and giving off smoke. Ordinarily, someone might extinguish such a wick rather than allow it to continue smoking.

Isaiah uses these images to describe people who are weak, wounded, oppressed, or barely holding on. The bruised reed represents someone who has been damaged by the circumstances of life, while the smoldering wick represents someone whose strength or hope has nearly been exhausted.

Jesus doesn't look at people in their weakness and conclude that they are no longer worth His attention. He doesn't despise those whose faith is struggling or whose lives have been damaged by sin, suffering, or failure. Rather, He approaches them with gentleness, patience, and a desire to restore what has been damaged.

This is especially significant in the immediate context of Matthew's Gospel. The Pharisees had become so concerned with protecting their interpretation of the Sabbath that they were willing to destroy Jesus for healing a man with a withered hand (==Matthew 12:9–14==). The religious leaders who were supposed to shepherd God's people had become a source of oppression, while Jesus, the true Servant of the Lord, was restoring those who had been neglected and broken.

Just a few verses earlier, He invited those who were weary and burdened to come to Him and find rest (==Matthew 11:28–30==). He doesn't require us to become strong before coming to Him. He invites us to come precisely because we are weak and in need of His help.

The invitation isn't to remain in our brokenness, but to entrust ourselves to the One who has both the compassion and the power to restore us.
