## Commentary
“A soft answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger.”

Your answer decides what happens next. Wrath comes at you like a fire looking for fuel—and Solomon says you’re holding either water or gasoline. A soft answer *turns away* the wrath; a harsh word *stirs up* more of it. The other person started the exchange, but your reply determines what happens next. That’s a sobering amount of responsibility, and it’s also real power: the person who controls the temperature of his answer controls the temperature of the room.

A soft answer is not a weak one. Softness here is about the spirit of the reply, not the substance—you can hold your position, tell the truth, even deliver correction, with a gentleness that gives anger nothing to hold onto. And it isn’t improvised. “The heart of the righteous *studies* how to answer, but the mouth of the wicked pours forth evil” (==Proverbs 15:28==). Pouring forth takes no thought; studying does. The wise respond; the foolish just react. Gideon showed the proverb in action: when the men of Ephraim confronted him sharply, itching for a fight, his generous answer deflated them—“then their anger toward him subsided when he said that” (==Judges 8:1-3==).

Behind the studied answer stands a ruled spirit. “He who is slow to wrath has great understanding, but he who is impulsive exalts folly” (==Proverbs 14:29==), and “he who rules his spirit [is better] than he who takes a city” (==Proverbs 16:32==). Scripture ranks self-rule above conquest—the man who can govern his own reaction outranks the one who commands armies. So the next time a harsh word comes at you, recognize the moment for what it is: someone just handed you the thermostat. A soft answer isn’t surrender. It’s command of the one territory you’re actually responsible for.

## Application
Study your answers before you need them. Identify the person most likely to bring you a harsh word this week—the coworker, the customer, the family member—and decide now what a soft answer to them sounds like, so you’re not improvising in the heat. Put a delay between provocation and reply: the angry text sits in drafts overnight; the email gets reread in the morning; in conversation, a slow breath buys your spirit the second it needs to rule. And watch your volume and tone with the people closest to you—home is where harsh words cost the most and soft answers heal the most. One practical test for the week: end every heated exchange with the temperature lower than you found it.
