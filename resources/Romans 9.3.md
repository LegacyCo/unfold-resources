## Commentary
At the end of the previous chapter, Paul makes one of the strongest declarations of assurance in all of Scripture. He is persuaded that neither death nor life, angels nor principalities, things present nor things to come, nor anything else in creation will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus (==Romans 8:38–39==). Yet only a few verses later, Paul makes an astonishing statement: "For I could wish that I myself were accursed from Christ for my brethren, my countrymen according to the flesh."

The Greek word translated accursed is *anathema*, which refers to something devoted to destruction or placed under divine judgment. Paul is saying that if it were possible, he would willingly bear the judgment and separation from Christ that his fellow Israelites faced because of their unbelief.

This is not a contradiction of what he has just written in Romans 8, nor is Paul suggesting that someone can actually surrender their salvation on behalf of another person. He is using extraordinary language to communicate the depth of his love and grief for his people.

There is an important Old Testament parallel here. After Israel worshiped the golden calf, Moses returned to the Lord and pleaded on their behalf: "Yet now, if You will forgive their sin—but if not, I pray, blot me out of Your book which You have written" (==Exodus 32:32==). Moses had not participated in Israel's rebellion, yet he was willing to identify himself with the people who had sinned. He loved them enough to offer himself in their place.

Paul expresses that same heart toward Israel.

What makes this even more remarkable is Paul's personal history. Before encountering Jesus on the Damascus road, Paul had been a zealous persecutor of the church. He had been deeply committed to the religious traditions of his fathers and had viewed the followers of Jesus as enemies of God. Now, having encountered the risen Christ and received the revelation of the gospel, Paul understood that the very people he once considered righteous because of their devotion to the Law were in danger of missing the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ (==Romans 10:1–4==).

But this revelation did not cause Paul to become arrogant toward those who had not yet received what he had received. He did not distance himself from his Jewish heritage or develop contempt for those who rejected his message. Instead, his understanding of the gospel produced an extraordinary burden of love for the very people who opposed him.

Paul wasn't interested in proving that his theology was correct. He wanted his people to know Jesus.

It is possible to possess a correct understanding of the gospel while lacking the heart of the One who gave it to us. We can become so consumed with defending the truth that we lose our love for the people who have not yet received it.

Paul refused to separate the two. His unwavering conviction concerning the truth of the gospel was accompanied by genuine sorrow for those who remained outside of Christ. In the following chapter, he writes, "Brethren, my heart's desire and prayer to God for Israel is that they may be saved" (==Romans 10:1==).

The love Paul expresses in Romans 9 is therefore not simply an extraordinary example of human compassion. It is evidence of the life of Christ being formed within him. The same Jesus who willingly gave Himself for His enemies was now expressing His own heart through a man who had once been an enemy of the cross.

## Application
This ought to challenge the way we think about those who are far from God, particularly those who oppose what we believe. Do we primarily see them as people who need to be corrected, or as people whom Christ loves and desires to reconcile to Himself?

Paul's example reminds us that spiritual maturity is not measured by how much truth we understand, but by how deeply the love of Christ has transformed the way we see and respond to others.
