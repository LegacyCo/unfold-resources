## Commentary
“But by the grace of God I am what I am, and His grace toward me was not in vain; but I labored more abundantly than they all, yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me.”

Paul makes three statements about grace in one verse, and together they correct the most common misunderstanding of the word. First: grace made him what he was—the persecutor of the church became its apostle by gift, not achievement (==1 Corinthians 15:9==). Second: that grace “was not in vain”—it was not received and then left idle. Third, and most striking: the evidence that grace was not in vain is that Paul *labored more abundantly than they all.* Grace did not exempt Paul from labor. Grace produced labor—and then Paul immediately corrects even that accounting: “yet not I, but the grace of God which was with me.” The work was real, the sweat was his, and the power in it was God’s.

This means grace is not only pardon; it is empowerment. Much teaching treats grace as what God does *instead of* our effort. Paul describes grace as what God does *through* his effort: “To this end I also labor, striving according to His working which works in me mightily” (==Colossians 1:29==). The same doubled agency appears in Philippians: “work out your own salvation… for it is God who works in you both to will and to do” (==Philippians 2:12-13==). Grace and labor are not competitors in Paul’s vocabulary. Grace is the reason the labor exists and the power by which it accomplishes anything.

The verse also settles how a person can look at a transformed life without pride or false modesty. Paul doesn’t deny what he became (“I am what I am”) and doesn’t credit himself for it (“yet not I”). Both boasting and self-deprecation are answered by the same word: grace. What God’s grace has made you, you may fully own—and fully attribute.
