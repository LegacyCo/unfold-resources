## Commentary
“If You are going to deal with me this way, please kill me now, if I have found favor in Your sight, and do not let me see my misery!” These are not the words of a man who has everything under control. They are the words of a leader who has reached the end of himself.

Just thirteen verses earlier, Moses had interceded for the people when God's judgment fell upon them (==Numbers 11:2==). Now those same people have become such an overwhelming burden that Moses is asking God to take his life. The man who was pleading for their survival is now struggling with his own desire to continue.

The preceding verses help us understand his frustration. Moses asks God, “Did I conceive all these people? Did I beget them?” (==Numbers 11:12==). In other words, Moses is asking why he is being made responsible for satisfying the demands of an entire nation. He cannot produce meat for hundreds of thousands of people. He cannot satisfy their endless complaints. He cannot carry the emotional and spiritual weight of their constant dissatisfaction. And yet, he feels as though all of that responsibility has been placed upon his shoulders.

Moses had been called to lead Israel, but he had not been called to be Israel's source. That responsibility belonged to God alone. Somewhere in the overwhelming demands of leadership, Moses was feeling the weight of responsibilities that were beyond his ability to fulfill.

God does not rebuke Moses for reaching his limit or tell him to become stronger. Instead, He instructs Moses to gather seventy elders and promises to place upon them the Spirit who was upon Moses so that “they shall bear the burden of the people with you, that you may not bear it yourself alone” (==Numbers 11:16–17==).

God's answer to Moses' exhaustion was to provide Spirit-empowered people who could share the responsibility.

There is also an important distinction between Moses' response and the complaining of the Israelites. The people were grumbling against God and questioning His goodness, while Moses brought his anguish directly to God. His prayer was raw, emotionally charged, and certainly not an example of measured restraint. But he brought his pain to the One who could actually do something about it. There is a difference between allowing our frustration to become an accusation against God's character and honestly bringing our distress into His presence.

We see something similar in Elijah, who, after an extraordinary victory on Mount Carmel, became so overwhelmed that he asked God to take his life (==1 Kings 19:4==). God's response included providing food, rest, renewed direction, and eventually a successor who would share in the prophetic work.

## Application
Sometimes we confuse faithfulness with the willingness to carry everything ourselves. We assume that because God has entrusted us with a responsibility, we must personally meet every need, solve every problem, and carry every burden. But God's calling does not make us the source of everything the people around us need.

There is a difference between being faithful to the responsibility God has given us and assuming responsibility for things only God can do.

If you find yourself overwhelmed by the demands of ministry, family, or leadership, perhaps the answer is not to work harder or become stronger. Perhaps you need to examine whether you have taken ownership of burdens God never intended you to carry alone. Ask Him for wisdom to recognize the difference between what He has called you to do and what you have assumed you must do for everyone else.

Moses' experience reminds us that even the most faithful servants of God have limits. Recognizing those limits is not a failure of faith. Sometimes it's the beginning of learning to depend upon God in a deeper way, allowing Him to provide both the strength and the people necessary to accomplish what He has called us to do.
