## Commentary
“But now, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells in me.”

Paul wrote two “no longer I” statements, and they describe two different men. Here: “it is no longer I who do it, but *sin* that dwells in me.” In Galatians: “it is no longer I who live, but *Christ* lives in me” (==Galatians 2:20==). Both sentences describe a man defined by what dwells in him—and that is precisely why this section of Romans 7 has divided interpreters for centuries. Who is the man in verses 14–25, doing what he hates, failing to do the good he wills (==Romans 7:15==; ==Romans 7:19==)?

One reading, held by Augustine in his later years and by Luther, Calvin, and modern commentators like C. E. B. Cranfield, says this is the mature believer's ongoing struggle. Their strongest evidence sits in verse 22: “I delight in the law of God according to the inward man”—and an unregenerate man does not delight in God's law (==Psalm 1:2==). The other reading, held by most of the early Greek fathers and defended by scholars like Douglas Moo and Gordon Fee, says Paul is describing life under the law apart from Christ—dramatized in the present tense, but not the Christian's normal condition. Their evidence is the tension you cannot unsee once you've seen it: the man in this chapter is “carnal, sold under sin” (==Romans 7:14==), held “captive to the law of sin” (==Romans 7:23==)—but Paul has just written that the believer “has been set free from sin” (==Romans 6:18==; ==Romans 6:22==), and is about to write that “the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus has made me free from the law of sin and death” (==Romans 8:2==). *Sold under sin* and *set free from sin* cannot both be the believer's identity. Fee and others also point out what is missing from Romans 7:14-25: the Holy Spirit is never mentioned—the struggle is fought entirely in the strength of “I,” a word that appears over twenty times—while the Spirit fills nearly every verse of chapter 8.

The two views agree on more than they dispute. Believers genuinely experience Romans 7:15 moments—doing what we hate is not foreign territory to anyone honest. But experience is not identity, and this is where Paul's other letters seem to settle the issue. The man Paul says he *is* looks like Galatians 2:20, not Romans 7:17: crucified with Christ, indwelt by Christ, a new creation (==2 Corinthians 5:17==), dead to sin and alive to God (==Romans 6:11==). Romans 7 describes what life looks like whenever a person—saved or unsaved—tries to overcome sin by willpower under the law: sincere, defeated, and exhausted. Paul doesn't leave the wretched man in verse 24 with advice to try harder. He answers the cry with a Person (==Romans 7:25==) and then opens the chapter that was true of him all along: “There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus” (==Romans 8:1==). The believer's address is chapter 8. Chapter 7 is what it feels like to forget that.
