## Commentary
"Death and life are in the power of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit."

We understand the principle of sowing and reaping when it comes to money: sow sparingly, reap sparingly; sow generously, reap generously (==2 Corinthians 9:6==, ==Galatians 6:7==). But do we understand that the same principle governs our words?

Solomon says the tongue plants. Every sentence goes into the ground of our lives and the lives around us as a seed of one of two crops—death or life. And here's the sobering part: "those who love it will eat its fruit." We don't just speak our words and walk away. We *live in their harvest*. The family atmosphere we're eating today was seasoned by years of words. The faith or the discouragement in our own hearts has been fed by what we've been declaring over our situations.

Let's be careful here. Not every hardship is a harvest of our own words—we live in a fallen world, and conflict and trouble find even the most careful mouth (==John 16:33==). But some of us have suffered season after season in the same patch of ground, eating the fruit of what we keep planting: predictions of failure, rehearsals of offense, verdicts of hopelessness spoken over ourselves and our children. And we've called it "just how it always is", when some of it is the fruit of our speech.

The good news is that the principle works in both directions. The tongue that can plant death can plant life. Start sowing different seed—God's promises spoken over your circumstances, blessing spoken over your family, thanksgiving spoken over what He has done. You will see a difference.

## Word Study
- *power* : **yad** (Hebrew) — literally "hand." Death and life are *in the hand* of the tongue.

The Hebrew doesn't picture the tongue's power as an abstract force. It gives the tongue a *hand*—something that grips, wields, hands over, delivers. Elsewhere *yad* is the hand into which things are entrusted: "Into Your hand I commit my spirit" (==Psalm 31:5==). Solomon's image is that every time we speak, the tongue's hand is delivering something—either death or life—into a situation.

Summary: the tongue is not a bystander commenting on your life. It has its hands on your life.
