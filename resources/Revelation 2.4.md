## Commentary
“Nevertheless I have this against you, that you have left your first love.”

Ephesus didn’t lose their first love—they *left* it. This was a departure, not a misplacement. And notice everything they still had when Jesus said it: works, labor, patience, doctrinal discernment, endurance without weariness (==Revelation 2:2-3==). A church can keep the whole machine running—busy, orthodox, persevering—after the flame that started it has gone out. Ephesus kept the activity of love long after leaving the love itself.

How does that happen? They left the cross. First love and the cross are inseparable, because the fire of God and the crucified life are two sides of the same coin—Jesus people never leave the upper room, and they never graduate from Calvary. Ephesus drifted from the place of daily surrender; Laodicea shows the finished trajectory: “rich, wealthy, in need of nothing”—and lukewarm (==Revelation 3:16-17==). Comfort is how first love dies. The idea that your internal flame will sustain itself while you settle down is not just unrealistic; it’s the enemy’s lie designed to get you to settle. A diluted cross will never keep anyone under the flame of Pentecost. A.W. Tozer described the undiluted version: “The man in Roman times who took up his cross and started down the road was never coming back. The cross made no compromise, modified nothing, spared nothing; it slew all of the man, completely and for good.” Leonard Ravenhill said it even shorter: “Jesus didn’t come to make bad men good, He came to make dead men live.”

And that’s exactly the remedy Jesus prescribes to Ephesus: “Remember therefore from where you have fallen; repent and do the first works” (==Revelation 2:5==). Go back to where you left it. The solution for lack of fire is always the cross—the daily surrender, the first works, the life laid down all over again (==Luke 9:23==). First love isn’t recovered by trying to feel what you felt. It’s recovered at the place you last had it: Calvary.
