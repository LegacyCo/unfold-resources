## Commentary
“Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted up his heel against me.”

David isn’t describing an enemy. That’s what makes this verse ache. The wound came from a familiar friend—someone he trusted, someone who ate at his table. And a thousand years later, Jesus took this exact verse on His own lips in an upper room, with the bread in His hands and Judas at the table: “that the Scripture may be fulfilled, ‘He who eats bread with Me has lifted up his heel against Me’” (==John 13:18==). The deepest betrayal in history didn’t come from Rome. It came from inside the family, sealed with a kiss.

So let’s be honest about the church: family is hard, family is messy, and many of us have been hurt by people we once called family. Entering any real relationship exposes you to risk, because you’re dealing with people. But here’s what the hurt doesn’t give us: permission to rewrite God’s definitions. Church is a family—that’s His design, not our experiment—and swearing off the church because church people hurt you is like getting food poisoning and deciding to never eat again. You don’t quit food. You learn discernment—what to look for before you take something in. The answer to a bad experience of family is a healthy one, not a life of starvation.

And there’s something more. If you’ve been wounded by God’s people, you've walked the same road your King walked—trusted friends, shared bread, a lifted heel. You carry some of the same scars He does. When Jesus rose, He didn’t hide those scars; He showed them (==John 20:20==), and they became the very thing that convinced the doubter (==John 20:27==). Kingdom people end up looking like their King—scars and all. So bring the wound to the One who understands it from the inside, let Him heal it, and come back to the table. That’s where He was betrayed. It’s also where He’s known in the breaking of bread (==Luke 24:35==).
