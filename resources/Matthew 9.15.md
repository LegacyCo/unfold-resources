## Commentary
“And Jesus said to them, ‘Can the friends of the bridegroom mourn as long as the bridegroom is with them? But the days will come when the bridegroom will be taken away from them, and then they will fast.’”

Jesus' followers were apparently known for *not* fasting—and His answer explains why. You don't fast at the wedding; that would be an insult to the groom. While Jesus was with His disciples, fasting was unnecessary, because the thing every fast in Israel's history had longed for was standing there with them, eating with sinners. But the days will come, He says. He will be taken away. And then His people will fast.

We live in those days. And this reframes what fasting is: not merely a discipline of self-denial, but an expression of longing. A bride doesn't skip a meal to prove something; she has lost her appetite because the One she loves isn't present. Every Christian fast has that ache underneath it—*Maranatha*, come, Lord Jesus; make the wrong things right (==Revelation 22:20==). Something is wrong because Someone is missing. This is why nothing tenderizes a heart like fasting. And it is why fasting has nothing to do with legalism: if the practice has felt like law to you, hear the Bridegroom language of this verse. Fasting is not the language of legalism. It is wedding language—covenantal, personal, and aimed at a Person. We fast because life distracts us from what is ultimate, and fasting recenters us on the eternal—on the Groom whose absence we feel and whose return we ache for (==Titus 2:13==).
