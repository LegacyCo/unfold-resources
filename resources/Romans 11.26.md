## Commentary
Few statements in Paul's writings have generated more theological debate than these five words: “And so all Israel will be saved.” What exactly does Paul mean by *all Israel*? Is he referring to every Jewish person who has ever lived? Is he describing the Church, composed of believing Jews and Gentiles? Or is he anticipating a future turning of the Jewish people to Jesus as their Messiah?

I believe the immediate context supports the third interpretation.

Throughout Romans 9–11, Paul has been wrestling with the unbelief of his own people. His concern is not an abstract theological question about salvation, but the present condition and future destiny of his own people. Paul has distinguished Israel from the Gentiles, described the hardening that has come upon part of the nation, and explained that Gentile salvation is intended to provoke Israel to jealousy. It would be difficult to imagine that Paul suddenly changes the meaning of Israel in verse 26 to refer to the Gentile Church.

The expression “all Israel” should also not be understood to mean every individual Jewish person who has ever lived, or even every Jewish person alive at a particular moment. Throughout the Old Testament, the expression can refer to Israel corporately, as a people, without requiring the inclusion of every individual (==1 Samuel 7:5; 2 Samuel 5:3; 1 Kings 12:1==). Paul appears to be anticipating a substantial, corporate turning of the Jewish people to their Messiah, a restoration that exceeds the remnant of Jewish believers who have already received Him.

But there is something even more important than identifying who Paul means by Israel. We need to consider what he means by *saved*.

Notice the Scripture Paul quotes immediately after making this declaration: “The Deliverer will come out of Zion, and He will turn away ungodliness from Jacob; for this is My covenant with them, when I take away their sins.” Paul draws upon ==Isaiah 59:20–21== and ==Isaiah 27:9== to explain the nature of Israel's promised salvation.

Israel's salvation is not a matter of national preservation, political restoration, or renewed recognition among the nations. It is the removal of ungodliness and the forgiveness of sin. The people who rejected their Messiah will be brought into a right relationship with the very One they rejected.

And the Deliverer Himself will turn away ungodliness from Jacob. This is not Israel somehow coming to their senses on their own. This is God acting in sovereign mercy to accomplish what Israel could never accomplish for herself.

There is a connection here to ==Ezekiel 36:25–27==, where God promises to cleanse Israel from her uncleanness, remove her heart of stone, give her a heart of flesh, and place His Spirit within her. The repeated emphasis in Ezekiel is upon what God Himself will do. Israel's restoration is not ultimately the achievement of human religious effort, but the consequence of divine intervention.

We also hear something of this promised awakening in ==Zechariah 12:10==, where God declares that He will pour out upon the house of David and the inhabitants of Jerusalem a spirit of grace and supplication, and they will look upon the One whom they pierced and mourn. It is a picture of profound recognition and repentance, brought about by the gracious initiative of God.

Paul is not introducing a second means of salvation for the Jewish people. There is no salvation for Israel apart from Christ, just as there is no salvation for the Gentiles apart from Him. The mystery is not that Israel will be saved without recognizing Jesus. It is that the people presently hardened toward Him will, through God's mercy, come to recognize the One in whom their covenant promises find fulfillment.

Even the opening expression, “And so,” is significant. The Greek *houtōs* can mean “in this way,” suggesting that Paul is describing not merely when Israel will be saved, but something of the manner in which God's purposes will unfold. Israel's stumbling, the salvation of the Gentiles, the provocation to jealousy, and Israel's eventual restoration are connected to the mystery Paul has been revealing.

Paul anticipates a day when the people who have largely stumbled over the stumbling stone will recognize Him as their Deliverer. And when that happens, the glory will belong entirely to God. Israel will not be able to boast in her faithfulness, and the Gentiles will have no reason to boast in theirs. Both will stand as recipients of a mercy they could never have earned.
