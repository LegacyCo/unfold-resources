## Commentary
This verse is the theological center of the psalm. The people do not appeal to their innocence. They appeal to God’s name.

Jerusalem’s destruction was not random. The prophets had warned Judah for generations that covenant rebellion would bring judgment. Idolatry, injustice, bloodshed, and refusal to hear the word of the Lord had brought the nation to this place. So the psalmist doesn't pray, “Lord, we don't deserve this.” Instead, he prays, “Deliver us, and atone for our sins, for Your name’s sake.”

That is honest repentance.

The psalmist knows Israel needs more than rescue from Babylon. They need atonement before God. Their deepest problem is not only that enemies have surrounded them, but that sin has overtaken them. So the prayer reaches beyond military deliverance into mercy: “atone for our sins.”

And yet the plea is bold: “for the glory of Your name.” The nations are mocking. Jerusalem is ruined. The temple is defiled. To the watching world, it looks as though Yahweh has either abandoned His people or failed to save them. So the psalmist asks God to act in a way that reveals His glory again.

This is praying covenantally. Moses prayed this way. Joshua prayed this way. Daniel prayed this way. The people of God often have no argument left except the character, mercy, and reputation of God.

## Application
When we have fallen short, we can still appeal to the God of our salvation. When our failure is real, mercy is still available. Psalm 79:9 teaches us to pray from the ruins without pretending we are innocent: “Help us. Deliver us. Atone for us. Do it for Your name.”
