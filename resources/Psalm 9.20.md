## Commentary
“Put them in fear, O LORD, that the nations may know themselves to be but men.”

David ends the psalm with a prayer that the nations would be brought back into a right understanding of themselves.

The danger of human power is that it can create the illusion of divinity. Nations rise, kings conquer, armies expand, wealth accumulates, and people begin to imagine that they are untouchable. Power has a way of making mortal people forget that they are mortal.

So David prays, “that the nations may know that they are but men.”

The Hebrew word used here is _enosh_, a word that often emphasizes humanity in its frailty, weakness, and mortality. David is saying, in effect, “Remind them that they are fragile human beings.” There is a great mercy in this.

One of the most dangerous things that can happen to a person or a nation is to forget the distance between Creator and creature. Pride blurs that distinction. 

But Scripture repeatedly brings humanity back to reality: “All flesh is grass” (==Isaiah 40:6==), “Man is like a breath” (==Psalm 144:4==), and even the most powerful rulers are still dependent on the God who “removes kings and raises up kings” (==Daniel 2:21==).

Psalm 9 is therefore not just a prayer for God to defeat hostile nations. It is a prayer for revelation.

“Let them know what they are.”

There is something deeply healthy about remembering our creatureliness. We are not God. We do not hold the world together. We cannot guarantee tomorrow. Our strength has limits, our knowledge has limits, and our lives are brief.

The fear of the Lord begins when human beings recover a proper sense of proportion. God is God. And we are but men.
