## Commentary
“…who Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree, that we, having died to sins, might live for righteousness—by whose stripes you were healed.”

Look at the purpose clause in the middle of this verse, because it tells you what the cross was *for*. Christ bore our sins in His own body—not just so we could be forgiven and wait for heaven, but “that we, having died to sins, might live for righteousness.” The cross doesn’t just cancel a debt. It initiates an exchange from one way of living to another.

Sin, at its root, is distortion—living out of alignment with the design God gave you, a broken identity that then produces broken behavior. Righteousness is the opposite: the condition of one who is as he was designed to be, restored to the image-and-likeness blueprint of ==Genesis 1:26==. Put those definitions into Peter’s sentence and it reads like this: *He bore our sins in His body so that we would die to the distorted lie and come alive to the original design.* That’s what the Bible calls being born again. The old, misaligned self went into the tomb with Him; what came out is a person God looks at and says, “You remind Me of Me.”

This is why Peter can attach healing to the same verse—“by whose stripes you were healed.” The cross is restoration at every level: identity, alignment, wholeness. And it’s why the believer’s relationship to sin has fundamentally changed. You haven’t just been pardoned for sinning; you have *died* to the whole distorted system (==Romans 6:11==).
