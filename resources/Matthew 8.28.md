## Commentary
Matthew now brings geography into the discussion to show us who Jesus is.

Jesus has crossed the Sea of Galilee into “the country of the Gergesenes” (==Matthew 8:28==). There is some variation among ancient manuscripts over the name of the region—Gergesenes, Gadarenes, or Gerasenes—but all of the possibilities locate the story on the eastern side of the Sea of Galilee, in or near territory associated in Jesus’ day with the largely Gentile Decapolis.

Interestingly, this region is directly connected to Israel's past.

When Israel entered the Promised Land, the tribe of Gad received territory east of the Jordan (==Numbers 32:33==; ==Joshua 13:24–28==). But by the first century, this area now contained a substantial Gentile population.

And Matthew gives us an unmistakable clue about the character of the region: there is a large herd of pigs nearby (==Matthew 8:30==).

According to the Law, pigs were unclean animals (==Leviticus 11:7==). Mark tells us that the herd numbered approximately two thousand (==Mark 5:13==), something difficult to imagine being raised for an observant Jewish population.

Look at how much _uncleanness_ has accumulated in this scene. Jesus has entered Gentile territory. He encounters demonized men. They are living among tombs—the place of the dead, contact with which also produced ceremonial uncleanness (==Numbers 19:16==). There are pigs nearby.

Everything about the scene would have communicated _unclean_ to a Jewish reader.

And Jesus deliberately crossed the sea to get there.

That becomes even more striking when we consider everything Matthew has shown us throughout this chapter.

First, an unclean leper approaches Jesus (==Matthew 8:2==). Then a Gentile centurion approaches Him (==Matthew 8:5==). Jesus takes sickness and disease upon Himself in fulfillment of Isaiah (==Matthew 8:16–17==). He crosses the chaotic sea and demonstrates His authority over it (==Matthew 8:23–27==).

And now He steps onto the other side into a place saturated with everything Israel would have considered unclean.

Matthew keeps moving Jesus toward the people and places others would have avoided. And the remarkable thing is that _none of their uncleanness makes Jesus unclean._ The movement always goes the other direction. The leper does not contaminate Jesus; Jesus cleanses the leper. The Gentile’s distance from Israel does not diminish Jesus’ authority; Jesus heals his servant. The demons do not overpower Jesus; they recognize His authority and submit to Him.

Jesus is not threatened by uncleanness. _Uncleanness is threatened by Jesus._

There may even be something significant in the journey itself. Jesus has just demonstrated that the sea cannot stop Him from reaching the other side (==Matthew 8:26–27==). Now, having arrived, the powers of darkness cannot stop Him either.

Matthew is showing us a kingdom advancing into territory that appears completely hostile to holiness. But Jesus does not remain safely among the clean and wait for the unclean to find their way back.

_He crosses over to them._
