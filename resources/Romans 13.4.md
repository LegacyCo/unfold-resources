## Commentary
"For he is God's minister to you for good. But if you do evil, be afraid; for he does not bear the sword in vain; for he is God's minister, an avenger to execute wrath on him who practices evil."

A few years before Paul wrote this, the Roman Senate declared the emperor Claudius a god. Augustus had been given the same honor before him. Rome liked its rulers to be worshiped. But Paul gives the emperor a very different title. Twice in this one verse he calls him God's *minister*, His servant.

A servant doesn't own the house. He answers to the One whose house it is, and he's there to do what his master wants done. Paul tells us what God wants from the people He puts in authority. The ruler is "God's minister to you *for good*." The verse before says, "rulers are not a terror to good works, but to evil" (==Romans 13:3==). Governing authority exists to protect people who do good and to restrain people who do evil.

That's why Paul says the ruler "does not bear the sword in vain." The sword is the power to punish, and Scripture doesn't treat it as evil in itself. A few verses earlier, Paul told believers never to take revenge but to "give place to wrath" (==Romans 12:19==). Here he uses the same words, wrath and avenger, for the ruler. One of the ways God restrains evil in this age is through courts, police, and just punishment. Take that sword away and the strong are free to prey on the weak. The book of Judges shows what that looks like. "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (==Judges 21:25==), and the chapters leading up to that verse tell of a woman abused and murdered, a tribe that refused to hand over the men who did it, and a civil war that nearly wiped that tribe out (==Judges 19–21==).

But what happens when the servant turns the house upside down? What do Christians do when the people holding the sword use it against the innocent?

This isn't a theoretical question. Christians have lived under rulers like that for most of church history, and many still do today. The clearest modern example is Nazi Germany. Hitler's government murdered about six million Jews, along with disabled people and many others, while many church leaders quoted Romans 13 to keep Christians loyal. That regime was a terror to good works and a protector of evil. It had turned Paul's description inside out.

God's people are never to take part in the evil a ruler commands. When Pharaoh ordered the Hebrew midwives to kill the baby boys, they "feared God, and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them," and "God dealt well with the midwives" (==Exodus 1:17, 20==).

Protecting the innocent from a murderous ruler is righteous. When Queen Athaliah set out to kill every heir of David, Jehosheba hid the infant Joash and saved his life (==2 Kings 11:1–3==). Obadiah hid a hundred prophets in caves to keep them from Jezebel (==1 Kings 18:4==). Rahab hid the spies from her own king, and she's honored for it in the list of the faithful (==Hebrews 11:31==). The wise men ignored Herod's instructions and went home another way (==Matthew 2:12==). Christians who hid Jews from the Nazis were standing in a long biblical line of resistance.

Speaking up for the victims is part of our calling. "Open your mouth for the speechless, in the cause of all who are appointed to die" (==Proverbs 31:8==).

Where faithful Christians disagree is on the next step. May believers use force to resist or remove a ruler like Hitler? Many Christians in the first three centuries never took up arms against Rome, even under Nero, and some traditions still hold that the believer may refuse, flee, protect, and speak, but must suffer rather than fight. The Reformed tradition, following John Calvin, taught that lower officials have a God-given responsibility to restrain a tyrant, so resistance should come through lawful authorities rather than private individuals. Others go further and argue that a ruler who becomes a murderer of the innocent has abandoned the authority this verse describes. Scripture itself records a priest named Jehoiada leading the overthrow of Athaliah and putting Joash on the throne (==2 Kings 11:4–16==), though it describes that event without turning it into a rule for every situation.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a pastor in the Confessing Church, was drawn into the conspiracy against Hitler and was executed in April 1945, weeks before the war in Europe ended. He never claimed his choice was blameless. He believed a Christian acting to stop great evil might still carry real guilt and would have to throw himself on the grace of God. Whatever we conclude about what he did, he took both Romans 13 and the suffering of his neighbors seriously, and he didn't pretend the choice was easy.

What Scripture doesn't allow is using Romans 13 to make Christians comfortable with evil. A ruler who wields the sword against the innocent hasn't escaped God's authority. He's still God's servant, and he will answer to his Master for what he did with the sword.

Only one Ruler has ever perfectly been God's servant for good. Jesus said He "did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many" (==Mark 10:45==). The word Paul uses for the ruler here, *diakonos*, is the same word he uses for Christ in ==Romans 15:8==. Every earthly government is temporary, and some are wicked. His isn't. "Of the increase of His government and peace there will be no end... to order it and establish it with judgment and justice" (==Isaiah 9:7==).

## Word Study
**Minister** is *diakonos* (διάκονος), a servant, someone who carries out the will of another. It's the word behind our word "deacon." Paul uses it for Phoebe, "a servant of the church" (==Romans 16:1==), for himself and Apollos (==1 Corinthians 3:5==), and for Jesus (==Romans 15:8==). Summary: in Paul's mind, the emperor had the same job description as a deacon. He was in his position to serve God and do good to others.

## Application
Most of us will never face a choice like Bonhoeffer's. But every Christian has to decide where his loyalty lies when a ruler, a boss, or a system asks him to go along with something wrong. Respect the authority God has put over you, and never let that authority talk you into doing evil or staying silent while the innocent are harmed. Open your mouth for the people who can't.
