## Commentary
For three chapters, Paul has wrestled with some of the most difficult questions surrounding God's covenant faithfulness, Israel's unbelief, the salvation of the Gentiles, and the eventual restoration of the Jewish people. And when he finally reaches the end of his explanation, he breaks into worship.

“Oh, the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are His judgments and His ways past finding out!”

Consider where Paul began this discussion. In ==Romans 9:1–3==, he confessed that he carried “great sorrow and continual grief” in his heart over Israel's unbelief. He was so burdened for his Jewish brethren that he wished himself accursed from Christ for their sake. The issue of Israel was not an abstract theological question for Paul. He was wrestling with the apparent contradiction between God's covenant promises and Israel's present rejection of her own Messiah. How could the people God had chosen, prepared, and entrusted with His revelation fail to recognize the One to whom all of it pointed? And what did their unbelief mean for the promises God had made to their fathers?

Over the course of these three chapters, Paul unfolds the extraordinary wisdom of God, and ends by erupting in praise.

What began with grief over Israel's unbelief has brought him to awe at the wisdom of God. Paul has come to recognize that even Israel's failure has not placed God's purposes beyond fulfillment. The Lord is accomplishing something far greater than Paul could have imagined, weaving together Israel's stumbling, Gentile salvation, and Israel's promised restoration in a way that reveals the extraordinary depth of His mercy.

Paul has received extraordinary revelation concerning the mystery of Israel and the Church, yet the more he understands, the more overwhelmed he becomes by how much greater God's wisdom is than his own. Revelation has not made Paul presumptuous about his own ability to understand God's plan. It has made him worship the one true living God who does the impossible.

Perhaps this is one of the more important lessons of Romans 9–11. The faithfulness of God is far more extraordinary than we often imagine. His wisdom is not limited by our understanding of how His promises should unfold, and His purposes are not defeated by the failures of the people He has called.
