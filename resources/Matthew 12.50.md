## Commentary
At first glance, Jesus' response might seem dismissive of His own family. But Jesus isn't dishonoring His mother or suggesting that natural family relationships are unimportant. In fact, while suffering on the cross, He would make provision for His mother's care (==John 19:26–27==). Rather, He is revealing that there is a relationship available to those who follow Him that is even more significant than the relationships established through natural birth.

This statement would have carried particular significance in the context of first-century Israel. The Jewish people understood themselves to be God's covenant people through their descent from Abraham. Their identity was deeply connected to their ancestry, their families, and their place within the nation of Israel. Yet Jesus is revealing that participation in the family He is gathering cannot be established through natural descent or religious heritage. Those who belong to Him are identified by their relationship to the Father and their willingness to believe in the Son He has sent.

But what does it mean to do the will of the Father and be in right relationship with Him?

Jesus makes a nearly identical statement earlier in Matthew's Gospel when He declares, “Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of My Father in heaven” (==Matthew 7:21==). In both passages, Jesus is distinguishing between those who profess an association with Him and those whose lives demonstrate genuine submission to God.

We need to be careful not to interpret this as though our obedience earns us a place in God's family. The New Testament makes it clear that we become children of God through faith in Jesus Christ (==Galatians 3:26==). But genuine faith produces a life that is increasingly submitted to the Father's will. Obedience isn't the means by which we purchase our relationship with God; it is the expression of a relationship that has already begun by faith in Jesus.

There's also something remarkable about the way Jesus speaks of His disciples. These weren't men who had demonstrated perfect obedience or extraordinary spiritual maturity. They were still learning who Jesus was, struggling with unbelief, and frequently misunderstanding His teaching. Yet Jesus points to them and says, “Here are My mother and My brothers!” They had responded to His invitation to follow Him, and in doing so, they had entered into a relationship that would transform every other aspect of their lives.

What Jesus is describing is more than belonging to a religious community. Through Him, we are brought into a genuine relationship with the Father and become members of His household (==Ephesians 2:19==). The Church, therefore, isn't simply a gathering of people who share similar beliefs or religious practices. We are brothers and sisters because we have been brought into the same family through Christ.
