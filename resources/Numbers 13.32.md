## Commentary
This is a monumental moment in the story of God's people. The fate of the nation rested on the words of these twelve men. And rather than stand on the faithfulness of God, and the testimony of what they had seen Him do in every impossible situation, the spies gave a "bad report." 

The Hebrew word translated "bad report" is *dibbah*, a word for a damaging or slanderous report. The spies had been sent to investigate the land, not to decide whether God's promise was worth believing. Their job was to report what they'd seen. Instead, their report became an argument against doing what God had commanded.

A few verses earlier they'd stood before Moses with a cluster of grapes so heavy it took two men and a pole to carry it (==Numbers 13:23==). "It truly flows with milk and honey," they admitted, "and this is its fruit" (==Numbers 13:27==). Now the same land is "a land that devours its inhabitants." The language in that phrase is difficult to translate. It may point to a land worn down by constant war, or to conditions that made survival difficult. Whatever they meant, the goal of those words was clear: convince Israel that going in would be a disaster.

And the people in the land got bigger with every telling. In ==Numbers 13:28== the spies singled out the descendants of Anak. Now "all the people whom we saw in it are men of great stature."

Joshua and Caleb walked the same land and saw the same walled cities. They called it "an exceedingly good land" and said, "If the LORD delights in us, then He will bring us into this land and give it to us" (==Numbers 14:7–8==). They didn't ignore the reality of the situation. They just refused to allow the facts on the ground to change the truth of what God had told them. 

While the other ten men claimed the land eats its people, Joshua and Caleb turned the image around: "they are our bread" (==Numbers 14:9==). All twelve men saw the same land. What separated them was whether they read what they saw through the lens of who God is or what their situation told them.

Unfortunately, the people of Israel believed the bad report. They wept through the night, complained against Moses and Aaron, and finally said, "Let us select a leader and return to Egypt" (==Numbers 14:1–4==). Fear gave way to open rebellion. They insisted their children would become victims in Canaan. God answered, "But your little ones, whom you said would be victims, I will bring in, and they shall know the land which you have despised" (==Numbers 14:31==). And the ten men who spread the *dibbah* died by plague before the Lord (==Numbers 14:36–37==).

The obstacles were real, and the spies weren't condemned for acknowledging them. They were condemned for letting what they saw decide what they believed about God's faithfulness. When your circumstances start to carry more authority than what God has said, it won't be long before you're questioning His goodness.

The spies had been sent to examine the land God was giving them. They came back and talked an entire generation out of receiving it.
