## Commentary
When Jesus compares a scribe instructed concerning the kingdom of heaven to a householder who brings out of his treasure “things new and old,” He is describing someone who understands the Scriptures in light of the revelation of God's kingdom through Christ.

To appreciate what Jesus is saying, we need to understand the role of a scribe in first-century Israel. Scribes were men devoted to studying, interpreting, and teaching the Scriptures. They were responsible for preserving Israel's understanding of God's Law and helping the people live according to His commandments. Yet throughout Matthew's Gospel, we frequently encounter scribes who, despite their extensive knowledge of Scripture, fail to recognize the One to whom the Scriptures are pointing.

Here, however, Jesus describes a different kind of scribe.

The Greek word translated “instructed” is *mathēteutheis*, which comes from the same root as the word for disciple. It carries the idea of being trained or made into a disciple. Jesus is describing a scribe who has become a disciple of the kingdom. This is significant because this kind of scribe's understanding of Scripture is no longer limited to what he has learned through religious instruction. He has encountered the Messiah, and that encounter has transformed the way he understands everything God has previously revealed.

The immediate context helps us understand what Jesus means by bringing out things new and old. Throughout ==Matthew 13==, Jesus has been revealing mysteries concerning the kingdom of heaven through a series of parables. He has described the kingdom as something that has already entered the world, yet whose full manifestation remains future. He has explained why some receive the word while others reject it, why righteousness and wickedness coexist during the present age, and how God's kingdom will ultimately triumph.

These were revelations concerning the kingdom that even the prophets had longed to understand (==Matthew 13:16–17==).

Yet none of these revelations contradicted what God had previously spoken. They were bringing greater understanding to the promises, prophecies, and purposes already revealed in the Old Testament.

This is what makes the imagery of the householder so significant. Jesus describes a man who possesses a treasury and is able to bring out both new and old things. He doesn't discard what is old because something new has arrived. Nor does he refuse what is new because he is satisfied with what he already possesses. He recognizes the value of both and understands how they belong together.

The same is true of someone who has been instructed concerning the kingdom of heaven.

Jesus had already declared, “Do not think that I came to destroy the Law or the Prophets. I did not come to destroy but to fulfill” (==Matthew 5:17==). His coming wasn't a rejection of everything God had previously revealed to Israel. It was the fulfillment of those promises and the unveiling of mysteries that had been concealed within God's redemptive purposes.

We see this demonstrated beautifully after the resurrection. When Jesus encountered two disciples on the road to Emmaus, He began with Moses and all the Prophets and explained the things concerning Himself throughout the Scriptures (==Luke 24:27==). He wasn't giving them an entirely new revelation disconnected from what God had previously spoken. He was opening their understanding to recognize what had been there all along.

This has important implications for the way we read and teach Scripture today.

We can't regard the Old Testament as something that has largely been replaced by the New. Israel's history, covenants, and prophetic promises shouldn't be treated as though their significance ended with the coming of Jesus. This is not how Jesus understood the Scriptures. The revelation of the kingdom doesn't eliminate what God previously promised. It brings those promises into clearer focus and reveals how they find their fulfillment in Christ.

At the same time, we shouldn't become so attached to our previous understanding of Scripture that we resist the greater revelation God has given through His Son. This was precisely the failure of many of the scribes and Pharisees. They possessed the Scriptures but resisted the One who came to fulfill them.

The faithful teacher must be able to hold both together. We need to understand the historical promises God made to Israel, the revelation of His kingdom through Jesus, and the continuing unfolding of His redemptive purposes through the Church and ultimately the restoration of all things (==Acts 3:19–21==; ==Romans 11:25–29==).

There's also a responsibility implied in the imagery Jesus uses. The householder doesn't just accumulate treasure. He brings it out. In the same way, those who have been entrusted with an understanding of God's Word are responsible for making that treasure available to others.

The more clearly we see Christ, the more fully we begin to understand the Scriptures that testified of Him long before His coming. And the more deeply we understand those Scriptures, the more clearly we recognize the faithfulness of God in everything He is accomplishing through His Son.

## Word Study
**Instructed** is the Greek *mathēteuō*, the verb form of the word “disciple.” Jesus is describing a scribe who has been “discipled” for the kingdom of heaven. A person can study the Bible for years and still miss what God is doing, as many of the scribes did. The teacher Jesus describes is a disciple first. Matthew uses the same verb at the end of his Gospel, when Jesus says, “Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations” (==Matthew 28:19==). The disciples were discipled first. Then they were sent to disciple others.

## Application
The next time you read an Old Testament chapter, ask what it shows you about Jesus. Then tell someone what you found.
