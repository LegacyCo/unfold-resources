## Commentary
“‘Go up to the mountains and bring wood and build the temple, that I may take pleasure in it and be glorified,’ says the LORD.”

God gives two reasons for rebuilding His house, and both of them are about Him. Not “build the temple so the nation stabilizes,” not “build it so the blessing returns”—though the surrounding verses show that blessing did hang on it (==Haggai 1:9-11==). The stated reasons are these: *that I may take pleasure in it, and be glorified.* The house exists for God’s enjoyment and God’s honor. When the Lord has His house, He has what His heart desires; when the Lord has His house, His name is glorified in the earth.

This reframes the question of why God’s people build at all. The remnant had stalled for eighteen years, and their reasoning was practical—opposition, discouragement, the press of their own affairs (==Ezra 4:4-5==; ==Haggai 1:2==). Behind the stall was an assumption: the building project was one priority among many, and it could wait. Verse 8 corrects the assumption at its root. The house of God is not one item on the community’s agenda; it is the thing God Himself wants, for His own pleasure. That has been His stated desire since Sinai—“let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them” (==Exodus 25:8==)—and it remains His desire now that the house is made of people rather than cedar and stone (==Ephesians 2:21-22==).

It also clarifies what to pray. Most prayer is occupied with what we need God to do; this verse points to a different question: “God, what do *You* want?” A people who build for God’s pleasure—who order their common life around what He delights in rather than what they prefer—are giving God the desire of His heart. And Haggai’s account shows how God responds to that shift: the moment the people aligned their priorities with His, the word came—“I am with you” (==Haggai 1:13==), and blessing was declared over them (==Haggai 2:19==).
