## Commentary
“What shall we say then? Is the law sin? Certainly not! On the contrary, I would not have known sin except through the law. For I would not have known covetousness unless the law had said, ‘You shall not covet.’”

Paul could have illustrated his point with any of the commandments. He picked the tenth. Of the Ten Commandments, the first nine can all be measured from the outside. A man can demonstrate that he hasn't murdered, hasn't stolen, hasn't committed adultery, hasn't perjured himself; witnesses can vouch for his Sabbaths and his honored parents. The tenth is different. “You shall not covet” is the one command aimed entirely at the interior—not at what the hands take but at what the heart wants. The commandment sits inside the Decalogue as proof that God was never only regulating behavior; He was always after the desires underneath it (==1 Samuel 16:7==).

That is why this commandment, of all of them, was the one that found Paul. By every external measure he was untouchable—“concerning the righteousness which is in the law, blameless” (==Philippians 3:6==). A man like that could stand before nine commandments with his record intact. The tenth found its way into the man's heart and found sin very much alive there (==Romans 7:8-9==). The same thing happened to the rich young ruler: he had kept the commandments from his youth, and Jesus, with one sentence about his possessions, put a finger on the desire that the man's spotless record had concealed (==Mark 10:20-22==). Jesus taught the whole law this way—murder traced back to anger, adultery traced back to lust (==Matthew 5:21-22==; ==Matthew 5:27-28==)—but the tenth commandment had been making that point since Mount Sinai. The law is not sin; the law is the light that reaches the one room external religion can never clean. And a man who has seen that place in his own heart stops defending his record and starts asking for a new heart (==Psalm 51:10==; ==Ezekiel 36:26==).
