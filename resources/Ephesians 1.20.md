## Commentary
“…which He worked in Christ when He raised Him from the dead and seated Him at His right hand in the heavenly places.”

Where is Jesus right now? Not standing outside an empty tomb in Jerusalem celebrating His resurrection. Raised—and *seated*, at the right hand of the Father, far above all principality and power and might and dominion and every name that is named (==Ephesians 1:21==). The resurrection was not the end of the story; it was the doorway to the enthronement. Heaven has received Him as King.

The question this verse leaves with every believer is whether we have. In a monarchy, authority transfers the moment the old king dies—but the coronation comes later. The coronation is when the *people* come out of mourning and acknowledge the new king’s lordship. Between the two events, a kingdom can exist in a strange condition: a king with all authority on the throne, and a people still living as though the old order still existed. Many believers live in that space. They genuinely believe in the death, burial, and resurrection of Jesus—they cling to the risen Savior—but they have never acknowledged the enthroned King. Their desires still govern their decisions; their personality still governs their reactions; they remain, functionally, on the throne of their own lives. It is possible to believe in the resurrection and still rule your own kingdom.

That divided condition explains a great deal of Christian experience. A life with two claimants to one throne cannot have peace—“a house divided against itself cannot stand” (==Mark 3:25==). Believers who cannot find rest, victory over sin, or authority against the enemy are often not lacking faith in the resurrection; they are lacking a coronation. The remedy is what Mary was sent to announce: not only “He is risen,” but “I am ascending to My Father” (==John 17:5==; ==John 20:17==). The gospel's announcement runs all the way to the throne (==Acts 2:36==). Cling to Him—but cling to Him where He is: seated, crowned, and Lord.
