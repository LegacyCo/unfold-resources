## Commentary
In the summer of 386, a thirty-one-year-old teacher of rhetoric named Augustine sat weeping in a garden in Milan. He believed the gospel was true, but he couldn't let go of the life he was living. Then he heard a child's voice from a nearby house repeating, "Take up and read." He went back to where his friend Alypius was sitting, picked up the copy of Paul's letters he'd left there, and read the first lines his eyes fell on: "not in revelry and drunkenness, not in lewdness and lust, not in strife and envy. But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh, to fulfill its lusts" (==Romans 13:13–14==). He later wrote that he didn't want to read any further, and he didn't need to. The following Easter, Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, baptized him. Augustine went on to become the bishop of Hippo in North Africa and wrote *Confessions* and *The City of God*, and few people outside the pages of Scripture have shaped the church's thinking more. All of that started with the two verses he read at the end of Romans 13.

In this verse, Paul is describing someone getting dressed. Two verses earlier he wrote, "The night is far spent, the day is at hand. Therefore let us cast off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armor of light" (==Romans 13:12==). When the sun comes up, you take off what you slept in and dress for the day. Verse 13 describes the night clothes: revelry and drunkenness, lewdness and lust, strife and envy. The last pair sits on the same list as drunkenness and sexual sin. Quarreling and jealousy are night clothes too, and the believers in Rome needed to hear it. In the very next chapter Paul has to settle their arguments over food and holy days (==Romans 14:1–6==).

Then, where we might expect a new set of rules, Paul names a Person. Put on...the Lord Jesus Christ. The thing that replaces the old life is Jesus Himself, wrapped around you so closely that people see Him when they look at you.

Paul told the Galatians they had already done this. "For as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ" (==Galatians 3:27==). The Roman believers had been baptized too (==Romans 6:3–4==). So why tell them to put on what they're already wearing? Because both things are true. In Christ you've been clothed, once and for all. And every morning you get up and live dressed in what you've been given.

"Make no provision for the flesh" means don't plan ahead for it. Most sin doesn't ambush us. It sets an appointment. We leave the door open, keep the bottle in the cabinet, save the number we said we'd delete, and rehearse the argument on the drive home so we're ready to win it. By the time the temptation arrives, we've already set a place for it at the table. Paul says to stop making those arrangements. Don't give the flesh a schedule, a budget, or a back door.

## Word Study
**Provision** is *pronoia* (πρόνοια), forethought, planning ahead. It shows up only one other time in the New Testament. When the lawyer Tertullus opened his case against Paul before the governor Felix, he flattered him by saying, "prosperity is being brought to this nation by your foresight" (==Acts 24:2==). *Pronoia* is the careful planning a governor gives to running a province. Summary: the flesh will gladly take that kind of attention. Paul says not to give it any.

## Application
Tonight, look ahead at tomorrow and ask where you've already made room for the flesh. Maybe it's a conversation you're planning to win, a habit you've left an easy path to, or a place you know you shouldn't go. Undo that one arrangement before you go to bed. Then when you wake up, before anything else, ask Jesus to clothe you with Himself for the day.
