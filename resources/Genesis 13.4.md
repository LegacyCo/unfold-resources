## Commentary
“…to the place of the altar which he had made there at first. And there Abram called on the name of the LORD.”

The Abram who came back to Canaan is a different man from the Abram who went down to Egypt. Going to Egypt in the famine was a mistake—he left the land God had called him to and moved out of fear, scheming and lying to protect himself (==Genesis 12:11-13==). Scripture records no altar in Egypt. Abram built an altar at Shechem when the LORD appeared to him (==Genesis 12:7==), and another between Bethel and Ai, where he called on the name of the LORD (==Genesis 12:8==). In Egypt there is no altar, no calling on the name, no word from God—only silence, self-preservation, and trouble. The altars mark where Abram walked with God; Egypt is the gap in the record.

So when Abram realizes he is out of God’s will, he retraces his steps to “the place of the altar which he had made there at first,” and there he calls on the name of the LORD again. Restoration means going back to where you left off with God. This is the same instruction the risen Jesus gives a church that had slipped: “Remember therefore from where you have fallen; repent and do the first works” (==Revelation 2:5==). 

The return produced something in Abram. The man who walks away from that altar is no longer scheming but preferring others, no longer grasping but giving Lot the first choice of the land (==Genesis 13:9==)—no longer moving from fear and worry, but living by faith. This is a defining quality of the righteous. It is not that they never act selfishly, or never feel fear when famine comes. What sets the righteous apart is what they do with their failures—they allow their mistakes to mature them. “For a righteous man may fall seven times and rise again” (==Proverbs 24:16==). Peter and Judas both failed Jesus on the same night. Peter took his failure to the cross, trusting that there was grace enough for forgiveness and power enough for restoration (==Luke 22:32==). Judas’s mistake killed him; Peter’s mistake matured him. Abram did not allow Egypt to define him. He allowed Egypt to mature him.

## Word Study
**Called** is the Hebrew *qara* (קָרָא)—to call out, cry out, proclaim, summon. To “call on the name of the LORD” is to invoke Him personally—worship and dependence expressed out loud. The phrase first appears in the days of Seth, when “men began to call on the name of the LORD” (==Genesis 4:26==); it marks Abram’s altars (==Genesis 12:8==); and it carries all the way into the gospel: “whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved” (==Joel 2:32==; ==Romans 10:13==). Summary: *qara* is the vocabulary of dependence—Abram’s recovery from Egypt begins the moment he opens his mouth toward God again.

## Application
The way back from failure is the way back to the altar. When you recognize you have stepped out of God’s will, return to the place where you last met with Him—the surrendered place of worship and calling on His name—rather than camping in shame over the detour. Be specific about it the way Abram was: identify the practice you abandoned when you drifted—the prayer, the fellowship, the obedience—and take up that first work again. The righteous are not the ones who never go down to Egypt. They are the ones who come back changed.
