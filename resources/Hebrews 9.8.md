## Commentary
The temple was proof that God desired to be with His people. Think about the astonishing condescension of it: the God of the universe builds a two-room house in a small town in Israel so He can dwell in the middle of a tiny nation of former slaves. But the temple also revealed a second, more painful fact: while God wanted to be with man, man could not come to God. The building wasn’t the answer to God’s desire. It was foreshadowing.

Everything hinged on two things the temple contained: the veil and the blood. The veil separated God and man. The blood allowed God and man to be together. The priests served in the outer room every day, but into the Holy of Holies—the place of God’s presence—only the high priest could go, only once a year, and “not without blood” (==Hebrews 9:7==). The writer of Hebrews tells us the Holy Spirit was teaching something through that architecture: “the way into the Holiest of All was not yet made manifest while the first tabernacle was still standing.”

Imagine being one of those priests. Every day you walk into the outer room. Every day you see the veil hanging there, and you know the presence of God is on the other side—and there is nothing you can do to enter in. Worship in the temple revealed both man’s longing to be with his God *and* his complete inability to do anything about it. Love calls the sinner near; righteousness keeps him at a distance. The whole system groaned with that tension for centuries.

Then the true High Priest came. At the moment Jesus died, “the veil of the temple was torn in two from top to bottom” (==Matthew 27:51==)—from top to bottom, because God tore it, not man. Hebrews tells us the veil pointed to something all along: we now have “boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus, by a new and living way which He consecrated for us, through the veil, that is, His flesh” (==Hebrews 10:19-20==). The two things the temple always required—the veil and the blood—both turned out to be Jesus. His body was the veil; His blood was the entrance. The tension the priests carried every day for centuries has been resolved. The way in is open, and it stays open.

## Application
The veil is gone—but many believers keep sewing it back up. We delay prayer until we “feel worthy” again. We do a little penance before we approach. We treat the throne like the Holy of Holies still has a once-a-year policy. Hebrews draws the opposite conclusion: “Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (==Hebrews 4:16==). Notice when that boldness is for: your *time of need*—which usually means right after you’ve blown it. The moment you most want to hide behind a veil is the exact moment the torn one invites you in. So practice going straight in this week: no probation period, no self-imposed waiting room. The blood already handled the entrance requirements.
