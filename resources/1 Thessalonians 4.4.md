## Commentary
Paul writes that each of us should “know how to possess his own vessel in sanctification and honor, not in passion of lust.” Notice the assumption inside the command: desire can be taught. Scripture’s goal for desire is not extinction but education.

God is not anti-beauty, anti-desire, or anti-sex. He created these things and placed them within the safety of covenant for our enjoyment. The problem is never desire itself; the problem is desire detached from love. That is what lust is—desire without love, desire turned in on itself. Love asks, “How can I honor you?” Lust asks, “How can I use you?” Love sees a person; lust sees a body. Love protects; lust consumes. Love gives; lust takes. Love moves toward honor and communion; lust moves toward possession.

Lust distorts the person being looked at, reducing an image-bearer of God to an object for consumption. But it also distorts the one looking. We were not created to be ruled by impulse and appetite, nor to live divided between a public self and a private self—the double-minded life that James calls “unstable in all its ways” (==James 1:8==). We were created to bear the image of God in bodies that are temples, not tools for self-gratification (==1 Corinthians 6:19-20==).

Holiness, then, is not starving desire; it is desire aimed at its true target. The goal of the gospel is not merely to stop looking at the wrong thing. It is to become the kind of person who sees rightly—whose desire has been so healed by love that beauty can be honored without being consumed.

## Application
When desire rises, don’t just suppress it—interrogate it with love’s question. In the moment, ask: “How can I honor this person?” instead of “What do I want?” That one substitution reframes everything: a body becomes a person, an object becomes an image-bearer, and you become someone capable of love rather than a consumer driven by appetite.

Examine where you are living divided—one self in public, another in private. Double-mindedness doesn’t stay contained; it makes you unstable in all your ways. Ask God not merely to restrain your desires but to educate them: to teach them what they were made for. The aim is not a person with no appetite. It is a person whose desire has learned to love—and that person is free in a way mere self-control never achieves.
