## Commentary
“He made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might *become* the righteousness of God in Him.”

The exchange the gospel promises is this: your sin for God’s righteousness.

Paul didn’t write that sentence haphazardly. He constructed it like a scale—two sides, perfectly balanced. On one side: He who knew no sin was made sin. On the other: we, who never knew righteousness, become the righteousness of God. Whatever weight you place on one side, the grammar places the equal amount on the other. Paul doesn’t give you the option of weighing them differently.

So let’s weigh the first side. When Jesus died on the cross, did He take your sin—all of it? Of course He did. He forgave “all our trespasses, having wiped out the handwriting of requirements that was against us… having nailed it to the cross” (==Colossians 2:13-14==). “The blood of Jesus Christ His Son cleanses us from *all* sin” (==1 John 1:7==). You believe that with everything in you. In all my years as a pastor, I have never once heard a Christian say, “Jesus bore my sin—well, positionally. Mostly on paper. A few of the bigger ones are still under review.” You can’t even say it with a straight face.

Now watch what happens when you hesitate over the second side of the scale. The moment you say, “Righteous, sure—but not as righteous as Jesus,” you just tipped a scale that Paul balanced. If you discount your half of the verse, you’re required to discount His as well: He was made… *most* of my sin. And you would never say that. You would never stand at the foot of the cross and call it a partial payment.

Paul chose his words like a man who knew they’d be doubted. He didn’t write that Jesus was made a *sinner*—He never was. He was made *sin*: the whole weight, the full liability, laid on Him. And on your side of the scale, Paul doesn’t say you become *righteous*—an adjective you could line up next to other adjectives. Adjectives live on a sliding scale—clean, cleaner, cleanest; good, better, best—always looking sideways at the competition. But a noun doesn’t compare itself. A noun just *is*. And Paul says you become *righteousness*—the noun. The thing itself. And not some human grade of it, either: the righteousness *of God*. There is no higher righteousness in existence. You cannot be more righteous than God’s own righteousness.

Then there’s the verb. The Greek word we translate “become” is *ginomai*—the word for coming into existence. It’s a birth word. Paul uses this very verb when he writes that Jesus was “born (ginomai) of a woman, born under the law” (==Galatians 4:4==). Hold those two verses side by side and let the symmetry do its work: He was born into your condition so that you could be born into His. Nobody earns a birth. Nobody achieves one, either. And nobody un-births themselves on a bad week.

And the last two words of Paul’s sentence—*in Him*—are the hinge your identity swings on. This righteousness is never yours at a distance, like a possession you carry away from the Giver. It’s yours by union: “he who is joined to the Lord is one spirit with Him” (==1 Corinthians 6:17==). You must be born again (==John 3:3==)—and what gets born, this time, is the righteousness of God.

If you are in Christ, you are as righteous as He is. To be clear about what that’s *not* saying: it’s not saying you’re perfect. I’m not. Paul wasn’t. What it’s saying is that in Christ, His righteousness is now fully yours.

## Word Study
- *become* : **ginomai** (Greek) — to come into being, to be born; origin language, not achievement language. The most famous use in the New Testament: “the Word *became* (ginomai) flesh and dwelt among us” (==John 1:14==). The same verb that brought God into our condition brings us into His righteousness—and see ==Galatians 4:4==, where Jesus is “born (ginomai) of a woman.”
- *righteousness* : **dikaiosynē** (Greek) — the noun, not the adjective. Greek has a perfectly good adjective, *dikaios* (“righteous”), and Paul uses it elsewhere—but not here. Here he chooses the noun: the quality itself, the very right-standing of God that the gospel reveals (==Romans 1:17==).

Summary: the verb says you were born into it; the noun says it doesn’t come in degrees. The Word became flesh so that, in Him, flesh could become righteousness.
