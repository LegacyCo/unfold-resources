## Commentary
Matthew has barely finished telling us about one outsider approaching Jesus before another one appears.

First came the leper. Now comes a Roman centurion.

The significance of the sequence is easy to miss. Matthew has just recorded Jesus touching a man whose condition made him ceremonially unclean (==Matthew 8:2–3==). Immediately afterward, Jesus enters Capernaum and is approached by a Gentile military officer.

A centurion was an officer in the Roman army, ordinarily responsible for about one hundred soldiers. But more importantly for Matthew’s Jewish audience, this man was a Gentile. He was outside Israel’s covenant identity. Peter would later describe the social and religious boundary between Jew and Gentile: “You know how unlawful it is for a Jewish man to keep company with or go to one of another nation” (==Acts 10:28==).

So Matthew places two people beside one another who, for very different reasons, lived on the other side of Israel’s boundaries.

The leper was an insider whose uncleanness had pushed him outside.

The centurion was an outsider who had never been inside.

And _both of them come to Jesus._

That is what Matthew wants us to see.

This becomes even more significant when we remember what has just happened. Jesus has come down from the mountain after announcing the nature of His kingdom in the Sermon on the Mount (==Matthew 5–7==). Now Matthew begins showing us what that kingdom looks like when it arrives among actual people.

And the first people we see approaching the King are the people everyone else would have kept at a distance.

The leper comes saying, “Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean” (==Matthew 8:2==). The centurion comes saying, “Lord, my servant is lying at home paralyzed, dreadfully tormented” (==Matthew 8:6==).

Notice that both men call Jesus _Lord._

Their access to Jesus is not based on their social standing, ritual condition, ethnicity, or proximity to Israel and its covenant. It's based upon their recognition of who stands before them.

In fact, the centurion story pushes the point even further. Jesus will eventually marvel at this Gentile’s faith and declare, “I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel!” (==Matthew 8:10==). Then He immediately speaks of people coming “from east and west” to sit with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom (==Matthew 8:11==).

The outsider standing before Jesus becomes a picture of the outsiders who are yet to come.

Matthew is showing us something about the kingdom before he ever explains it explicitly: _the boundaries are being redrawn around Jesus._

The unclean come near. The Gentiles come near. The people who had been told, for one reason or another, _You cannot come any closer,_ discover that Jesus is not moving away from them. He is the One they can approach.
