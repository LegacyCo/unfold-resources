## Commentary
In this verse, Jesus continues preparing His disciples for the opposition they will encounter as they proclaim the kingdom of God. He tells them that when they are persecuted in one city, they should flee to another. Then He makes a statement that has generated considerable discussion among biblical scholars: "For assuredly, I say to you, you will not have gone through the cities of Israel before the Son of Man comes."

There are two important things to understand in this verse.

First, Jesus gives His disciples permission to flee persecution. This is significant because, throughout this passage, He has been preparing them to endure suffering for His name. Yet He does not suggest that faithfulness requires them to remain in every dangerous situation or deliberately expose themselves to unnecessary harm.

There will be times when obedience requires us to stand our ground and endure opposition. But there will also be times when wisdom requires us to leave and continue the work elsewhere. Jesus is not calling His disciples to pursue suffering for its own sake. Their assignment is to proclaim the kingdom, and if persecution prevents them from doing so in one place, they are free to carry the message somewhere else.

We see this demonstrated throughout the book of Acts. When persecution broke out against the church in Jerusalem, believers were scattered throughout Judea and Samaria. But rather than abandoning their mission, "those who were scattered went everywhere preaching the word" (==Acts 8:4==).

Paul himself followed this same pattern. Shortly after his conversion, when a group of Jews plotted to kill him in Damascus, the disciples helped him escape by lowering him in a basket through an opening in the city wall under the cover of darkness (==Acts 9:23–25==). Later, when Paul and Barnabas learned of a plot to mistreat and stone them in Iconium, they fled to the neighboring cities of Lystra and Derbe, where they continued preaching the gospel (==Acts 14:5–7==).

Paul was certainly not afraid of suffering. His ministry was marked by imprisonments, beatings, and tremendous opposition. Yet he understood that there was a difference between willingly enduring suffering when obedience required it and unnecessarily exposing himself to danger when another opportunity to continue the work was available.

Jesus never instructed His disciples to pursue persecution or prove their devotion by remaining in dangerous situations. Their calling was to faithfully proclaim the kingdom. Sometimes that required remaining in the face of opposition, and other times it meant leaving one place to continue their assignment somewhere else. Their circumstances might change, but their calling remained the same.

The second part of the verse is more difficult to interpret. What does Jesus mean when He says the disciples will not finish going through the cities of Israel before the Son of Man comes?

There are several interpretations worth considering.

Some understand this as a reference to the second coming of Christ at the end of the age. According to this interpretation, Jesus is describing an ongoing mission to Israel that will continue until His final return. The difficulty with this view is that Jesus appears to be speaking directly to the Twelve about events they themselves will experience.

Others understand His words as a reference to the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. In this interpretation, the coming of the Son of Man describes the vindication of Jesus and the judgment that would come upon Jerusalem within the lifetime of that generation. This interpretation draws support from Jesus' later warnings concerning Jerusalem and the temple in ==Matthew 24:1–34==.

Another compelling interpretation connects Jesus' statement to His resurrection, ascension, and exaltation as the Son of Man.

To understand this view, we need to return to ==Daniel 7:13–14==, where Daniel sees "One like the Son of Man" coming with the clouds of heaven. What is often overlooked is the direction of this movement described in Daniel 7. The Son of Man is not descending *from* heaven *to* earth (ie; the second coming). He is approaching the Ancient of Days, where He is given dominion, glory, and an everlasting kingdom.

In other words, Daniel's vision describes the heavenly ascension and enthronement of the Son of Man.

Jesus repeatedly identifies Himself with this figure, including when He stands before the high priest in ==Matthew 26:64== and declares that they will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming on the clouds of heaven.

If this is what Jesus has in mind in Matthew 10:23, He is telling the disciples that they will not complete their mission throughout Israel before He is vindicated through His resurrection and exalted to the place of authority.

This interpretation fits the progression of Matthew's Gospel. In ==Matthew 10:5–6==, Jesus specifically instructs the disciples not to go to the Gentiles or the Samaritans, but to the lost sheep of Israel. Their initial mission is deliberately limited. Yet after His resurrection, Jesus declares, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth," and commissions them to make disciples of _all nations_ (==Matthew 28:18–19==).

The mission has expanded because the risen Christ has been revealed as the One who possesses authority over every nation.

Although faithful interpreters disagree about precisely which event Jesus is referring to, we should not miss the encouragement He is giving His disciples. Their work is unfolding within a much larger story. They are being sent into cities where they will encounter rejection, hostility, and suffering, but none of these things will prevent God from accomplishing what He has promised through His Son.
