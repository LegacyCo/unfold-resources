## Commentary
After the fat of the sacrifice was burned on the altar, the priest took his portion of the meat home to share with his family. The worshipper took the rest for a festive meal with family and friends. This offering meat had to be eaten either the same day or the day after. The meal was intended to be shared with others. It was not an individualistic act. The law designed it as a communal act since one could not eat an 800lb bull in one day alone. As John Mark Hicks writes in "Come to the Table," "God never intended humanity to eat alone. Rather, God eats with them and the family of God surrounds them."

When a sacrifice was made at the altar there were always three things present:
	1. the worshipper
	2. the One being worshipped
	3. the sacrifice
The worshipper offers up the sacrifice in order to recieve a blessing from the One being worshipped. After the sacrifice the worshipper would eat the meat of the sacrifice. They would invite friends and family, and God Himself would be with them. The meal confirmed the covenant made on the altar.
