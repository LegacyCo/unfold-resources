## Commentary
“…for I am a stranger with You, a sojourner, as all my fathers were.”

David, a king in his own land, calls himself a stranger and a sojourner—a temporary resident, a guest. He is describing the posture of everyone who belongs to God: this world is not home. Paul says the same to the churches. “Our citizenship is in heaven, from which we also eagerly wait for the Savior” (==Philippians 3:20==). Believers are *presently* “seated… together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (==Ephesians 2:6==) even while their feet are on the earth, and because they have been raised with Christ they are to “seek those things which are above” (==Colossians 3:1==). The consistent point is that this place is not your home; you are a visitor.

Anyone who has lived in a foreign country knows the feeling. When our family lived in Iraq it was clear we were guests in a foreign land. We lived there, but our citizenship was elsewhere—our culture, our language, our values, what felt normal and what felt foreign, all of it came from the land that had formed us, not the land where we were spending our time. We were in Iraq on assignment, but Iraq did not define the culture of our home; we were there to bring the culture of the Kingdom of God into that place. That is the believer's relationship to the world: in it, not of it (==John 17:14-16==), refusing to love the world or the things in it (==1 John 2:15==), and functioning as salt and light in a place that is not home (==Matthew 5:13-14==). The church is God's agent of representation and change in the world—assigned to transform what is being built for Babylon into foundation stones for Jerusalem. Augustine declared these words over new converts before they took communion: “Your hope must not be in this world, but in heaven. Your faith must be firmly directed toward God. What you do not see here, but believe, you will see there, in heaven, where you will rejoice without end.”

## Word Study
**Stranger** is the Hebrew *ger* (גֵּר), a resident alien—someone living in a land not his own—and **sojourner** is *toshab* (תּוֹשָׁב), a temporary dweller, a tenant. The pair appears together when Abraham describes himself to the Hittites while buying a grave in a land God had promised him (==Genesis 23:4==), and when God reminds Israel that the land is His: “you are strangers and sojourners with Me” (==Leviticus 25:23==). Peter carries both words into the church: “Beloved, I beg you as sojourners and pilgrims” (==1 Peter 2:11==). Summary: God's people have always held their residence in this world loosely, because their citizenship was formed somewhere else.

## Application
Live as someone on assignment. Let the culture of the kingdom—not the culture of the place you happen to live—set what feels normal to you: how you speak, what you value, how you spend, how you treat people. Where the world's way has become your default, treat it as a foreign custom you picked up and are laying down. Then do what an ambassador does: bring the culture of home into the place you have been sent.
