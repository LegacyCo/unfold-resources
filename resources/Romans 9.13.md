## Commentary
“Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated.”

This is one of those statements in Scripture that can be difficult to reconcile with what we know about the nature and character of God. How can the God who is love declare that He hates someone, particularly when Paul has just explained that God's choice was made before either Jacob or Esau had been born or had done anything good or evil?

To understand what Paul is saying, we need to consider both the original context of this statement and the argument Paul is making in Romans 9.

First, Paul is quoting from ==Malachi 1:2–3==, a prophecy written centuries after Jacob and Esau had died. In Malachi's day, the people of Israel were questioning God's love for them. They had returned from exile, but their circumstances were difficult, and they struggled to understand how God could claim to love them when their lives looked the way they did.

God answers their question by reminding them of His choice of Jacob over Esau. Both were sons of Isaac, both were descendants of Abraham, and both had the same claim to the family inheritance by natural birth. In fact, Esau was the firstborn and, according to the customs of the ancient world, would ordinarily have received the greater inheritance. Yet God chose Jacob to carry forward the covenant promise.

By the time Malachi was written, Jacob and Esau also represented the nations that had descended from them: Israel and Edom. God was reminding Israel of His covenant faithfulness by contrasting their history with that of Edom, whose land had experienced devastation and judgment.

This context is important for understanding the language Paul uses here in Romans 9.

The Greek word translated "hated" is _miseō_, which can describe hatred but can also express rejection or preference in a comparison. We see something similar in ==Genesis 29:30–31==, where Jacob is said to love Rachel more than Leah, and Leah is subsequently described as being "unloved" or "hated," depending on the translation. The point is not necessarily that Jacob felt personal hostility toward Leah, but that Rachel was preferred over her.

Jesus uses this same language in ==Luke 14:26== when He declares, "If anyone comes to Me and does not hate (_miseō_) his father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he cannot be My disciple." Clearly, Jesus is not commanding His followers to despise their families. In ==Matthew 10:37==, He tells us His point plainly: "He who loves father or mother more than Me is not worthy of Me." The issue is not hatred in the sense of hostility, but the ordering of our loves and loyalties. Jesus is insisting that our allegiance to Him must take precedence over every other relationship, even those we rightly love and cherish.

This helps us understand the language Paul borrows from Malachi. God's declaration that He loved Jacob and hated Esau should not automatically be understood as a statement of personal affection toward one brother and emotional hostility toward the other. The language can communicate a distinction in preference, choice, and covenant allegiance. In this case, God chose Jacob rather than Esau as the one through whom His covenant promise would continue.

However, we should not reduce God's statement in Malachi to simply meaning that He loved Esau a little less. The context also speaks of God's judgment upon Edom. The language describes a real distinction between those who had been chosen to carry the covenant promise and those who had not, as well as the historical consequences that followed.

The biblical scholar Matthew P. Harmon makes an important observation about this passage: "God's love is covenantal, a gratuitously established relationship that should elicit love in return." In other words, when God speaks of His love for Jacob, He is not simply describing an emotion He felt toward one brother rather than another. He is describing a covenant relationship that He freely chose to establish. His love was demonstrated in His commitment to Jacob and his descendants, and in His faithfulness to the promises He had made.

It is important to recognize that Paul's primary argument is not about whether God felt affection for one infant and hatred toward another. He is explaining how God's covenant purposes have always operated according to His own sovereign choice.

This becomes even clearer when we consider what Paul says immediately before this verse. While Rebekah was still pregnant, God told her, "The older shall serve the younger" (==Romans 9:12==). That statement comes from ==Genesis 25:23==, where God explains that there are _two nations_ in her womb and that the older will serve the younger.

God was announcing the direction His redemptive plan would take before either child had done anything to earn or disqualify himself from that role.

And this is what makes the example of Jacob especially significant. Jacob was hardly a model of moral superiority. His story is filled with deception, manipulation, fear, and attempts to secure God's promises through his own ingenuity. If the covenant had depended upon Jacob's character or personal righteousness, there would have been plenty of reasons to choose someone else.

But God's choice did not originate in Jacob's worthiness. It originated in God's purpose.

This brings us back to the question Paul has been addressing since the beginning of the chapter. Had God's promises to Israel failed because so many of Abraham's descendants had rejected Jesus? Paul's answer is no. From the beginning, God had never promised that every physical descendant of Abraham would automatically inherit the covenant blessings. He had always been working through His own gracious initiative to accomplish His redemptive purposes.

We should also be careful not to assume that God's decision to establish His covenant through Jacob meant that Esau was automatically condemned to eternal separation from Him.

John Wesley, commenting on this passage, wrote, "Nor does it relate to the eternal state either of them or their posterity." Wesley understood Paul's argument to concern God's choice of the family through whom His covenant promises would be fulfilled, rather than a declaration about the eternal destiny of either brother or their descendants.

Not all Christian interpreters agree with Wesley's conclusion, particularly regarding the relationship between God's election and individual salvation. Nevertheless, his observation highlights an important distinction between what the Old Testament passages explicitly say about God's covenant purposes and the larger theological questions surrounding salvation.

What is unmistakable, however, is Paul's insistence that God's purposes do not depend upon human achievement, natural advantages, or inherited privilege. As he explained in the preceding verses, God's purpose in election stands "not of works but of Him who calls" (==Romans 9:11==).

## Application
We often approach our relationship with God as though His willingness to work in and through us ultimately depends upon our ability to make ourselves worthy of His attention. We look at our weaknesses, our failures, the circumstances of our birth, or the mistakes of our past and imagine that these things must somehow limit what God can accomplish in our lives.

But Jacob's story reminds us that God has never been dependent upon the strength or worthiness of the people He calls. His purposes are established by His own faithfulness.

This does not mean our choices and obedience are not important. Jacob's life certainly demonstrates that our actions have consequences. But it does mean that God's grace precedes our achievements, and His faithfulness is greater than our failures.

The God who chose to carry His promise through a deeply flawed man named Jacob is the same God who has brought us into His family through Jesus Christ. We have not been included because of our natural advantages, our religious achievements, or our superior moral character. We have been included because of His grace.

And the appropriate response to that grace is not pride in having been chosen, but humility and gratitude toward the One who has welcomed us into His promise.
