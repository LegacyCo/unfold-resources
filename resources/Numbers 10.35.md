## Commentary
There is something beautiful about the prayer Moses prays whenever the ark of the covenant sets out before the people of Israel. As they prepare to journey into the wilderness, Moses cries out, "Rise up, O LORD! Let Your enemies be scattered, and let those who hate You flee before You!"

Remember what the ark represented. It was the place where God had promised to meet with His people, the visible representation of His covenant presence among them (==Exodus 25:21–22==). According to ==Numbers 10:33==, the ark went before Israel during this stage of their journey to seek out a resting place for them. And each time it set out, Moses would offer this prayer.

Notice what Moses does not pray. He does not ask God to make Israel stronger than its enemies, to give them superior military strategies, or to ensure that their armies are adequately prepared for whatever opposition they might encounter. Instead, he asks God Himself to arise.

Moses understood that their security was not ultimately found in the size of their army, the skill of their soldiers, or their ability to overcome the nations standing between them and the Promised Land. Their confidence was in the presence of God among them.

When Moses prayed, "Rise up, O LORD," he was not suggesting that God had been inactive or needed to be awakened. This was a poetic appeal for God to manifest His presence and power on behalf of His people. The language pictures God rising to confront those who oppose *Him*, with the result that *His* enemies are scattered before *Him*.

There is also significance in the way Moses identifies Israel's enemies. He doesn't say, "Let _our_ enemies be scattered." He says, "Let _Your_ enemies be scattered." Israel was not moving through the wilderness to accomplish its own ambitions. They were following the God who had called them, and their journey was part of His covenant purposes. Those who opposed what God was accomplishing through His people were ultimately opposing Him.

David would later use almost identical language in ==Psalm 68:1==: "Let God arise, let His enemies be scattered; let those also who hate Him flee before Him." The psalm celebrates God's victorious presence among His people and His power to deliver, protect, and establish them.

But I think there is an important application here for us as new covenant believers.

We are not Israel preparing to conquer Canaan, and we should be careful not to assume that every person who opposes us is therefore an enemy of God. Jesus explicitly commands us to love our enemies and pray for those who persecute us (==Matthew 5:44==). The enemies we face in our spiritual lives are not primarily other people. Paul reminds us that "we do not wrestle against flesh and blood" (==Ephesians 6:12==).

Nevertheless, we have been called to live in dependence upon the same God who went before Israel.

There are battles we encounter that cannot be overcome through our own strength, discipline, wisdom, or determination. We contend with temptation, deception, fear, spiritual opposition, and circumstances that seem far beyond our ability to manage. And while we are certainly called to resist the devil, stand firm in faith, and faithfully exercise the authority we have been given in Christ, our confidence must remain in the Lord Himself.

I wonder how differently we would approach the challenges before us if we learned to pray as Moses did.

Before entering a difficult conversation, "Rise up, O Lord." When confronted with temptation, "Rise up, O Lord." When facing spiritual opposition, when fear threatens to overwhelm us, or when we find ourselves in circumstances that seem impossible to overcome, "Rise up, O Lord."

And there is an even greater reason for our confidence. In Christ, God has already risen to confront and defeat our greatest enemies. Through His death and resurrection, Jesus has overcome sin, death, and the powers of darkness (==Colossians 2:13–15; Hebrews 2:14–15==). We are not asking Him to win a victory He has yet to secure. We are praying from within the victory He has already accomplished, trusting Him to bring the power and implications of that victory to bear upon our lives.

There is one final detail worth noticing. In the following verse, when the ark rested, Moses prayed, "Return, O LORD, to the many thousands of Israel" (==Numbers 10:36==). Whether Israel was moving or resting, Moses' concern was the same: the presence of God among His people.

And perhaps that is the greatest lesson in these two prayers. Moses didn't just want God to help them reach their destination. He wanted God to be with them throughout the journey.

May this become the posture of our own hearts. Whatever lies before us, whether we are stepping into a difficult battle or entering a season of rest, may our confidence be in the presence of the Lord. We do not need to know everything we will encounter along the way. We need to know the One who goes before us.
