## Commentary
There is an honest frustration in the psalmist's words: “His ways are always prospering.” He is looking at people who disregard God, mistreat others, and pursue their own selfish ambitions, and yet their lives seem to be going remarkably well. They have wealth, influence, security, and success. Meanwhile, those who are trying to walk faithfully with God often find themselves facing hardship, opposition, and disappointment.

This is one of the more difficult realities to reconcile with our understanding of God's justice. We know that God is righteous, that He loves justice, and that He opposes wickedness. So why does it sometimes appear that those who care the least about Him are the ones who prosper the most?

The psalmist Asaph wrestled with this same frustration in ==Psalm 73:2–3==: “But as for me, my feet had almost stumbled; my steps had nearly slipped. For I was envious of the boastful, when I saw the prosperity of the wicked.”

What makes Asaph's confession so significant is that the prosperity of the wicked had begun to affect his own faithfulness. He wondered whether his efforts to live righteously had been in vain (==Psalm 73:13==). He was making sacrifices to honor God while watching others enjoy the very things he had willingly surrendered.

Scripture doesn't dismiss this frustration or pretend that the problem isn't real. In fact, the Bible repeatedly acknowledges that wicked people sometimes prosper while righteous people suffer. Jeremiah even brought this question directly before the Lord: “Why does the way of the wicked prosper? Why are those happy who deal so treacherously?” (==Jeremiah 12:1==).

But notice the second half of ==Psalm 10:5==: “Your judgments are on high, out of his sight.” The wicked man interprets his prosperity as evidence that he is beyond accountability. Because he has not experienced the consequences of his actions, he assumes there are none. But God's judgments being beyond his sight does not mean they are absent. His inability to perceive God's justice does not mean God has ceased to be just.

We must be careful not to confuse God's patience with His approval or His delayed judgment with His indifference.

Asaph eventually came to this realization when he entered the sanctuary of God. He writes, “Then I understood their end” (==Psalm 73:17==). His circumstances hadn't necessarily changed, nor had the prosperity of the wicked suddenly disappeared. What changed was his perspective. He began to understand their present circumstances in light of eternity.

This is something we must learn as well. We cannot evaluate the goodness of God or the wisdom of obedience by comparing our present circumstances with those of people who have no regard for Him. Material prosperity is not necessarily evidence of God's approval, any more than suffering is necessarily evidence of His displeasure.

Some of the greatest rewards of obedience cannot be measured by the things we possess, and some of the greatest consequences of wickedness are not immediately visible.

But there is another side to this that ought to give us tremendous hope. _The same God who sees the wickedness of men also sees the faithfulness of those who love Him._

The writer of Hebrews reminds us, “For God is not unjust to forget your work and labor of love which you have shown toward His name” (==Hebrews 6:10==). God does not overlook our obedience. He remembers the sacrifices we have made out of love for Him. He sees the decisions no one else knows about, the compromises we have refused, the things we have surrendered, and the costly obedience that may never receive recognition or reward in this life.

There is something profoundly comforting about knowing that our faithfulness is not dependent upon other people noticing it or our circumstances validating it. Jesus repeatedly taught that our Father sees what is done in secret (==Matthew 6:4, 6, 18==). Nothing surrendered to Him in love goes unnoticed. We may never see the full fruit of our obedience in this life, but we can be certain that God has not forgotten a single act of faithfulness.

And perhaps this is what makes Asaph's eventual conclusion so beautiful. After wrestling with the prosperity of the wicked, he declares, “Nevertheless I am continually with You; You hold me by my right hand” (==Psalm 73:23==). He goes on to say, “Whom have I in heaven but You? And there is none upon earth that I desire besides You” (==Psalm 73:25==).

Asaph came to understand that his greatest reward was not something God would eventually give him. His greatest reward was God Himself. The wicked might possess things he did not have, but they could not compare with the treasure he had found in knowing and belonging to the Lord.

This does not mean that God will never reward our obedience in tangible ways. Scripture repeatedly assures us that He is a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him (==Hebrews 11:6==). But it does mean that our confidence in His goodness must rest on something more substantial than the immediate outcomes of our faithfulness.

When we see godless people in our world prosper, we are being invited to trust something that our circumstances cannot always confirm: God is just, He sees what is happening, and His judgment is not limited to the timetable we would prefer.

And when our own obedience seems costly, overlooked, or without reward, we can rest in the equally important assurance that _God sees us, too._ He knows what we have surrendered to follow Him. He knows what it has cost us to remain faithful. And He is not unjust to forget our labor of love.

The psalmist is teaching us that we can bring our frustration before God without surrendering our confidence in His character. We may not understand why He allows certain things to continue, or why our own faithfulness sometimes seems to produce so little visible fruit, but we can trust that His apparent silence is not evidence of His absence. Our obedience is never wasted, our surrender is never unnoticed, and the God who has called us to follow Him will prove Himself faithful.
