## Commentary
“And in this mountain the LORD of hosts will make for all people a feast of choice pieces, a feast of wines on the lees, of fat things full of marrow, of well-refined wines on the lees.”

God is the One making this meal; the LORD of hosts—the commander of armies—personally preparing a feast of the best food and the best wine, for all people, on His mountain. This isn't an isolated image. It's one frame of a thread that runs through the whole Bible: God keeps setting tables. He ate with Moses, Aaron, and the seventy elders on Sinai—“they saw God, and they ate and drank” (==Exodus 24:9-11==). David knew Him as the host who “prepares a table before me in the presence of my enemies” (==Psalm 23:5==). The risen Jesus was recognized at Emmaus “in the breaking of bread” (==Luke 24:35==), and His first move after the resurrection appearance in Galilee was to stand on a beach making breakfast—a redemptive meal where Peter’s three denials were healed by three declarations of love (==John 21:9-17==). And the story ends where Isaiah points: a wedding feast a King has prepared for His Son, with servants sent to the far reaches compelling everyone they find to come, because “all things are ready” (==Matthew 22:2-10==). Jesus even promises that when the Master returns and finds His servants watching, “he will gird himself and have them sit down to eat, and will come and serve them” (==Luke 12:37==). The God of the universe, in an apron, serving His family. That’s the God of the Bible.

The eternal plan of God is to gather His family and eat with them—and communion is where that plan touches your week. Every time the bread is broken and the cup is poured, the church simultaneously remembers the meal’s price and prophesies the meal’s consummation: “you proclaim the Lord’s death till He comes” (==1 Corinthians 11:26==). Communion is the gospel made visible—Isaiah’s feast served in advance.

Anyone who has ever prepared a real meal for guests knows it costs something. This one cost blood. But the greater labor wasn’t preparing the meal. It was preparing the *guests*. That’s what the table is doing to you every time you come to it—readying you for the day the Master seats you at His feast on the mountain, and serves you Himself.
