## Commentary
When the crowds responded to John’s preaching with “What shall we do?”, his answers were entirely practical. He who has two tunics, give to him who has none. Tax collectors, collect no more than what is appointed to you. Soldiers, don’t intimidate and don’t falsely accuse (==Luke 3:12-14==). Good instructions, every one of them. And notice what they share: stop doing what is contrary to God’s law, and start doing what it requires.

Don’t make the mistake of thinking that because John’s story appears in the New Testament, John preached the New Covenant gospel. He didn’t. John prepared the way for the Messiah, but his message was still an Old Covenant message—the final, greatest voice of an era that addressed behavior. John himself knew its limit: his baptism could wash a repentant man with water, but it couldn’t fill him with the Spirit (==Matthew 3:11==). Which is why Jesus could say that the least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than John (==Matthew 11:11==).

The New Covenant is not “stop sinning.” The New Covenant gospel of the kingdom is, “You *can’t* stop sinning. Admit it. Give up.” And it’s in that surrender that healing for the disease of sin begins. The gospel doesn’t hand you a better list. It hands you a new heart (==Ezekiel 36:26==).

That’s the difference between reformation and regeneration. Behavior modification can rearrange a life for a while—John’s instructions prove even the Old Covenant could do that much. But only the Spirit can raise one from the dead. The beautiful starting line of the gospel is the realization that you can’t heal yourself.
