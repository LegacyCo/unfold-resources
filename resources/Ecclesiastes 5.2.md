## Commentary
"Do not be rash with your mouth, and let not your heart utter anything hastily before God. For God is in heaven, and you on earth; therefore let your words be few."

Most of Scripture's warnings about the tongue concern how we speak to *people*. This one concerns how we speak to *God*.

Solomon has just said something startling in the previous verse: when you go to the house of God, "draw near to listen rather than to offer the sacrifice of fools" (==Ecclesiastes 5:1==). It's possible to show up to worship and be the fool in the room—not because of scandalous sin, but because of a mouth that runs ahead of a listening heart. We can fill the presence of God with impulsive words, hasty vows, and recycled phrases, and never once actually *listen*.

The reason Solomon gives for restraint is not that God is annoyed by us. It's a matter of position: "God is in heaven, and you on earth." When we remember who is actually in the room, our words get fewer and heavier—less filler, more substance. Jesus made the same point about prayer: the Father is not impressed by many words, as if He could be worn down by repetition (==Matthew 6:7==). He already knows what we need before we ask.

Scripture gives us pictures of what this listening posture looks like. When the Lord called to the boy Samuel in the night, Eli didn't teach him a speech to give. He taught him a sentence that opens up space for God to speak: "Speak, LORD, for Your servant is listening" (==1 Samuel 3:9-10==). Samuel's first prayer was an open ear—and God filled it. Elijah learned the same lesson on the mountain: the Lord was not in the wind, the earthquake, or the fire, but in a still small voice (==1 Kings 19:11-12==). Some things God will only say when we are quiet. And when Job finally saw the Lord for who He is, the man who had spoken chapters of words responded, "I lay my hand over my mouth" (==Job 40:4==). Nearness to God has a way of ending our monologues.

This is also why Solomon adds, "a fool's voice is known by his many words" (==Ecclesiastes 5:3==). Our words expose us. The vow made hastily in the emotion of the moment (==Ecclesiastes 5:4-5==), the promise God never asked for, the prayer designed to sound spiritual to those nearby—all of it is the mouth running ahead of the heart.

Fewness of words before God is a posture of honor. Some of the most profound moments in His presence will have no words at all—just a heart that came near to listen, and a Father who was pleased to find it quiet enough to hear Him.

## Application
Restructure the opening of your prayer time this week. Before you bring a single request, give God the first five minutes: open with Samuel's sentence—"Speak, LORD, for Your servant is listening"—then read a few verses slowly and stay quiet long enough for Him to answer. Let Him set the agenda before you present yours. And the next time you gather with the church, arrive as a listener: walk in asking "What are You saying, Lord?" before "What am I going to say?" You may find, like Samuel, that He had something to say to you all along.
