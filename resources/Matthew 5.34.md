## Commentary
“But I say to you, do not swear at all.” At first glance the command seems severe. Oaths were not forbidden in the law—Moses regulated them (==Leviticus 19:12==; ==Numbers 30:2==), and God Himself swears in Scripture. To understand what Jesus prohibits, we have to see the system He was confronting.

By the first century, the teachers of Israel had constructed an elaborate hierarchy of oaths. An oath sworn by the name of the Lord was fully binding. But oaths sworn by lesser things—heaven, the earth, Jerusalem, the temple, even one’s own head—were held to be less binding, or not binding at all (==Matthew 23:16==). In practice, what a man swore *by* functioned as fine print. A merchant could raise his hand, swear by heaven that his animal was healthy, and later walk away from his word with his reputation intact. Language itself had become an economy, with some words that locked a man in and others that quietly let him out.

Words work much like currency. When money is devalued, more of it is required to buy the same bread; when words are devalued, more of them are required to buy the same trust. A culture in which a simple yes is no longer enough must resort to escalating oaths—or, in our day, contracts, disclaimers, and fine print—to secure what one true word should have carried. And every generation develops its own vocabulary of sounding sincere: promises made in forms both parties know are not binding.

Jesus does not repair the system by ranking the oaths correctly. He abolishes it. “Do not swear at all”—because the entire structure existed to create distance between a speaker and his word, and in the kingdom no such distance exists. Every word is spoken before God, and every word is expected to be true.
