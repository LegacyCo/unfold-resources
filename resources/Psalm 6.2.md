## Commentary
Psalm 6 has historically been known as one of the seven "penitential psalms," along with Psalms 32, 38, 51, 102, 130, and 143. These psalms have often been used by the church as prayers of repentance, sorrow over sin, confession, and renewed dependence on the mercy of God. The designation has ancient roots in the church’s worship and devotional life, with the grouping commonly associated with early Christian and medieval tradition. 

Psalm 6 David bringing his weakness and inability into the presence of God.

The psalm begins with David pleading, “O LORD, do not rebuke me in Your anger, nor chasten me in Your hot displeasure” (Psalm 6:1). David seems to recognize that his suffering is not just external. There is a spiritual weight in this psalm. He feels the discipline of God, or at least fears that his present anguish is connected to God’s displeasure.

But David doesn't run from God. He runs to Him.

He says, “Have mercy on me, O LORD, for I am weak.”

The Hebrew word translated “weak” is ’*umlal*, which can carry the idea of being feeble, languishing, withered, or wasting away. David is not saying, “I am a little tired.” He is saying, “I am coming apart. My strength is drying up. I don't have the ability to hold myself together.”

Then he says, “Heal me, for my bones are troubled.”

The word translated “troubled” is bahal, meaning terrified, disturbed, alarmed, dismayed, or deeply shaken. David uses this word twice, first of his bones and then of his soul: “My soul also is greatly troubled” (Psalm 6:3). In Hebrew thought, the bones often represent the deepest structure of a person’s life and strength. David is describing distress that has gone all the way down into the deepest recesses.

His body is shaken.

His soul is shaken.

His strength is failing.

And his prayer is simple: “Have mercy.”

That word “mercy” comes from the Hebrew *ḥānan*, which means to show favor, be gracious, or extend compassion to someone in need. David isn't presenting a résumé of righteousness. He is appealing to the gracious character of the Lord.

This is what makes Psalm 6 such a powerful penitential psalm. Repentance is not self-repair. It's not pretending we are stronger than we are. It is the honest turning of the whole self toward the mercy of God.

## Cross Reference
We see this similar theme of being confident in the Lord's mercy throughout Scripture. When Moses asked to see God’s glory, the Lord revealed Himself as “merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth” (==Exodus 34:6–7==). Psalm 103 says the Lord is “merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in mercy,” and that He remembers we are dust (==Psalm 103:8–14==). Hebrews tells us to come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need (==Hebrews 4:14–16==).
