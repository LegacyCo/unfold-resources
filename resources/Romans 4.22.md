## Commentary
“Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness” (==Genesis 15:6==). Abraham did not slowly accumulate righteousness through a lifetime of good behavior. He believed God—and his faith became the gate through which righteousness was transferred to his account.

Imagine a billionaire who promises you a million dollars and asks for your banking information. You check your account and find one hundred dollars. When you call to ask about it, he explains: “I will give you the million—one hundred dollars a day for the rest of your life, until it adds up.” That is how most of us think righteousness works: a seed deposited at conversion that gradually grows into an oak across a lifetime of successful sanctification. That picture may describe growth in maturity—the slow transformation of habits, desires, and behavior, which matters enormously. But it does not describe righteousness, because righteousness is not a process. It is a transfer, credited in full.

This is why you cannot grow *in* righteousness. Righteous is something you either are or are not. You can grow in maturity, in obedience, in your understanding of what it means to be righteous and how to live in agreement with it—but you cannot become more accepted than God already declares you to be. You do not grow into righteousness. You grow from it.

And Paul insists this was not written for Abraham’s sake alone: righteousness “shall also be imputed to us who believe in Him who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead” (==Romans 4:24==). Do you believe in Him who raised Jesus? Then His righteousness is now your righteousness—the full amount, already deposited (==2 Corinthians 5:21==).

## Application
Check which picture of righteousness you are living under. If you secretly believe God is paying out acceptance a hundred dollars at a time based on your performance, your spiritual life will feel like an anxious audit—always counting, never sure of the balance. Faith looks at the account and takes God at His word: the transfer is complete.

Then put growth in its right place. Pursue maturity aggressively—new habits, deeper obedience, transformed desires—but pursue it *from* acceptance, not *for* it. The difference shows up on your worst days: an employee who fails fears for his position; a child who fails runs to a father. Let your effort flow from a settled account, and watch how much freer the effort becomes.
