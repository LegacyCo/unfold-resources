## Commentary
The book of Ezekiel opens in exile. The prophet is not prophesying from Jerusalem. He is “among the captives by the River Chebar,” living in Babylon with the exiles of Judah.

Ezekiel was from a priestly family (==Ezekiel 1:3==), which means his life would have been ordered around the temple, sacrifice, holiness, and the prescribed worship of Yahweh. But now the priest is far from the temple and the people are living under the reality of God's judgment.

For generations, the Lord had warned His people that if they continued to turn from Him, worshiped other gods, practiced injustice, shed innocent blood, and refused His word, the land itself would eventually vomit them out (==Leviticus 18:24–28==; ==Deuteronomy 28:15–68==). The exile was the result of God doing exactly what He said He would do if His people broke covenant with Him.

The northern kingdom of Israel had already fallen to Assyria in 722 BC because of persistent idolatry and rebellion against the Lord (==2 Kings 17:6–23==). Judah saw that judgment happen, but did not learn from it. Though there were seasons of reform, especially under kings like Hezekiah and Josiah, the nation continued returning to idolatry, injustice, false worship, and political trust in surrounding nations instead of the Lord (==2 Kings 21:10–15==; ==2 Chronicles 36:15–16==; ==Jeremiah 25:3–11==).

Eventually, Babylon became the instrument of judgment against Judah.

In 597 BC, Babylon took King Jehoiachin, many officials, warriors, craftsmen, and priests into exile (==2 Kings 24:10–16==). Ezekiel was likely among that group. Then, in 586 BC, Jerusalem was destroyed, the temple was burned, and the kingdom of Judah collapsed under Babylonian power (==2 Kings 25:1–21==).

So when Ezekiel begins, we are meeting a priest far from the temple, a prophet among displaced people, and a nation living under the consequences of long-term rebellion.

Ezekiel is written to a people who have lost everything. The book wrestles with some of the most painful questions God’s people could ask: Has the Lord abandoned us? Can judgment and covenant faithfulness exist at the same time? Is there any hope after such deep failure?

The book confronts Israel and Judah with the seriousness of their rebellion. Ezekiel is filled with warnings, symbolic actions, visions of judgment, and hard words about the corruption of priests, prophets, leaders, and people.

But the book is not only about judgment. Ezekiel also holds out hope for restoration. God promises to gather His people again, cleanse them, give them a new heart, put a new spirit within them, and place His own Spirit in them so they can walk in His ways (==Ezekiel 36:24–27==). He promises to raise what is dead, restore what has been ruined, and shepherd His people Himself (==Ezekiel 34:11–16== and ==Ezekiel 37:1–14==).

So Ezekiel begins in exile, but exile is not the end of the story.

The people have been carried away because of their sin, but God has not surrendered His covenant purposes. Judgment is real, but so is mercy.
