## Commentary
John opens chapter 2 with both tenderness and sobriety. This verse holds together two things many people separate. First, John says plainly, “that you may not sin.” That's the goal. That's the expectation of life in Christ. He doesn't lower the standard of following Jesus to make people comfortable in their experience. He writes with the clear purpose that the people of God would not continue in sin. But then, in the same breath, he speaks to the provision of grace: “if any man sin, we have an advocate.”

That is important because the gospel is neither permissive nor crushing. It's not permissive, because John does not write as though sin is inevitable and therefore not worth resisting. And it's not crushing, because when failure occurs, he doesn't leave the believer in despair. He points immediately to Christ. Jesus Christ the righteous stands as our advocate with the Father. The One whose own finished work is the basis of His intercession is praying for *us*.

God's grace is not given to us to make peace with sin. Grace is given so that we might break with sin forever...in this life. And His grace is present even when we fail. The same gospel that teaches us to deny ungodliness also teaches us where to go when we've fallen short. John is confronting two errors: one is careless Christianity that treats sin lightly; the other is condemned Christianity that acts as though one failure has cut the believer off from hope. John allows neither.

There is a holy balance here. The one born of God is not meant to live casually in sin. The Christian life is not a management plan for ongoing defeat. John writes so that sin resisted. But when a believer does stumble, the answer is not hiding, self-punishment, or endless introspection. The answer is Christ. Our confidence is not in our consistency, but in His righteousness. He is our advocate with the Father.

That is why mature Christianity neither plays with sin nor panics in the face of it. It hates sin because it sees what sin is. And it clings to Christ because it sees who Christ is.

As Dean Briggs rights in "The Power of Your New Creation Life": "You possess this reality now (the ability to live free from sin), but until you believe and become renewed in your habits and thought patterns to the spiritual physics of your new creation (ie; grow up into maturity), the experience of victorious living will remain a largely dormant, conflicted, inconsistent theory. This is why much of our teaching and theology have formed around the deficit more than the promise. We do not teach people it is possible; we teach them, wrongly, why it is not."
