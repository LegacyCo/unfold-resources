## Commentary
There is an interesting pattern throughout Scripture in which movement toward the east is associated with judgment, exile, and separation from God's presence, while movement toward the west is frequently associated with return, restoration, and drawing near to God. This is not an absolute rule governing every geographical movement in Scripture, but it is a recurring theme worth recognizing.

The pattern begins in Genesis. When Adam and Eve sinned, they were expelled from the Garden of Eden, and God stationed cherubim at its eastern entrance to guard the way to the tree of life (==Genesis 3:23–24==). Later, after Cain murdered Abel, Scripture tells us that he departed from the presence of the Lord and settled in the land of Nod, east of Eden (==Genesis 4:16==). In both accounts, the eastern direction is associated with humanity's separation from the place of God's presence. 

We see something similar when Lot separates from Abraham and journeys east toward the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah (==Genesis 13:11–13==). His movement eastward eventually places him in a city destined for judgment. Centuries later, the people of Judah are carried into exile in Babylon, east of the Promised Land. Yet when God restores His people, they return westward to Jerusalem (==Ezra 1:1–5==). Even Israel's original entrance into the Promised Land involved crossing the Jordan River from east to west (==Joshua 3:14–17==).

Perhaps one of the most significant expressions of this imagery is found in the design of the tabernacle and temple. The entrance faced east, meaning that anyone approaching the Holy of Holies would move westward, toward the place where God's glory dwelled (==Exodus 27:13–16==). The physical arrangement of the sanctuary reinforced the picture of drawing near to God.

Interestingly, just two chapters before this passage, Ezekiel witnesses twenty-five men standing in the temple with their backs toward the sanctuary and their faces toward the east, worshiping the sun (==Ezekiel 8:16==). They had literally turned their backs on the presence of God to worship something else. The direction of their bodies reflected the condition of their hearts. 

With this larger biblical pattern in mind, the movement of God's glory in Ezekiel 10 becomes particularly significant. The glory moves eastward, stopping at the eastern gate before eventually leaving Jerusalem and resting on the mountain east of the city (==Ezekiel 11:22–23==). This is a movement of judgment. God is withdrawing His glory from a people who have persistently rejected Him.

Yet this is not the end of the story. In ==Ezekiel 43:1–5==, Ezekiel sees the glory of the Lord returning from the east, moving westward into the temple and filling the sanctuary once again. The very direction associated with departure and judgment is reversed in a beautiful picture of restoration. The God whose glory departed because of His people's rebellion is the same God who promises to return and dwell among them.

Centuries later, Jesus approaches Jerusalem from the Mount of Olives, the very mountain where Ezekiel had witnessed the glory of God resting as it departed the city. Riding on a donkey, Jesus descends from the mountain to the east and enters Jerusalem as the crowds cry out, "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" (==Luke 19:29–38==). The geographical movement is striking. In Ezekiel, the glory of God departs Jerusalem toward the Mount of Olives. In the Gospels, Jesus, the One in whom the fullness of God dwells bodily (==Colossians 2:9==), approaches Jerusalem from that same mountain. Although the Gospel writers do not explicitly identify this as the fulfillment of Ezekiel's vision, the imagery is difficult to overlook. The glory that had departed is now, in the person of Jesus Christ, coming to His people.
