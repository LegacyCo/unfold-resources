## Commentary
“Now if their fall is riches for the world, and their failure riches for the Gentiles, how much more their fullness!”

Consider what Paul is saying here. Israel's rejection of the Messiah became the occasion for the gospel to spread among the Gentiles. What appeared to be a catastrophic failure in God's covenant purposes became the means through which extraordinary spiritual riches were extended to the nations. People who had once been strangers to the covenants of promise were now being brought near through the blood of Christ (==Ephesians 2:11–13==).

But Paul is not celebrating what has come to the Gentiles through Israel's stumbling. He is directing our attention toward something that has not yet happened. If Israel's failure has resulted in such extraordinary blessing for the world, what will happen when Israel enters into her fullness?

The Greek word translated “fullness” is *plērōma*, which carries the idea of completeness or that which fills something up. In this context, it points toward a future reality in which Israel's present condition of unbelief will be dramatically reversed. Paul does not explain every detail of that fullness here, but the surrounding verses make clear that he is anticipating something far greater than the preservation of a small remnant. In ==Romans 11:15==, he describes Israel's future acceptance as nothing less than “life from the dead.”

This is where why we must resist the temptation to make the Church the conclusion of God's redemptive purposes. The salvation of the Gentiles is a glorious accomplishment of the gospel, but Paul reveals that it belongs to a much larger divine purpose. It is a means, not an end. God has brought the nations into the blessings promised through Israel's Messiah, and He intends to use that mercy to provoke Israel to jealousy, leading toward her eventual restoration (==Romans 11:11, 25–26==).

Paul clearly expects Israel's restoration to bring an increase in blessing that exceeds what has already come to the nations through her stumbling. Whatever that future blessing may ultimately look like, Paul wants the Gentile believers in Rome to recognize that the greatest implications of God's faithfulness to Israel may still lie ahead.

We have witnessed extraordinary things through the spread of the gospel among the nations, but we should not assume that everything God intends to accomplish has already been realized.

Paul looks at the riches that have come to the world through Israel's failure and asks us to consider what her fullness might bring. “How much more?”
