## Commentary
“Do not boast against the branches. But if you do boast, remember that you do not support the root, but the root supports you.”

To understand the weight of this statement, we need to consider the illustration Paul has been developing. He describes Israel as an olive tree, with some of its natural branches broken off because of unbelief. Gentile believers, whom he describes as branches from a wild olive tree, have been grafted into this cultivated tree and now share in its root and richness (==Romans 11:17==).

Paul does not describe God planting an entirely new tree to replace the old one. He describes Gentiles being grafted into a tree that was already established. The Gentiles have not become participants in a new and unrelated redemptive purpose. They have been brought into something God began long before their inclusion.

This means that the Gentile Church does not stand independently of Israel's covenant history. Everything we have received through Jesus Christ has come to us through the purposes God established with Israel. The Messiah is Israel's Messiah. The Scriptures that prepared the world for His coming were entrusted to Israel. The apostles who proclaimed His resurrection were Jewish men. Even the new covenant in which we now participate was originally promised to the house of Israel and the house of Judah (==Jeremiah 31:31–34==). 

We have been made participants in promises that were not ours.

This should produce profound humility in the Church.

But consider how differently much of Christian history has unfolded. Rather than recognizing our dependence upon the covenantal promises given to Israel, portions of the Church developed the conviction that Israel had been permanently rejected and replaced. The Jewish people were increasingly regarded as a discarded nation, while the Church claimed the entirety of their spiritual inheritance.

Paul anticipated this kind of arrogance. He is confronting a theological pride that fundamentally misunderstands the nature of our salvation. The Gentiles have no grounds for boasting over Israel because their inclusion is an act of mercy, not evidence of their superiority.

God did not bring the Gentiles into His covenant purposes so that we could establish a religious identity independent of Israel. He brought us near through Israel's Messiah, making us fellow heirs and partakers of His promises in Christ (==Ephesians 2:11–19; 3:6==).

If the Church is to recover something of its apostolic calling, it must recover a right understanding of its relationship to the Jewish people. Not an uncritical endorsement of everything associated with modern Israel, nor the suggestion that Jewish people possess salvation apart from Jesus Christ, but a recognition that God's covenant purposes concerning them remain unfinished.

We cannot properly understand ourselves apart from the people and promises through whom our salvation came. A branch has no life independent of the tree into which it has been grafted. It does not produce its own nourishment or sustain its own existence. Everything it possesses is received.

This is the fundamental posture of the Christian life. We are recipients before we are participants. Everything we have is the result of grace, and everything we become depends upon the life God supplies.

Paul's warning should therefore produce more than a corrected theology of Israel. It should confront every expression of spiritual pride that imagines we have become something significant in ourselves.

The Gentile Church does not sustain God's covenant purposes. We have been graciously included in them.
