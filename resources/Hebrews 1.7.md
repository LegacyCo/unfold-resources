## Commentary
“And of the angels He says: ‘Who makes His angels spirits and His ministers a flame of fire.’”

God makes His servants flames of fire. Not lamps He occasionally lights—flames. The source is five sentences from the end of this same letter: “our God is a consuming fire” (==Hebrews 12:29==), and the nature of fire is that it spreads. Fire reproduces fire. Whatever He touches should also burn. So a servant of the consuming Fire who isn’t burning is a contradiction in terms—like wet wood in the middle of a furnace.

You were made to burn, so you *will* burn for something. If we don’t burn for the things of heaven, we’ll burn for inferior things—sports, money, ambition, sin. That sounds extreme until you trace the origin of whatever steals your affection from Jesus. Anything that quenches your fire for Him doesn’t have to be evil to do it; it can look innocent, even good—that’s why the tree was called the knowledge of good *and* evil, and the good of that tree kills as surely as the evil (==Genesis 2:17==). The question is never whether you’ll be passionate. It’s what your passion will be spent on—and only one fire purifies while it burns.

This was normal Christianity once. The internal flame of the Spirit is what propelled the early church to turn the world upside down (==Acts 17:6==)—to say things like “Christ will be magnified in my body, whether by life or by death” (==Philippians 1:20==) and mean it. We read that and call it extreme, which mostly measures how far we’ve drifted from normal. Jim Elliot prayed the honest version of this verse: “God makes His ministers a flame of fire. Am I ignitable? God deliver me from the dread asbestos of other things. Saturate me with the oil of Thy Spirit that I may be aflame.” That’s the prayer this verse deserves. He makes His ministers flames of fire—so ask Him to burn off the dross, and be what you were made to be.
