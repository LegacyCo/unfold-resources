## Commentary
“To the law and to the testimony! If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.”

Isaiah speaks these words into a moment of fear, political instability, and spiritual confusion. Judah is threatened by powerful enemies, and the temptation of the people is to look everywhere except to God for certainty. Some are turning to mediums and spiritists, seeking guidance from the dead: “Should not a people seek their God? Should they seek the dead on behalf of the living?” (==Isaiah 8:19==).

Isaiah’s counsel is clear: “To the law and to the testimony!”

The Hebrew word translated “law” is _torah_, which means instruction, teaching, or direction. “Testimony” refers to the revealed witness God had given His covenant people. Together the phrase points back to what God has spoken. When voices multiply and darkness closes in, Isaiah tells God's people to return to the Word they have already been given.

“If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.”

That sentence establishes an important principle that runs throughout Scripture: spiritual claims are not authenticated just because they sound spiritual. A supernatural experience is not self-validating. A prophetic word is not true simply because someone says, “The Lord told me.” A teacher is not trustworthy simply because the teaching is powerful, emotional, or persuasive. The measure is the revealed Word of God.

This becomes especially important in Isaiah’s setting because the alternative voices were supernatural voices. These people were consulting mediums, necromancers, and those who claimed access to hidden knowledge. Isaiah does not deny that spiritual experiences may occur. His concern is whether those experiences agree with what God has already revealed.

This same principle appears throughout Scripture. Moses warned Israel that even if a prophet produced a sign or wonder, the message was to be rejected if it led them away from the Lord (==Deuteronomy 13:1–4==). Paul tells the Galatians that even if “an angel from heaven” preached a different gospel, they were not to receive it (==Galatians 1:8==). The Bereans were commended because they “searched the Scriptures daily” to determine whether Paul’s teaching was true (==Acts 17:11==). John tells believers, “Do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits” (==1 John 4:1==).

The consistent biblical pattern is that revelation is tested by what God has already revealed.

When Israel was preparing to enter the Promised Land, Moses told them that God’s words were to occupy the center of their lives: “You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up” (==Deuteronomy 6:6–7==). Joshua was told that the Book of the Law must not depart from his mouth but should be meditated upon “day and night” (==Joshua 1:8==). The blessed person of ==Psalm 1:2== is the one whose “delight is in the law of the LORD” and who meditates upon it day and night.

The Word was never intended to be a religious reference book consulted occasionally when a problem arose. It was meant to shape the imagination, vocabulary, convictions, desires, and decisions of God's people.

Israel was to become a people whose minds had been formed by what God had said.

That remains true for the Church.

Jesus answered Satan in the wilderness repeatedly with the words, “It is written” (==Matthew 4:4, 7, 10==). He described His disciples as those who “abide” in His Word (==John 8:31==). Paul told Timothy that the Scriptures were able to make him “wise for salvation” and were profitable for teaching, correction, and training in righteousness (==2 Timothy 3:15–17==). He told the Colossians, “Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly” (==Colossians 3:16==).

This is why Scripture must remain central even when other voices are legitimate. We may receive wisdom from pastors, teachers, counselors, scholars, traditions, prophetic words, dreams, impressions, or personal experience, but none of these occupy the place of Scripture. They stand beneath it.

God's Word is not one voice among many. It is the measure by which the other voices are judged.

Isaiah's warning is therefore as relevant now as it was in Judah: “To the law and to the testimony!”

When culture speaks, to the Word.

When fear speaks, to the Word.

When experience speaks, to the Word.

When a teacher speaks, to the Word.

When someone says, “God told me,” to the Word.

When our own emotions are loudest, to the Word.

Not because Scripture answers every imaginable question directly, but because Scripture reveals the God whose character, purposes, and ways become the framework through which every other question must be understood.

And Isaiah connects this directly with light: “If they do not speak according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.”

Light comes from what God has spoken.

A few verses later Isaiah will describe the nation as walking in darkness (==Isaiah 9:2==), and then announce the coming of the Child who will bring light into that darkness (==Isaiah 9:6==). Ultimately, the Word of God leads us to the One who is Himself the Light of the world (==John 8:12==).

God has always been forming a people who hear His voice, trust His Word, and order their lives around what He has spoken. They are designed to be a people of His Word.
