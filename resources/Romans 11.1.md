## Commentary
Paul's question arises naturally from everything Paul has been explaining in the previous two chapters. Israel, the nation entrusted with the covenants, the promises, the Law, and the revelation of God, had largely rejected the Messiah who came to them. The very people who had been prepared through centuries of prophetic revelation failed to recognize the One to whom that revelation pointed. So what does their rejection of Jesus mean for God's relationship with Israel? Has He finished with them? Have His promises been transferred to another people?

Paul's answer leaves no room for such a conclusion.

The Greek expression translated “Certainly not!” is *mē genoito*, an emphatic rejection that Paul frequently uses when confronting a conclusion that is fundamentally incompatible with the truth he is establishing. The expression carries the force of “May it never be!” or “Absolutely not!” We see this same language throughout Romans. When Paul asks whether Israel's unbelief could nullify God's faithfulness, he responds, *mē genoito* (==Romans 3:3–4==). When he asks whether believers should continue in sin so that grace might abound, his answer is again *mē genoito* (==Romans 6:1–2==). And when he raises the question of whether there is unrighteousness with God, he responds with the same emphatic rejection (==Romans 9:14==). Paul uses this expression when he wants to make it unmistakably clear that a particular conclusion must be rejected. And now, when confronting the suggestion that God has rejected His covenant people, he responds with the same force: *Absolutely not!* The very thought is incompatible with everything Paul understands about the faithfulness and character of God.

And the evidence Paul immediately offers? “For I also am an Israelite, of the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin.” Paul points to himself as living proof that God has not abandoned His people. The same God who called Abraham, established His covenant with Israel, and spoke through the prophets had revealed His Son to Paul, an Israelite who had once violently opposed the gospel.

Yet Paul's argument extends far beyond his personal testimony. The question of Israel's future is ultimately a question about the faithfulness of God Himself.

If God could make everlasting promises to Abraham and his descendants, only to abandon those promises because of Israel's unbelief, what would that reveal about the reliability of His word? His covenants? His nature? The issue is not whether Israel has been faithful. Paul has already established that they have not. The issue is whether God will remain faithful to what He has spoken *despite* their unfaithfulness.

This is the question Paul raised earlier: “For what if some did not believe? Will their unbelief make the faithfulness of God without effect? Certainly not!” (==Romans 3:3–4==).

There is a profound distinction between Israel's failure to fulfill her calling and God's failure to fulfill His covenant purposes. The first is painfully evident throughout Scripture. The second is impossible.

This does not mean that Jewish identity provides salvation apart from Christ. Paul has already made clear that righteousness comes through faith in Jesus, for Jew and Gentile alike (==Romans 10:9–13==). Nor does it mean that every individual Israelite will necessarily be saved (more on that as we move through the rest of chapter 11). But it does mean that Israel's present unbelief is not the end of the story.
