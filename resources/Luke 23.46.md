## Commentary
The skeptic—and honestly, a lot of theology—points to Jesus crying “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (==Matthew 27:46==) and stops there. An entire doctrine gets built on that one sentence: the Father turned His back on the Son because many believe God can’t look on sin. But those weren’t Jesus’ last words.

When Jesus cried out, He was quoting the opening line of ==Psalm 22:1==—a psalm every Jew within earshot knew by heart, a psalm that describes the crucifixion in stunning detail centuries before it happened. And that psalm doesn’t end in abandonment. It resolves: “He has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; *nor has He hidden His face from Him*; but when He cried to Him, He heard” (==Psalm 22:24==). Jesus had already told His disciples exactly how the cross would go: “you… will leave Me alone. And yet I am not alone, because the Father is with Me” (==John 16:32==). Everyone forsook Him. The Father didn’t. Paul confirms it: “God was *in Christ* reconciling the world to Himself” (==2 Corinthians 5:19==). Not watching from a distance with His back turned—in Christ, at the cross, reconciling.

And that’s where the whole “God can’t look on sin” doctrine collapses. Where did that doctrine even come from? A misreading of ==Habakkuk 1:13==—“You are of purer eyes than to behold evil, and cannot look on wickedness.” But read the rest of the verse: in the very next breath, Habakkuk asks God why He *keeps looking* at the treacherous. Habakkuk isn’t declaring a doctrine—he’s a confused prophet processing out loud, asking why God’s actions don’t match his assumptions. God was looking on the wicked, and Habakkuk couldn’t understand how. Religion took a prophet’s question and turned it into a doctrine—a doctrine that has become a golden calf in much of the church. And if the Father were ever going to look away from sin, it would’ve been at the cross—the one moment in history when the sin of the whole world was gathered in one place, on one Man. But He didn’t look away. The truth is, His loving eyes of fire saw our sin and kept burning with passion anyway—because if God didn’t find me when I was lost, I never would have been found. Scripture is clear that none of us were looking for Him (==Romans 3:11==). He’s the seeker in this story.

And that’s who your Father is toward sinners still. “Good and upright is the LORD; therefore He shows sinners the way” (==Psalm 25:8==). He doesn’t avert His eyes from sinners—He shows them the way home. He demonstrated His own love toward us in that while we were *still sinners*, Christ died for us (==Romans 5:8==). And whatever you do, don’t tell Jesus that God can’t look on sin—He’d have no one to go to dinner with (==Luke 15:2==).

And then come the actual last words: “Father, into Your hands I commit My Spirit.” That’s the glorious resolution. Jesus entered the darkest moment any human has ever faced—bearing the sin of the world—and He died the way He lived: with “Father” on His lips and His trust fully intact. He didn’t die questioning the Father’s presence. He died committing Himself into the Father’s hands.

How you answer the question “where was the Father when Jesus needed Him most?” means everything—because it’s the same question you’ll ask when *you* need Him most. It’s the question underneath the counseling session with someone who feels abandoned by God. It’s the question behind the eyes of the person you’re sharing your faith with who is afraid of God. If the Father forsook His own Son in His darkest hour, what confidence do you have for yours? But if the Father never left—not even for a moment—then His promise holds all the way down: “I will never leave you nor forsake you” (==Hebrews 13:5==).
