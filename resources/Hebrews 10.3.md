## Commentary
The writer of Hebrews is showing the built-in limitation of the old order. The law was holy and given by God, but it was still a shadow. A shadow can point to something, but it can't impart the substance itself. That's why the sacrifices had to continue. If they had truly perfected the worshipper, they would have ended. A cleansed conscience would not need to keep returning to the same altar with the same reminder.

But that's exactly what the old system did: it kept sin in view. Year after year, sacrifice after sacrifice, the worshipper was reminded that the problem had not yet been fully dealt with. The blood of bulls and goats could cover ceremonially, but it could not cleanse inwardly. It could not bring a man into perfected standing before God. It could not produce a conscience at rest. The repetition of sacrifice was itself the proof that the work was incomplete.

Hebrews sets that old system against the glory of the New Covenant. In Christ, God deals with sin decisively. “Their sins and lawless deeds I will remember no more.” That doesn't mean God becomes forgetful in the human sense. It means He no longer holds those sins against His people because the one sufficient sacrifice has been offered. Christ has done what the old order could never do. He has opened the way for a cleansed conscience and true maturity before God.

That is why maturity in the New Covenant includes learning to agree with the finished work of Christ. There is a false humility that keeps dragging old forgiven sin back into the presence of God, as if Jesus had not actually dealt with it. But perfected sons and daughters do not build their identity around what God has already put away. They do not keep rehearsing before the Father what the blood of His Son has already removed.

The promise of the New Covenant is that "their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more" (v17). Perfect, blameless, mature sons and daughters don't remind their Father of something He has already forgotten.

## Application
Many believers live with an old covenant conscience under a new covenant promise. They have been forgiven, but they still think like worshippers standing at the old altar—always circling back, rehearsing failure, always more conscious of sin than of the sufficiency of Christ. That is not maturity. That is living beneath the victory of the cross.

This doesn't mean we treat sin lightly. It means we treat the blood of Jesus seriously. When God says, “I will remember no more,” we don't honor Him by rehashing what He has put away. We honor Him by believing Him. We repent where repentance is needed, but we don't keep resurrecting what Christ has buried.
