## Commentary
“From now on let no one trouble me, for I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus.”

When Paul’s authority was challenged, his defense was his scars. Not his credentials, not his revelations, not his résumé of churches planted—his body. The stonings, the rods, the lashes, the shipwrecks (==2 Corinthians 11:23-27==) had written a record on his skin, and Paul points to it as the verification of his ministry: *if you want to know whether I’m real, read my body. The marks tell you who I belong to.*

There’s a sharp irony in Paul closing his letter this way, because the whole Galatian crisis was an argument about marks in the flesh. The agitators were pressuring Gentile believers to be circumcised—and Paul exposes their motive: “that they may boast in your flesh” (==Galatians 6:12-13==). They wanted to count marks they had persuaded other men to receive. Paul answers with marks of an entirely different kind—not a ceremonial cut but wounds absorbed in the service of the crucified King. And the Galatians didn’t have to take his word for it. They had seen at least one set of those scars made: it was at Lystra, a city of Galatia, that Paul was stoned, dragged out of the city, and left for dead (==Acts 14:19==). Some of the first readers of this letter may have washed that blood off of him. When Paul writes “I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus,” he’s pointing at wounds they watched him take for their sake.

This is why apostolic authority can never be faked—because you can’t fake the cross. Apostolic language that isn’t undergirded by a crucified life has no apostolic authority, no matter how correct the vocabulary. The seven sons of Sceva proved it. They borrowed Paul’s words and tried the name of Jesus on a demonized man—and every word they said about Jesus was true. Right formula, right name, right doctrine. The demon’s answer exposes what they lacked: “Jesus I know, and Paul I know; but who are you?” (==Acts 19:15==). The realm of darkness keeps a record of people who have passed through the cross. Hell recognized a man branded by it—and saw straight through seven men quoting a crucifixion they had only watched from a distance. That’s the difference between apostolic doctrine and apostolic authority: you can get doctrine from a podcast. Authority comes from the cross—entrusted to those who have let it deal with everything in them that contradicts the message they preach.

And a servant is not greater than his master here. There is a Man on heaven’s throne right now whose resurrected, glorified body still bears the marks of the cross—He showed them to Thomas as the proof of who He was (==John 20:27==). The Master’s scars authenticated Him; His servants’ scars authenticate them. Whether they’re carried on the body like Paul’s or on the soul like most of ours—the healed-over places where pride died, where forgiveness cost something, where obedience left a mark. The life that carries real authority is the one that carries real marks.

## Word Study
- *marks* : **stigma** — the brand burned or cut into a slave’s skin so that everyone who saw him knew at a glance whose he was. This is its only appearance in the New Testament, and Paul chooses it deliberately: his scars aren’t battle trophies—they’re ownership marks. “Let no one trouble me” makes sense once you see it: you don’t argue with a brand. The question of whose he was had been permanently settled in his flesh.

The idea echoes elsewhere:

- ==2 Corinthians 4:10== — “always carrying about in the body the dying of the Lord Jesus, that the life of Jesus also may be manifested in our body.” The brand and the life come together.
- ==Revelation 7:3== — God’s servants “sealed… on their foreheads.” Heaven marks its own, too.

Summary: everyone is marked by what owns them. Paul’s stigma settled the ownership question so thoroughly that demons filed him by name.
