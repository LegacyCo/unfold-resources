## Commentary
A confederation of armies—Moab, Ammon, and others—has assembled against God’s people, and King Jehoshaphat is afraid. So what does he do with the fear? He proclaims a fast, gathers the entire nation to Jerusalem, and stands in the assembly to pray one of the most honest prayers in the Bible: “O our God… we have no power against this great multitude that is coming against us; nor do we know what to do, *but our eyes are upon You*.”

Notice where his eyes were not. Not on the enemies assembling at the border. Not on his military leaders, gathering counsel. Not on allies, weapons, or the strategies that worked last time. No power, no plan—just a gaze. And heaven responded immediately: the Spirit of the Lord came upon Jahaziel in the middle of the assembly with a word: “Do not be afraid… for the battle is not yours, but God’s… Position yourselves, stand still and *see* the salvation of the LORD” (==2 Chronicles 20:15-17==). The next morning Judah marched out with the worship team in front—and their enemies destroyed one another before God’s people ever drew a sword (==2 Chronicles 20:22-23==). They lifted their eyes off their enemies and onto the Lord, and they *saw* His salvation. The humility of God’s people made room for God to be God.

Here’s the principle underneath the story: whatever you look at, you empower. You can’t look at two things at once. When you give more attention to your problem, your feelings, or your fears than you give to Jesus, you empower the situation instead of empowering God to reveal Himself *into* the situation. That was Eve’s mistake in the garden—she took her eyes off the most beautiful Being in the universe, fixed them on something forbidden, “saw that the tree was good for food… pleasant to the eyes” (==Genesis 3:6==), and enthroned her temptation by gazing at it. Fixing your eyes is a throne-setting act. Look at the problem long enough and you crown it; look at Jesus and you enthrone Him—and your problem gets moved to where all His enemies belong: under His feet (==Hebrews 10:13==).

And understand what’s actually at stake in your battle. If Jehoshaphat—a righteous king who sought the Lord—is crushed by pagan armies, what does that say about Jehoshaphat’s God? The threat against God’s people was a threat against the *testimony of God* in the earth. Was Goliath David’s problem, or God’s problem—with David as the man God used to answer it? The situation standing in front of you is not primarily a puzzle for you to solve. It’s an assault on the testimony of God in your life. Which means the moment you give more attention to your situation than to the God of your situation, you take ownership of a battle that was never yours. If you belong to the Lord, the battle belongs to the Lord. The diagnosis is His. The bank account is His. The prodigal is His. You were bought at a price—your life, and everything touching it, is no longer your own (==1 Corinthians 6:19-20==). Keep your eyes on Him, and your enemies become His enemies. And He’s undefeated.

## Application
This is not a call to put your head in the sand. David didn’t ignore Goliath, and Jehoshaphat didn’t pretend the armies weren’t coming—you don’t throw the power bill in the trash or ignore the pain in your body. Wisdom engages the facts. The question is *affection*: you can acknowledge the situation while reserving your gaze for Jesus. So take inventory tonight: what got the most of your attention today—the problem, or the God of the problem? Then do what Jehoshaphat did with the fear: bring it into the presence of God, out loud—“I have no power and no plan”—and finish the sentence the same way he did. My eyes are upon You.
