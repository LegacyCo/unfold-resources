## Commentary
“For if God did not spare the natural branches, He may not spare you either.”

Consider the weight of what Paul is saying. Israel was the nation God had chosen, the people to whom He had entrusted His covenants, His promises, His Law, and His prophetic revelation. For centuries, God had demonstrated extraordinary patience toward them, preserving them through their repeated failures and remaining faithful to the promises He had made to their fathers. Yet despite all these privileges, many of the natural branches were broken off because of unbelief.

And now Paul turns to the Gentiles and warns them not to presume that they are somehow exempt from the same danger.

The issue is not ethnicity, religious heritage, or the possession of spiritual privileges. It is faith. Paul has just explained, “Because of unbelief they were broken off, and you stand by faith. Do not be haughty, but fear” (==Romans 11:20==).

This is an extraordinary warning because it comes immediately after Paul has described the riches of God's grace extended to the Gentiles. We have been brought near, grafted into the cultivated olive tree, and made participants in promises that were originally given to Israel. Yet Paul refuses to allow the experience of grace to become the basis for superiority.

The very mercy that brought us into the tree ought to produce humility, not arrogance. If Israel, with all her extraordinary history of divine revelation, could stumble through unbelief, what grounds do we have for imagining that our religious institutions, theological traditions, or history of spiritual blessing guarantee our continued faithfulness?

Israel's history shows us that yesterday's revelation does not guarantee today's obedience.

We can possess sound doctrine, experience genuine movements of the Spirit, establish churches, build ministries, and develop an impressive religious history while gradually losing the humble dependence upon God that ought to characterize His people.

The people who have received the greatest mercy should be the least inclined toward boasting.
