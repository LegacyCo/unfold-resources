## Commentary
"And I looked, and behold, in the midst of the throne... stood a Lamb as though it had been slain."

John has just been told to look for a Lion (==Revelation 5:5==). He turns—and sees a Lamb. And not a lamb restored to unblemished perfection, but one still bearing the marks of slaughter, standing at the very center of the throne of heaven.

The elder announces the Lion of Judah who has *conquered*. What John sees is a Lamb who was *slain*. Heaven's answer to the question "How did the Lion conquer?" is: by being the Lamb. He didn't overpower His enemies; He was overpowered by them, and in that very act broke their power. Isaiah saw it seven centuries earlier—"He was led as a lamb to the slaughter" (==Isaiah 53:7==)—and John the Baptist named Him by it before He'd done a single miracle: "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!" (==John 1:29==). The victory of God looks like a slaughtered Lamb. That is not a temporary strategy. That's a revelation of who He is.

The crucifixion was not just the way He died; it is His essential character, *forever*. He rose with His scars intact and showed them to Thomas (==John 20:27==). He reigns in glory as the slain Lamb. Samuel Whitefield put it this way: "The stigma of crucifixion became the glory of love. He is forever the Crucified One, and He has the scars to prove it."

In fact, John says elsewhere that He is "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" (==Revelation 13:8==). The cross happened at a point in history, but the *Crucified One* precedes history. Before there was a Calvary, before there was a garden, before there was a world to fall—self-giving love was already who God is. The cross didn't change God's character. It revealed it.

Which means if you want to know what God is like, look at the cross. Whitefield again: "Before the cross, aspects of God's nature were hidden by His glory. On the cross, He was fully exposed for the first time in eternity." The cross is not the exception to God's character. It's the clearest window into it. Self-giving love, all the way down, in the middle of the throne.

The wounded One is the center-point of heaven's government. As John points out, the Lamb is "in the midst of the throne"—at its center. Every judgment, every promise, every future in the book of Revelation flows out from a throne with a slain Lamb at its heart, which is why even the wrath of God is called "the wrath of the Lamb" (==Revelation 6:16==), and why the New Jerusalem needs no sun, because "the Lamb is its light" (==Revelation 21:23==). And the Lamb has "seven horns and seven eyes"—fullness of power, fullness of sight. All authority and all knowledge, carried in a body that still shows where the nails went. Power without wounds becomes tyranny. Wounds without power become mere tragedy. Only here are they joined.

And that's what heaven worships. The song around the throne is not "Worthy is the Lion who conquered" but "Worthy is the Lamb who was slain" (==Revelation 5:12==). 

## Word Study
- *Lamb* : **arnion** (Greek) — a little lamb; a diminutive form. This is John's chosen word for Jesus throughout Revelation—28 times—and it appears only once anywhere else in the New Testament (==John 21:15==, "feed My lambs"). Not the imposing *amnos* of ==John 1:29==, but the small, tender word. The most powerful figure in the book is described with the gentlest noun in the language.
- *slain* : **sphazō** (Greek) — to slaughter, butcher; the word for cutting the throat of a sacrificial animal. And the tense matters: it's a perfect participle—"having been slain"—describing a past act whose results continue. Grammatically, He is not the Lamb who *was once* slain and recovered. He is the Lamb who was slain and *remains* the slain One. The wounds are present tense.

Summary: the smallest word for the strongest Person, wearing a wound that healed us for eternity (==Isaiah 53:5==).

## Application
Spend some time this week deliberately looking at the cross as a *revelation of God's nature*, not just a transaction for your sins. Read the crucifixion account slowly (==John 19==) and ask a different question than usual: not "what did this do for me?" but "what does this show me about Him?" That question will change how you approach the throne—because the One seated on it still bears the marks of how much He loves you.
