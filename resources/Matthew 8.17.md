## Commentary
Matthew wants us to understand that the healings of Jesus are not merely demonstrations of compassion or displays of supernatural power. They are signs that something Israel had been waiting for was beginning to happen.

Matthew has just described Jesus healing Peter’s mother-in-law (==Matthew 8:14–15==). Then, when evening comes, people bring to Him “many who were demon-possessed,” and Jesus casts out the spirits “with a word” and heals “all who were sick” (==Matthew 8:16==).

Matthew immediately tells us how to interpret what we are seeing: “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet, saying: ‘He Himself took our infirmities and bore our sicknesses’” (==Matthew 8:17==).

The quotation comes from ==Isaiah 53:4==, part of Isaiah’s great prophecy of the suffering Servant. Matthew sees the healing ministry of Jesus as a fulfillment of what Isaiah anticipated the Servant would accomplish.

==Isaiah 53== belongs to a much larger vision in Isaiah of Israel’s coming restoration and the arrival of God’s salvation.

Isaiah had promised a day when “the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped,” when “the lame shall leap like a deer” and “the tongue of the dumb sing” (==Isaiah 35:5–6==). Later, the Servant is commissioned “to open blind eyes” and “to bring out prisoners from the prison” (==Isaiah 42:6–7==). And in ==Isaiah 61:1==, the Spirit-anointed Servant announces good news to the poor, healing for the brokenhearted, liberty to the captives, and the opening of the prison to those who are bound.

These promises belong to the prophetic expectation that God was going to act decisively to rescue, restore, forgive, and renew His people—the hope that ultimately finds its fulfillment in the new covenant.

Jeremiah describes that covenant as one in which God would write His law upon His people’s hearts, become their God, and “forgive their iniquity” (==Jeremiah 31:31–34==). Ezekiel describes the same coming restoration as God cleansing His people, giving them a new heart, putting His Spirit within them, and causing them to walk in His ways (==Ezekiel 36:25–27==).

So when Jesus begins healing the sick, Matthew recognizes that these are not isolated miracles. _They are signs that the promised age of restoration has arrived._

This becomes even clearer later when John the Baptist sends messengers asking Jesus, “Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?” (==Matthew 11:3==). Jesus does not simply answer yes. He tells them: “Go and tell John the things which you hear and see: The blind see and the lame walk; the lepers are cleansed and the deaf hear; the dead are raised up and the poor have the gospel preached to them” (==Matthew 11:4–5==).

Jesus points to the prophetic signs of restoration because _His works identify the age that has arrived in Him._

The kingdom is here. The promised restoration has begun.

And this makes Matthew’s inclusion of demons in ==Matthew 8:16== particularly interesting.

Matthew says Jesus “cast out the spirits with a word, and healed all who were sick,” and then explains _both_ with ==Isaiah 53:4==.

For Matthew, healing bodies and driving out demons belong to the same larger reality. Jesus has come to undo everything that holds humanity beneath the power of sin, death, sickness, corruption, and the kingdom of darkness.

This does not mean sickness and demonization are the same thing. Matthew clearly distinguishes them. Jesus casts out the spirits _and_ heals the sick. But he places both under the same announcement: _the Servant has come to bear humanity’s brokenness._

This is the scope of the salvation Scripture had anticipated.

Sin is forgiven.

The unclean are cleansed.

The sick are healed.

The oppressed are delivered.

The dead are raised.

The Spirit is given.

Humanity is being restored.

And all of it is centered in Jesus.

There is also something important in Matthew’s wording. Isaiah says the Servant _takes_ our infirmities and _bears_ our sicknesses. Jesus does not heal from a safe distance as though human suffering were beneath Him. The One who heals is also the One who bears.

That trajectory eventually leads to the cross.

The healings of ==Matthew 8== are therefore previews of something much larger than temporary physical relief. Every fever that leaves, every diseased body that becomes whole, every demon that releases its captive is an announcement that the Servant has entered humanity’s broken condition and has begun carrying it away.

This is why healing belongs to the hope of the new covenant. Salvation is not ultimately God rescuing disembodied souls from creation. It is God reclaiming what sin and death have damaged. The new covenant begins that restoration now through forgiveness, the indwelling Spirit, healing, deliverance, and new life, while pointing forward to the day when the work will be complete and “there shall be no more death, nor sorrow, nor crying” and “there shall be no more pain” (==Revelation 21:4==).

We still live in the tension between what has begun and what has not yet been completed. Even those who experience healing now remain mortal. The New Testament itself contains faithful believers who experience sickness (==Philippians 2:25–27==; ==1 Timothy 5:23==; ==2 Timothy 4:20==). So ==Matthew 8:17== should not be reduced to a formula that every sickness must presently disappear if enough faith is exercised.

But neither should we move healing outside the work of Christ. Matthew does the opposite. He looks at Jesus healing diseases and driving out demons and says, in effect: _This is what the prophets were talking about._

The Servant has come. The kingdom has arrived. The promised restoration has begun. And wherever Jesus makes broken things whole, we are seeing a preview of where His new covenant is ultimately taking creation.

## Application
When Jesus healed the sick and cast out demons, He was giving the world a glimpse of where His kingdom is ultimately going. Sickness, oppression, pain, and death do not belong to what God has promised His people.

That means we should never become comfortable with brokenness simply because we still live in a broken world.

We pray for healing because healing belongs to the kingdom Jesus brought near. We pray for deliverance because darkness does not have the final claim on anyone who belongs to Christ. And when healing does not come in the way or timing we hoped, we continue trusting Jesus because the promise has not changed: _what He began will one day be completed._

Every healing now is a preview. Every deliverance is a signpost. Every restored life points toward the day when Christ will finally make all things new (==Revelation 21:4–5==).

So when you encounter sickness or brokenness, don't just ask, _“How do I endure this?”_ There is certainly grace for endurance. But also ask, _“What does the kingdom of Jesus have to say about this?”_

Bring what is broken to Him. Ask Him to heal. Ask Him to restore. Ask Him to drive back what does not belong.

Because the Jesus who “took our infirmities and bore our sicknesses” (==Matthew 8:17==) is still the One in whom all of God’s promises of restoration find their fulfillment.
