## Commentary
“Faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen.” We usually apply that to things we can’t *see*. But it applies just as much to things we can’t *understand*. Obedience in the absence of understanding is what faith actually looks like—the evidence of things that can’t be seen, or explained.

Whenever I make understanding a precursor to obedience, the result is a God made in my image—a God who makes sense to me, whose commands always come with reasons I approve of. My obedience will have explanations. But when a person is open and obedient to a divine initiative that doesn’t make sense to them, something different is revealed: a God bigger than their categories, and a servant who trusts the Master more than the map. Scripture’s hall of faith is full of exactly this—Noah building for a flood no one had seen (==Hebrews 11:7==), Abraham going out “not knowing where he was going” (==Hebrews 11:8==).

We never get to put God on trial. So many of us withhold our obedience until He explains Himself—but we don’t have that right. God doesn’t owe me an explanation. If He gives me a reason, a purpose, or a promise along the way, that’s great. All I actually need to know is what He’s told me to do. I can’t hold my obedience hostage to my understanding.

And here’s what makes this more than a discipline—it’s actually the doorway to more revelation, not less. As Robert Coleman observed in *The Master Plan of Evangelism*, “Obedience to Christ was the very means by which His disciples learned more truth.” Jesus speaks in proportion to our willingness to respond (==Mark 4:24-25==). Understanding is nice. But in the kingdom, understanding is not nearly as valuable as obedience—and obedience is frequently how understanding finally arrives.
