## Commentary
“But they all with one accord began to make excuses. The first said to him, ‘I have bought a piece of ground, and I must go and see it. I ask you to have me excused.’”

Look closely at the three excuses, because none of them involve anything sinful. A piece of ground to inspect. Five yoke of oxen to test. A new marriage (==Luke 14:19-20==). Property, work, family—three of the most legitimate concerns a person can have. Nobody declined the supper for something shameful. They declined it for their ordinary lives. That’s what makes the parable cut so close: the invitations of God are rarely refused for evil. They’re refused for schedules.

Notice also the courtesy. “I ask you to have me excused”—twice. These are polite refusals, respectful in tone, reasonable in content, and they all arrive at the same moment: “at supper time,” when “all things are now ready” (==Luke 14:17==). The feast wasn’t declined in advance; it was declined when it was time to actually come. And the master’s verdict lands on the politeness and the reasonableness without softening at all: “none of those men who were invited shall taste my supper” (==Luke 14:24==). An excused seat is still an empty seat. The supper went on—filled with the poor, the maimed, the lame, and the blind (==Luke 14:21==)—but the original guests missed it, not because they were barred, but because something ordinary mattered more at supper time.

Jesus told this parable in response to a comfortable remark at the table: “Blessed is he who shall eat bread in the kingdom of God!” (==Luke 14:15==). The man assumed being invited was the same as attending. The parable answers: the blessing doesn’t belong to those invited to the feast, but to those who actually come when the servant says it’s ready. God’s invitations arrive at supper time—at inconvenient moments, in the middle of real estate and oxen and family life—and every one of them can be politely declined. Just know what the polite decline costs.
