## Commentary
“Please say you are my sister, that it may be well with me for your sake, and that I may live because of you.”

The first consequence of stepping outside God's will is recorded immediately: Abraham lied. Had God not told him, “I will bless you and make your name great”? Had God not said, “I will bless those who bless you, and I will curse him who curses you” (==Genesis 12:2-3==)? A man carrying those promises had nothing to fear from Pharaoh. But this is what happens when a person moves from a place of fear—he forgets the promises God has given him. Out of God's will, Abraham moved from trusting to scheming, from confidence to fear, from considering others to considering himself, and from bringing blessing to bringing judgment on others—the LORD plagued Pharaoh's house because of Sarai (==Genesis 12:17==).

The cost ran in every direction. Sarai, asked to join the deception, was left vulnerable and unprotected; “the woman was taken to Pharaoh's house,” and Pharaoh's own words later—“I took her as a wife” (==Genesis 12:19==)—show how far Abraham's scheming exposed her. The lie itself became a family trait: Abraham repeated it with Abimelech (==Genesis 20:2==), and even his son Isaac told the identical lie about Rebekah a generation later (==Genesis 26:7==). Even the wealth looked like gain and worked like a burden: the livestock acquired in Egypt fed the strife that separated him from Lot (==Genesis 13:6-7==), which placed Lot in Sodom and dragged Abraham into a war to rescue him (==Genesis 14:12-16==). And Egypt supplied one more thing—an Egyptian maidservant named Hagar (==Genesis 16:1-3==), whose story would bring grief into the household for years. Everything about Egypt, and everything received in Egypt, became a problem. One fearful decision does not usually stay one decision; it compounds.
