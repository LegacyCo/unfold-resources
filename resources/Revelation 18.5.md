## Commentary
“For her sins have reached to heaven, and God has remembered her iniquities.”

The builders of Babel set out to raise a tower “whose top is in the heavens” (==Genesis 11:4==), and God stopped them. Here, at the end of the story, Babylon finally reaches heaven—not by her tower but by her sins. What the people of Shinar could not accomplish with brick and asphalt, later generations accomplished with the accumulated rebellion of their lives. That is what sin does. It constructs something—a city of rebellion so foul that it becomes “a dwelling place of demons, a prison for every foul spirit” (==Revelation 18:2==)—and it builds it high enough to reach heaven and be remembered there. The verse ends with the same divine remembering that brought the flood and delivered Israel; here it is remembering unto judgment.

Righteousness builds a city too. Those made righteous by the blood of the Lamb know that the testimony of their lives is building a different city—not one for this age, but one built for eternity. Like Abraham, they live as sojourners who are not at home here, waiting for “the city which has foundations, whose builder and maker is God” (==Hebrews 11:10==). If the sin of the world builds up Babylon until it reaches heaven, then the righteous lives of God's sons and daughters are being built up into the holy city that comes down from heaven (==Revelation 21:2==). This is why the voice from heaven says, “Come out of her, my people, lest you share in her sins” (==Revelation 18:4==). There are only two building projects in the earth, and every life contributes to one of them.

## Application
Ask which city your life is building. Every act of pride, self-reliance, and compromise is a brick laid in Babylon; every act of humility, forgiveness, and obedience to the way of the cross is a living stone set in the heavenly Jerusalem. Take one area where you have been building with the world's materials—the way you handle offense, the way you define rest or success—and deliberately rebuild it according to the heavenly pattern. Bricks for Babylon or stones for Jerusalem: there is no third city.
