## Commentary
It’s one of the most startling sentences in the Bible: “So the LORD relented from the harm which He said He would do to His people.”

The context makes it more startling still. Israel had just rejected God at Sinai and built the golden calf. God told Moses His wrath would consume them, and He would start over—making Moses into a great nation (==Exodus 32:10==). And Moses *pleaded*. He stood between a rebellious people and a righteous God, appealed to God’s reputation among the nations and His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (==Exodus 32:13==)—and verse 14 says God changed His mind.

It happens again in Jonah. Nineveh received a message of judgment with a date on it, repented in sackcloth, fasted, and cried out—and “God relented from the disaster that He had said He would bring upon them” (==Jonah 3:10==). Same Hebrew word as Exodus 32:14.

Now think about what those two stories have in common: in both cases, it was the prayers of *rebellious* people—or prayers on their behalf—that moved the heart of God to change the course of what He was planning to do. How much more will a people who love Him, and are surrendered to Him, move His heart in prayer?

This isn’t a picture of an unstable God. It’s a picture of a relational one—a God who has humbled Himself to work in cooperation with a praying people, and who builds *into His plans* the response He intends to make to intercession (==Ezekiel 22:30==). Prayer is not a formality performed in front of a decision already sealed. It’s a genuine participation in what God does in the earth. Heaven has real space in it for your asking.

## Word Study
- *relented* : **nacham** — to be moved with sorrow or compassion; to relent, console, change one’s course. Remarkably, it’s from the same root family as *comfort*—the word behind “Comfort, yes, comfort My people” (==Isaiah 40:1==).

Other places *nacham* appears:

- ==Jonah 3:10== — God “relented” over Nineveh when the city cried out.
- ==Psalm 106:45== — “He remembered His covenant for their sake, and *relented* according to the multitude of His mercies.”

Summary: when Scripture says God “relented,” it isn’t describing a God who was talked out of His character—it’s describing a God whose character *is* to be moved. Prayer doesn’t bend God away from who He is. It appeals to exactly who He is: rich in mercy, willing to be moved.
