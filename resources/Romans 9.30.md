## Commentary
“What shall we say then? That Gentiles, who did not pursue righteousness, have attained to righteousness, even the righteousness of faith.”

After spending much of Romans 9 explaining God's sovereign purposes, Paul now turns his attention to the remarkable way those purposes are being fulfilled. And what he describes is something that would have seemed almost unimaginable to many of his Jewish readers.

The Gentiles, who had lived outside of Israel's covenant, who had not received the Law of Moses, and who had not spent generations pursuing righteousness through obedience to its commandments, were now being declared righteous before God. Meanwhile, many within Israel, who had devoted themselves to the pursuit of righteousness, had failed to obtain what they were seeking.

How could those who were not pursuing righteousness obtain it, while those who were pursuing it failed to receive it?

The answer is found in the final words of the verse: _the righteousness of faith._

The Greek word translated "pursue" is _diōkō_, which means to chase after, seek earnestly, or press toward something. It describes an intentional pursuit of a desired goal. The word translated "attained" is _katalambanō_, meaning to take hold of, obtain, or grasp something. Paul is deliberately contrasting those who had been pursuing righteousness with those who had actually obtained it.

But we need to understand what Paul means by righteousness.

Throughout Romans, Paul has been explaining that restoration with God cannot be secured through human achievement or obedience to the Law. It is something God graciously provides through Jesus Christ and which we receive by faith (==Romans 3:21–26==).

This does not mean that righteousness has nothing to do with how we live. Paul has already explained that those who have been justified by faith are also brought into a new relationship with God, united with Christ in His death and resurrection, and called to walk in newness of life (==Romans 6:1–11==). But we do not become righteous before God because we have successfully transformed our behavior. Our transformation flows out of the new relationship with God that we have received through Christ.

This is the distinction Paul is making between Israel and the Gentiles.

Israel had received the Law, the covenants, the promises, and the worship of the one true God. These were extraordinary privileges, as Paul has already acknowledged in ==Romans 9:4–5==. Yet many within Israel had come to approach righteousness as something that could be established through their relationship to the Law and their obedience to its commandments.

Paul explains the problem in the following verses: "Because they did not seek it by faith, but as it were, by the works of the law" (==Romans 9:32==).

Notice that Paul is not condemning Israel for pursuing righteousness. Their desire to be right with God was not the problem. The problem was the *means* by which they sought to obtain it.

In fact, Paul will say in the following chapter, "For I bear them witness that they have a zeal for God, but not according to knowledge" (==Romans 10:2==). Their zeal was genuine, but their understanding of righteousness was incomplete. They were attempting to establish their own righteousness rather than submitting to the righteousness God had provided in Christ (==Romans 10:3–4==).

This is especially significant when we remember that the Law itself was never intended to replace faith in God. Long before the Law was given through Moses, Abraham believed God, and his faith was accounted to him for righteousness (==Genesis 15:6==).

Paul has already devoted an entire chapter to this point. Abraham was declared righteous before he was circumcised and centuries before the Law was given. His standing before God rested upon his faith in what God had promised, not upon his ability to accomplish that promise through his own strength (==Romans 4:1–25==).

So the righteousness now being received by believing Gentiles was not a new standard God had introduced after Israel failed. It was consistent with the way God had always intended His promise to be received.

What was new was the fulfillment of that promise in Jesus Christ, through whom people from every nation were now being welcomed into the family of God.

This is where the argument Paul has been crafting comes together.

God had promised Abraham that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him (==Genesis 12:3==). He had chosen Israel as the nation through whom that promise would ultimately be fulfilled. And now, through Jesus Christ, the blessing promised to Abraham was reaching the Gentiles, not through their conversion to Judaism or their observance of the Mosaic Law, but through faith in the Messiah.

Those who had once been strangers to the covenants of promise were now being brought near through the blood of Christ (==Ephesians 2:11–13==).

Paul is not suggesting that every Gentile had received righteousness or that every Jew had rejected it. Paul himself was a Jew who had received the righteousness that comes through faith in Christ. Rather, he is describing the stunning reality that Gentiles were entering into the blessings promised to Israel while many within Israel were stumbling over the very Messiah through whom those promises were being fulfilled.

The Gentiles had obtained something they could never have produced for themselves. And the tragedy of Israel's unbelief was that many were still pursuing through their own efforts what God was freely offering through His Son.
