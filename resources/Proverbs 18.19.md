## Commentary
“A brother offended is harder to win than a strong city, and contentions are like the bars of a castle.”

Winning back an offended brother is siege warfare. That’s Solomon’s actual comparison—not a difficult conversation, not an awkward season, but the taking of a fortified city. Once offense settles in, the contentions between you become “like the bars of a castle”: gates locked from the inside, walls manned, every approach watched. Anyone who has tried to repair a ruptured friendship knows the accuracy of the picture. Arguments that once would have blown over are now re-read as evidence. Apologies get inspected for loopholes. The person who used to give you the benefit of the doubt now gives it to no one—least of all you.

That’s why wisdom puts its energy on the front end. “The beginning of strife is like releasing water; therefore stop contention before a quarrel starts” (==Proverbs 17:14==). Water just released can still be dammed; an hour downstream, it cannot. The moment to address a brewing conflict is when it’s still small enough to feel like an overreaction to address it—because the alternative is the siege. Jesus put a clock on it: leave your gift at the altar and go; be reconciled quickly, while you’re still on the way (==Matthew 5:23-25==). Paul gave it a sunset: don’t let the day end on the anger (==Ephesians 4:26==).

And when the walls have already gone up? Sieges are won by patience, not force. You can’t argue a fortified brother open—but consistent love, humility, and time can do what battering rams can’t. Keep showing up. Own your part without litigating his. Let peace do the slow work: “the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace” (==James 3:18==). Cities that can’t be stormed can still open their gates from the inside—usually to someone who never stopped standing outside them.

## Application
Two assignments come out of this verse. First, prevention: name the conflict in your life that’s still small—the comment that landed wrong, the tension that hasn’t been spoken yet—and address it this week, while the water can still be dammed. Don’t wait until it feels big enough to justify the conversation; by then the walls are up. Second, the siege you’re already in: if there’s an offended brother or sister with the gates locked against you, stop trying to argue your way in. Own your part, without a footnote about theirs. Then commit to the long game—the birthday text, the prayer you don’t tell them about, the consistent kindness that expects nothing back. Ask God for one thing: to keep your own heart free of offense while you wait outside the walls.
