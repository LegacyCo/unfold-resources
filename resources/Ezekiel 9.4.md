## Commentary
“And the LORD said to him, ‘Go through the midst of the city, through the midst of Jerusalem, and put a mark on the foreheads of the men who sigh and cry over all the abominations that are done within it.’”

Ezekiel has just been shown the extraordinary corruption taking place in Jerusalem, including within the temple itself. In the previous chapter, God revealed the idolatry of the elders, the worship of false gods, and the desecration of the very place where His presence was intended to dwell. Now, in chapter 9, judgment is about to fall upon the city. Six men appear with weapons of destruction, accompanied by another man clothed in linen who carries a writer's inkhorn.

But before judgment begins, God gives an important instruction. The man clothed in linen is commanded to move throughout Jerusalem and place a mark on the foreheads of certain individuals. These are the people who will be spared from the destruction about to take place.

What is remarkable is how God identifies them. He doesn't describe them by their social position, religious office, tribal affiliation, or outward association with the temple. He identifies them by their response to the evil taking place around them. They are those who _sigh and cry_ over the abominations being committed in Jerusalem.

The Hebrew words translated “sigh” and “cry” describe deep expressions of grief and distress. These people saw the corruption around them and were deeply troubled by it. They were grieved by the things that grieve the heart of God.

This is especially significant because the sins being committed in Jerusalem had become so widespread that they were even being practiced by the religious leaders. In ==Ezekiel 8:12==, God reveals the elders worshiping idols in secret, convinced that He neither sees nor cares about their actions. The people marked in chapter 9 were living in the same city and surrounded by the same corruption, but their hearts had not become indifferent to it.

There is an important distinction here between recognizing sin and grieving over it. It is possible to identify what is wrong without being particularly troubled by it. We can become accustomed to things that Scripture clearly identifies as contrary to God's character, especially when those things become accepted within the culture around us. But the people God identifies in this passage had retained a sensitivity to His holiness.

This kind of grief is not moral outrage or frustration with the behavior of others. It comes from loving God and being deeply troubled by what dishonors Him. It should produce humility, prayer, repentance, and intercession rather than self-righteousness or contempt for the people involved.

There is also something fascinating about the mark itself. The Hebrew word translated “mark” is _tav_ (תָּו), which is also the name of the final letter of the Hebrew alphabet. In the ancient Hebrew script used during Ezekiel's time, this letter could be written in a form resembling an X or a cross (+). This has led some interpreters to suggest that the man clothed in linen was instructed to draw a cross-shaped symbol on the foreheads of those who grieved over Jerusalem's sin.

While the passage does not explicitly describe the shape of the mark, the connection is historically plausible. What Ezekiel describes is a prophetic vision in which God identifies those who belong to Him before judgment begins. In ==Ezekiel 9:6==, the executioners are specifically commanded not to come near anyone bearing this mark.

Early Christian writers, including Origen and Tertullian, recognized the resemblance between the ancient _tav_ and the cross of Christ. They understood the marking of the faithful in Ezekiel's vision as a foreshadowing of the salvation that would ultimately come through Jesus. 

This imagery also recalls the Passover in ==Exodus 12:7–13==, when the blood placed upon the doorposts of the Israelites' homes distinguished them from the Egyptians and protected them from the judgment that passed through the land.

It also anticipates the imagery of ==Revelation 7:2–3==, where an angel commands that the servants of God be sealed on their foreheads before judgment proceeds. In both visions, God distinguishes those who belong to Him from those who have persisted in rebellion.

But notice something else. The people in Ezekiel's vision were marked _before_ judgment began. God knew exactly who they were, even when they were surrounded by a community that had largely abandoned Him. Their faithfulness had not escaped His attention.

This should encourage us. God is never confused about who belongs to Him. Even in seasons of widespread spiritual compromise, He recognizes those whose hearts remain devoted to Him. Paul makes a similar statement in ==2 Timothy 2:19==: “The Lord knows those who are His.”

The question this passage raises is whether our hearts remain sensitive to the things that grieve God. We live in a world where repeated exposure to sin can gradually dull our consciences. Things that once troubled us can become familiar, and eventually we may stop noticing them altogether.
