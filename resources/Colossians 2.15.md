## Commentary
“When He had disarmed the rulers and authorities, He made a public display of them, having triumphed over them.”

The word translated “disarmed” is *apekdyomai*—literally, to undress. Jesus stripped the devil. And the scene Paul paints next is military conquest language his readers knew well. When a Roman general won a battle, he returned to his city in a triumphal procession: the conquered enemies, stripped of weapons and armor, paraded through the streets behind the conquering king. It was the most humiliating spectacle imaginable for a warrior. The point wasn’t just that they lost. The point was that *everyone could see* they lost. No one in that city went to sleep afraid of the enemy that night. They had watched their king march him down the main street.

That’s the picture the cross gives us—like David holding up Goliath’s head (==1 Samuel 17:51==). The gospel is not a story of two equal powers locked in conflict, where the outcome hangs in suspense. Jesus is the fullness of Deity in bodily form, head over every ruler and authority (==Colossians 2:9-10==). The enemy isn’t fighting to attain victory. He’s already lost, and he knows it. He’s a liar—he’s only ever been a liar (==John 8:44==)—and the only fight left in him is the fight to make you believe a lie.

## Application
Have you allowed yourself to *see* Jesus as the conquering King over every enemy? Not just believe it as doctrine—see it. Fear survives on obscured vision: it keeps your eyes on the enemy’s threats instead of on the procession. Next time fear or accusation comes knocking, answer it with the spectacle Paul describes: the powers stripped, paraded, and publicly shamed at the cross. You don’t face an undefeated enemy. You face an exposed one.
