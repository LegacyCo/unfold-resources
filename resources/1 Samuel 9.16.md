## Commentary
Saul thought he was out looking for his father's lost donkeys (==1 Samuel 9:3==). He had no idea that the day before, God had told Samuel, "Tomorrow about this time I will send you a man." Saul didn't go looking for a calling. The calling came looking for him.

And God told Samuel exactly what Saul would do for God's people: "he will save My people from the hand of the Philistines." That was the word of the Lord over Saul's life.

The Philistines had crushed Israel's army and carried off the ark of God (==1 Samuel 4:10–11==). By Saul's day they had disarmed the nation so completely that there wasn't a blacksmith left in the land, and when the battle finally came, only Saul and Jonathan had swords (==1 Samuel 13:19–22==).

The end of the verse tells us why God was sending a deliverer: "For I have looked upon My people, because their cry has come to Me." That's the language of the burning bush. "I have surely seen the oppression of My people... the cry of the children of Israel has come to Me" (==Exodus 3:7, 9==). The God who saw His people in Egypt was seeing them again, and once again He was sending someone.

God says "My people" three times in this one verse, and He says it right after Israel had demanded a king "like all the nations" (==1 Samuel 8:5==), and God told Samuel, "they have rejected Me, that I should not reign over them" (==1 Samuel 8:7==). They had rejected Him, but He hadn't let go of them. They were still His people, and their cry still reached Him.

But as you read through to the end of Saul's story, you find that the Philistines overrun Israel. Saul's sons are killed, Philistine archers wound Saul badly, and he falls on his own sword (==1 Samuel 31:1–6==). The man anointed to *save* Israel from the Philistines *died* at the hands of the Philistines.

So what happened? It started at Gilgal, the first time the Philistines came against Saul in full force. Samuel had told Saul to wait seven days for him there (==1 Samuel 10:8==). Saul watched his army slip away while he waited, got afraid, and offered the sacrifice himself (==1 Samuel 13:8–10==). When Samuel arrived, Saul explained, "When I saw that the people were scattered from me... I felt compelled, and offered a burnt offering" (==1 Samuel 13:11–12==). *When I saw.* Samuel responded and told Saul the Lord "would have established your kingdom over Israel forever" (==1 Samuel 13:13==). The word of the Lord over Saul's life was true, but Saul trusted what he could see more than what God had said. Fear turned into disobedience, and disobedience turned into a pattern (==1 Samuel 15:22–23==).

Even the great Philistine rout of Saul's reign didn't come through Saul. He was sitting under a pomegranate tree at the edge of Gibeah (==1 Samuel 14:2==) while Jonathan and his armor bearer climbed up to a Philistine garrison, convinced that "nothing restrains the LORD from saving by many or by few" (==1 Samuel 14:6==). The text says, "So the LORD saved Israel that day" (==1 Samuel 14:23==). And for the rest of Saul's life, "there was fierce war with the Philistines all the days of Saul" (==1 Samuel 14:52==).

Saul wasn't the last king to lose a promise this way. God told Jeroboam, "I will be with you and build for you an enduring house, as I built for David." But that promise began with a condition: "if you heed all that I command you" (==1 Kings 11:38==). Jeroboam made two golden calves instead, and the house God offered to build him was swept away (==1 Kings 14:10==). A word from God is never something we carry around apart from the God who spoke it.

God made this promise because He'd heard the cry of His people. So when Saul couldn't faithfully handle the promise, God didn't abandon His word. "The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart" (==1 Samuel 13:14==)... "a man after My own heart, who will do all My will" (==Acts 13:22==).

That man was David. His first public victory was over a Philistine champion (==1 Samuel 17==). Years later Abner reminded Israel's elders, "the LORD has spoken of David, saying, 'By the hand of My servant David, I will save My people Israel from the hand of the Philistines'" (==2 Samuel 3:18==). Same promise, almost word for word. Different man. And David accomplished the victory over the Philistines Saul never could (==2 Samuel 5:17–25==).

## Word Study
"Commander" (NKJV) or "leader" (NASB) translates the Hebrew *nagid*. Notice that God doesn't call Saul *melek*, "king," in this verse, even though Israel had asked for a king. A *nagid* is a leader God designates to go before His people under His authority. The same word is used for David (==1 Samuel 13:14==; ==2 Samuel 5:2==). Saul would wear the title of king, but in God's eyes he was always a leader serving under the true King. At Gilgal, Saul acted as if he were the one in command.

## Application
God's purposes for His people won't fail. The question is whether we'll keep walking with Him while He fulfills them. Saul had the word, the anointing, and at times even the Spirit's power (==1 Samuel 10:6–10==). What he didn't have was a heart that chose obedience to God, especially when what he could see was convincing him to take matters into his own hands.

You may be standing in your own Gilgal right now, holding on to something God has said while everything around you seems to be falling apart. Don't let what you see overrule what God has said. Keep waiting on Him, and when He speaks, do what He tells you. That's the kind of heart the Lord will not allow to fail.
