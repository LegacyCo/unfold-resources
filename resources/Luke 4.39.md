## Commentary
Peter’s mother-in-law is sick with a high fever. Jesus stands over her, rebukes the fever, and it leaves her—“and immediately she arose and *served* them.”

Don’t rush past the sequence, because it’s the whole pattern of kingdom ministry in one verse. Jesus touches her. Jesus changes her. And her immediate response is to turn and serve the people around her—the same way the King had just served her. Touched, changed, serving. That’s not a nice detail about a grateful woman. That’s the assembly line of the kingdom, and every believer has been down it.

Ask most Christians who they think are “in the ministry” and they’ll point to the pastor, the missionary, the person who left their job to go “full time.” The result is that the overwhelming majority of born-again believers don’t see themselves as ministers at all—they see themselves as the audience. But that split between ministers and members doesn’t exist in the Bible. If you have been changed by Jesus, you’re in the ministry. Everyone in this kingdom, by way of being touched by the King, is called, qualified, and commissioned to serve—which is why Paul says the fivefold gifts exist “for the equipping of *the saints* for the work of ministry” (==Ephesians 4:12==). The leaders equip; the saints do the ministry. And what does that ministry look like? “Through love serve one another” (==Galatians 5:13==)—at your job, on your street, in your home. The electrician and the salesman are as commissioned as the missionary.

## Word Study
- *served / ministry* : **diakoneō / diakonia** (Greek) — to serve, to wait on, to attend to; from *diakonos*, a servant. This is the exact word most Bibles translate “ministry” in ==Ephesians 4:12==—and the exact word used for what Peter’s mother-in-law did with dinner.

Watch the same word in both worlds:

- ==Luke 4:39== — she arose and *served* (diakoneō) them. We call that hospitality.
- ==Ephesians 4:12== — equipping the saints for the work of *ministry* (diakonia). We call that clergy.
- ==Matthew 20:26-28== — “whoever desires to become great among you, let him be your *servant* (diakonos)… just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to *serve*.”

Summary: the Bible uses one word where we use two. There is no Greek distinction between “minister” and “servant”—only an English one. Which means the question is never whether you’re called to the ministry. If the King has touched you, you already are.
