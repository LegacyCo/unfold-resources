## Commentary
There may be few statements in Scripture more heartbreaking than this:

“We do not see our signs; there is no longer any prophet; nor is there among us any who knows how long.”

The temple could still be standing while the signs of God’s presence were disappearing. The priests could still serve, the sacrifices could still be offered, the menorah could still be lit, and the people could still gather—but heaven had grown increasingly silent.

Jewish tradition describes a gradual withdrawal of the Shekhina. In the First Temple (built by Solomon), the presence of God was visibly evident throughout the house: His glory filled the house from the Holy of Holies, His fire descended upon the altar, and—according to later Jewish tradition—the enduring flame of the menorah testified that the Shekhina remained with Israel. 
In the Second Temple (built by Zerubbabel after the Babylonian exile), the Holy of Holies stood empty, the fire of God was absent from the altar, and only a remnant of the former signs remained. Tradition even records that during the final forty years before the temple’s destruction in AD 70, the western lamp no longer continued burning as it once had.

The signs disappeared one by one.

This physical emptiness was accompanied by another absence: the voice of prophecy. Haggai, Zechariah, and Malachi spoke during the period of Israel’s return from exile, but after them the prophetic voice fell silent. There was still Scripture, but there was no new word. Israel possessed what God had *said*, but no longer heard what God was *saying* through His servants the prophets.

That does not make the Torah unimportant. In fact, Jewish teachers have argued that this silence forced Israel to give greater attention to the smallest details of the Law. As the nation prepared for centuries of exile and dispersion, careful obedience to the Torah became one of the means by which its identity would be preserved. The people learned how to carry the Scriptures into lands where they could not carry the temple.

There is something admirable in that faithfulness—but there is also something heartbreaking about it.

They became increasingly skilled in the details of what God had spoken while living without the intimacy of His voice. They studied the words because the Voice had fallen silent. They guarded the commands because the visible signs of the Presence had disappeared. What preserved them in exile also testified to what they had lost.

This is the spiritual ache behind Psalm 74:9: “We do not see our signs; there is no longer any prophet.” It is possible to retain the forms of worship after the fire has gone out. It is possible to preserve the words of God while no longer recognizing the movement of God. It is possible for the house to remain standing while the Presence that gave the house its meaning is absent.

But the silence was not the end of the story.

The final prophetic voice of the Old Testament promised that the Lord whom Israel sought would suddenly come to His temple (==Malachi 3:1==). After generations of studying the written word, the living Word appeared among them. God did not merely send another prophet. He spoke through His Son (==Hebrews 1:1-2==). The Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and once again humanity beheld the glory of God (==John 1:14==).

The tragedy is that many had become so accustomed to preserving yesterday’s revelation that they could not recognize the Presence when He stood in front of them.
