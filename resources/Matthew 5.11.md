## Commentary
Corrie ten Boom lived a quiet, simple life of devotion to Jesus above her father’s watch shop in Holland. Then the Nazis occupied her country, and that simple devotion suddenly became dangerous. Moved by compassion and conviction, her family built a secret room behind a false wall in her bedroom and helped rescue hundreds of Jews from deportation. We love stories like that in hindsight—but Corrie didn’t get to obey God in hindsight. She had to decide, in real time, whether following Jesus was worth it even if it cost her. And it cost her. In February 1944 the Gestapo raided the house. Her father died shortly after imprisonment. Corrie and her sister Betsie were sent to Ravensbrück, and Betsie never came home.

That story presses on the assumption many of us carry: that obedience places us on the smooth path—a fast-track to blessing and open doors. But that isn’t the gospel Jesus preached. “Narrow is the gate and difficult is the way which leads to life” (==Matthew 7:14==). Jesus didn’t die so we don’t have to; He died to show us the way of a resurrected life. And in this beatitude He goes further still: “Blessed are you when they revile and persecute you, and say all kinds of evil against you falsely for My sake. Rejoice and be exceedingly glad, for great is your reward in heaven.” Conflict *because of Him* isn’t evidence that the blessed life has broken down. It’s evidence you’re living it—in the company of the prophets who came before you. Scripture is consistent here: all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will suffer persecution (==2 Timothy 3:12==). Even Abraham obeyed, walked a thousand miles—and arrived in a famine (==Genesis 12:10==). Obedience often costs us before it rewards us.

But the reward is real, and sometimes God lets us taste it on this side of heaven. Years after the war, Corrie was speaking in a Munich church about the forgiveness of God when a man approached, hand extended: “You mentioned Ravensbrück in your message. I was a guard there.” And he asked her forgiveness. Corrie later said the blood ran cold in her veins—she couldn’t do it. Then she remembered what the Lord had taught her through the pain: forgiveness is not an emotion; it’s an act of obedience. She prayed, “Jesus, help me,” reached out her hand—and warmth flooded through her. “I forgive you, brother! With all my heart!”

In that moment Corrie discovered that her suffering hadn’t taken something from her. God had used the pain to *form* something in her. She forgave because obedience had formed Jesus in her—and where her own power ended, His moved through her weakness. That’s the arithmetic of this beatitude: what obedience costs, reward outweighs. Rejoice and be exceedingly glad.
