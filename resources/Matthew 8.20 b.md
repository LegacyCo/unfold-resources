## Commentary
At first, Jesus’ response seems strange. A scribe approaches Him and says, “Teacher, I will follow You wherever You go” (==Matthew 8:19==). That sounds like exactly the kind of commitment Jesus should welcome.

Instead, Jesus responds: “Foxes have holes and birds of the air have nests, but the Son of Man has nowhere to lay His head” (==Matthew 8:20==).

Jesus is not rejecting the man. He is making sure the man understands what he has just volunteered for.

The scribe says, _“I will follow You wherever You go.”_

Jesus essentially responds, _“Do you understand where I am going?”_

Foxes have dens. Birds have nests. Even wild animals have places of belonging and security. But Jesus describes Himself as having no settled place to call His own. Following Him therefore cannot be separated from His own life of displacement, rejection, and dependence upon the Father.

This is especially significant because the man approaching Jesus is a _scribe_. Scribes were educated interpreters of the Law and occupied a respected place within Jewish religious society. Matthew may be deliberately placing a socially established man before an unsettled Messiah. Jesus is warning him that following Him may cost precisely the kinds of security and status he has known.

But there is another important detail here. This is the first time in Matthew that Jesus refers to Himself as _“the Son of Man.”_

That title will become Jesus’ favorite way of referring to Himself throughout the Gospel. On one level, “son of man” can simply mean a human being. But Jesus’ use of the title also reaches toward ==Daniel 7:13–14==, where Daniel sees “One like the Son of Man” coming with the clouds of heaven and receiving everlasting dominion, glory, and a kingdom from the Ancient of Days.

That makes Jesus’ statement wonderfully paradoxical.

The Son of Man who will inherit _every kingdom_ presently has nowhere to sleep.

The One to whom “all peoples, nations, and languages” will ultimately serve (==Daniel 7:14==) is presently living without the ordinary securities even animals possess.

Matthew is beginning to show us what kind of King Jesus is.

His kingdom does not arrive through earthly status, comfort, or power. The road to glory runs through humility, rejection, suffering, and eventually the cross. Later Jesus will say that “the Son of Man” will be betrayed, condemned, mocked, scourged, and crucified (==Matthew 20:18–19==).

So when this man says, “I will follow You wherever You go,” Jesus forces him to confront what _wherever_ actually means.

Following Jesus is not adding Him to an otherwise unchanged life. It means going where He goes—even when following Him leads away from comfort, security, recognition, or control.

Jesus is not discouraging discipleship. He is refusing to advertise it falsely. Before the man follows Him, Jesus wants him to understand the cost.
