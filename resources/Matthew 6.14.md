## Commentary
“For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.”

Jesus has just finished teaching His disciples how to pray (==Matthew 6:9-13==). The prayer ends—and instead of moving to a new subject, Jesus circles back to one line of it and expands it. Out of everything in that prayer—the hallowed name, the coming kingdom, the daily bread, the deliverance from evil—the only petition He returns to explain is forgiveness: forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. He states it positively here, and then negatively in the next verse: “But if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses” (==Matthew 6:15==). Whatever else we conclude, this much is plain: Jesus considered this the part of the prayer we were most likely to miss, and the part He was least willing to let us miss.

The connection between forgiving and being forgiven runs through His teaching. He attaches the same condition to His other major teaching on prayer (==Mark 11:25-26==), and He builds an entire parable on it—the servant forgiven an unpayable debt who refused to release a small one (==Matthew 18:32-35==). This is not God making His grace fragile. The forgiveness we have received from Him is complete (==Colossians 2:13==). But receiving that forgiveness and refusing to extend it are two things that cannot live together in the same heart. Extending forgiveness is the evidence that we have actually received it; withholding it is evidence that grace has been claimed but not embraced.

So take Jesus’ placement of these verses seriously. He put forgiveness at the door of the prayer closet. Before you bring Him your requests, your needs, and your worship, He asks you to check what you’re holding against the people in your life—because a heart closed toward a brother is not in a position to be open toward the Father. Forgive first. Then pray.

## Word Study
- *forgive* : **aphiēmi** — to send away, release, let go; to dismiss a debt so that it no longer stands against the debtor. The word describes an action, not a feeling: something held is deliberately released.

Other places the word shows its meaning:

- ==Matthew 4:20== — the disciples “immediately *left* (aphiēmi) their nets and followed Him.” The nets weren’t destroyed; they were let go of and walked away from.
- ==Matthew 18:27== — the master “*released* him and *forgave* (aphiēmi) him the debt.” Forgiveness cancels the record itself.

Summary: aphiēmi means the debt leaves your hands. Forgiveness in this verse is not managing to feel warmly toward someone; it is releasing what they owe you the way a fisherman drops a net—and not picking it back up.
