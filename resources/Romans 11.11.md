## Commentary
Paul asks a question that is absolutely essential to understanding God's purposes for Israel and the Church: “I say then, have they stumbled that they should fall? Certainly not! But through their fall, to provoke them to jealousy, salvation has come to the Gentiles.”

There is an enormous distinction between stumbling and falling beyond recovery. Paul has just described Israel's failure to obtain the righteousness it was seeking and the hardening that has come upon much of the nation. But he refuses to allow us to interpret that failure as God's final rejection of His people. Israel has stumbled, but God has not abandoned His covenant purposes. Her present condition is neither the conclusion of her story nor evidence that God has finished with her.

What Paul reveals next is extraordinary. Israel's stumbling has become the occasion for the gospel to go to the Gentiles. But Paul doesn't stop there. He explains that God's purpose in bringing salvation to the nations includes provoking Israel to jealousy.

This is a staggering statement because it means that the gospel going to the Gentiles is directly connect to God's ultimate purposes for Israel. Gentiles receiving Jesus as the Messiah is a means of grace for the Jews. God intends to use the salvation of the Gentiles as part of the means by which Israel will eventually be awakened to her Messiah.

The Greek word translated “provoke to jealousy” is *parazēloō*, meaning to stir someone to emulation. Paul had already introduced this language in ==Romans 10:19==, quoting ==Deuteronomy 32:21==, where God declares that He will provoke Israel to jealousy through those who were not previously recognized as His people.

Consider the implications. God intends for Israel to look upon what He has accomplished among the Gentiles and recognize something that rightfully belongs to her own covenant inheritance. The presence of God, the fulfillment of His promises, the life of the Spirit, and the reality of the Messiah were never meant to be foreign to Israel. These were the very things for which she had been prepared through centuries of covenant history with Yahweh.

The Gentile Church, then, is meant to become a living demonstration of what it means to be the people of God.

But this raises a profoundly uncomfortable question: Has the Church actually become the kind of people whose lives would provoke Israel to jealousy?

Would the Jewish people look at our churches and recognize the holiness of Israel's God? Would they encounter a people who tremble at His Word, who live in genuine dependence on His Spirit, whose relationships are marked by sacrificial love and preference for one another, and whose lives demonstrate the reality of the kingdom to come? Or would they see another religious institution, distinguished primarily by its traditions, programs, buildings, and theological claims?

If the purpose of Gentile salvation includes provoking Israel to jealousy, then our calling cannot be fulfilled by possessing correct doctrine concerning Israel (however essential that may be!). We must embody the life of the Messiah in such a way that His reality becomes undeniably visible among us.

Throughout much of Christian history, the Church has responded to Jewish unbelief not with the humility and mercy Paul will command in this chapter, but with contempt, exclusion, and, at times, horrific persecution. Rather than becoming a witness to the Jewish people of the mercy of their own God, Christians have often become an obstacle to their recognition of Jesus. 

This is not an exaggeration of Christian history. In the fourth century, John Chrysostom, one of the most influential preachers of the early Church, delivered a series of sermons that spoke of the Jewish people and their synagogues with extraordinary contempt. During the First Crusade in 1096, Christian crusaders massacred Jewish communities throughout the Rhineland in what is now Germany. In 1215, the Fourth Lateran Council required Jews living under Christian rule to wear distinctive clothing that identified and separated them from Christians. And in 1543, Martin Luther, the great Protestant reformer, published On the Jews and Their Lies, in which he advocated burning synagogues and destroying Jewish homes.

These were not simply failures of individual Christians to love their neighbors. In many cases, the persecution was encouraged or justified through theological arguments that portrayed the Jewish people as rejected and condemned by God. Although there were Christians who opposed such treatment, the consequences of this hostility were devastating. Consider the contradiction: the very people Paul instructed the Gentile Church to provoke to jealousy were instead subjected to persecution in the name of their own Messiah. Rather than encountering a people who demonstrated the mercy and faithfulness of the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, generations of Jewish people encountered a Christianity that seemed to contradict the character of the God it claimed to represent.

Paul will later warn the Gentiles not to boast against the natural branches of the olive tree, reminding them that they do not support the root, but the root supports them (==Romans 11:17–18==). We are recipients of a mercy that came to us through Israel's Messiah and Israel's covenant promises. There is no room for superiority, much less hostility, toward the people through whom God brought salvation into the world.

But Paul's argument goes beyond correcting our attitudes toward Israel. He is revealing something about the ultimate purpose of the Church.

We have not just been saved *from* something. We have been saved *for* something. Our salvation brings us into a story that began long before our conversion and extends beyond our individual lives. God has brought Gentiles into the blessings of the new covenant, and He intends for that mercy to become a witness to the people from whom those blessings first came.

This does not mean that the Church replaces Israel or that Jewish people can be restored to God apart from faith in Jesus. The same Messiah who saves the Gentiles is Israel's only hope of salvation. But it does mean that God's purposes for Israel and the nations are bound together in ways we cannot afford to ignore.

Israel's eventual recognition of her Messiah is connected, in God's purposes, to the kind of people the Gentile Church becomes. We cannot manufacture Israel's restoration, nor does Paul suggest that the Church's spiritual maturity would somehow guarantee it. But he does show us that God intends to employ the salvation of the Gentiles to open the eyes of a blind Israel.

Israel must encounter more than our arguments. She must encounter a people whose lives bear witness that the promises of her God have found their fulfillment in Jesus, and anyone who believes in Him is born again into His image.

And this brings us to the heart of Paul's argument. God has not abandoned Israel. In His wisdom, He has taken even her stumbling and made it an occasion for mercy to reach the nations. But that mercy is not intended to terminate with us. It is meant to return, in witness and love, toward the very people through whom it came.

The Church's salvation is therefore not an end in itself. It is part of a larger redemptive purpose in which God intends to demonstrate His faithfulness to Israel and His mercy to the nations.

The question is whether we are living in a manner worthy of that calling.
