## Commentary
"Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in Your sight, O LORD, my strength and my Redeemer."

Psalm 19 is a psalm about speech. It opens with the heavens *declaring* the glory of God—"day unto day utters speech" (==Psalm 19:1-2==)—creation talking without a single word. Then it turns to the word of the Lord, perfect and sure, sweeter than honey (==Psalm 19:7-10==). God speaks through the skies; God speaks through His law. And only after David has listened to both does he finally open his own mouth—and the first thing he does with it is ask God to inspect it. The one who has heard God speak wants to speak words God can say "amen" to.

David knew that the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart could never be separated. What the heart meditates on, the mouth eventually announces (==Matthew 12:34==). He puts both on the altar in one breath: the loud life and the quiet one, the sentence everyone heard and the thought no one did.

Notice the standard David sets: acceptable *in Your sight*. Not acceptable to the room. Plenty of words win the room—clever words, cutting words, impressive words—and fail the audit that matters. It works the other way, too: some of the most acceptable words in God's sight will never impress anyone—a private confession, a quiet blessing spoken over a child, a "no" to gossip that costs you a friend. David wants his inner and outer life to pass review before an audience of One.

And if you see the is verse in the context of the whole passage, you'll see that David has just admitted why he needs his thoughts and his words examined by the Lord. A verse earlier he asked, "Who can understand his errors? Cleanse me from secret faults" (==Psalm 19:12==)—and then prayed to be kept from presumptuous sins (==Psalm 19:13==). Hidden faults live in the meditation of the heart. Presumptuous sins tend to come out of the mouth. David is asking God to deal with sin at both ends of the pipeline: the source and the outlet.

And notice how he ends: "O LORD, my strength and my Redeemer." A prayer like this needs both names. We need His *strength*—the Hebrew is literally "my Rock"—because no one tames the tongue alone (==James 3:8==), and a man who wants his words to be steady needs to be standing on something that is. And we need our *Redeemer*, because we've all already said things we can't take back. He is the Rock for the words ahead of us and the Redeemer for the words behind us.

## Word Study
- *acceptable* : **ratson** (Hebrew) — favor, delight, acceptance. This is sacrificial language. It's the word used for an offering brought "of his own free will... that he may be *accepted* before the LORD" (==Leviticus 1:3==), and for the sacrifice that must be without blemish to be *acceptable* (==Leviticus 22:20-21==). David is treating his words and thoughts as an offering laid on the altar—and asking that God find them pleasing. Paul picks up the same idea: "present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, *acceptable* to God" (==Romans 12:1==).
- *meditation* : **higgayon** (Hebrew) — a murmuring, a low sound; the same root as the "meditate" of Psalm 1:2 (*hagah*), which pictures someone muttering the word to himself day and night. It's also used for the quiet resonance of a harp (==Psalm 92:3==). Meditation in the Bible is not silent, empty-minded stillness—it's the heart quietly talking to itself. Which means even our inner life is a kind of speech, and David wants that speech reviewed too.
- *strength* : **tsur** (Hebrew) — rock, cliff, stronghold. "The LORD is my rock and my fortress" (==Psalm 18:2==); "He only is my rock and my salvation" (==Psalm 62:2==). Not strength as energy, but strength as *footing*.

Summary: David lays his words and his thoughts on the altar like a sacrifice, asks God to find them pleasing, and does it standing on the Rock who redeems everything he's already gotten wrong.
